# Child Development — what children can and can't do, ages 5–11

A great explanation pitched at the wrong developmental level fails. This file describes, band by band, what a child can realistically do **cognitively, linguistically, socially-emotionally**, as a **reader**, and as a **number-thinker** — and the **practical implications** for lesson length, pacing, examples, and tone.

The three bands map to the UK National Curriculum:
- **Ages 5–7 — Key Stage 1** (Years 1–2)
- **Ages 7–9 — Lower Key Stage 2** (Years 3–4)
- **Ages 9–11 — Upper Key Stage 2** (Years 5–6)

Treat the ages as a **guide, not a verdict.** Children vary enormously; a "Year 3" child may read like a Year 5 and reason about number like a Year 2. The point is to *calibrate and then watch* — let what the child actually does override the band. The two universal truths across all bands: **working memory is limited (smaller than an adult's), and attention is finite (and shorter than an adult's).**

---

## A note on Piaget (and its caveats)

Piaget's stages are a useful lens, but treat them loosely. Most primary children sit in the **concrete-operational** stage: they can reason logically about **concrete, real, present things** but struggle with **abstract or hypothetical** reasoning. A 6-year-old can share 12 sweets fairly between friends but can't reason about "a number *n*". Younger children may still show **pre-operational** features (centring on one feature at a time, shaky *conservation* — not yet grasping that a quantity stays the same when its appearance changes).

**Caveats:** the boundaries are fuzzy and earlier than Piaget claimed — children can reason abstractly sooner *when the content is familiar and concretely supported*. The practical takeaway isn't "they can't do X until age Y"; it's **"keep it concrete, make it real, and supply the picture they can't yet hold in their head."** This is exactly why the Concrete→Pictorial→Abstract sequence (see `pedagogy-core.md` §8) works across the whole primary range.

## Band 1 — Ages 5–7 (Key Stage 1, Years 1–2)

### Cognitive
- Firmly **concrete thinkers.** Reason about things they can see, touch, picture, or act out. Abstract symbols (numerals, letters) must stay tied to real meaning.
- **Conservation** is still settling early in the band: a 5-year-old may think a tall thin glass holds "more" than a short wide one with the same water, or that spreading out 6 counters makes "more" counters.
- **Working memory is very limited** — roughly **2–3 new items** at once. A three-part instruction ("get your book, turn to page 4, and underline the verbs") will lose them after part one.
- **Attention span is short:** very roughly **10–15 minutes** on a focused adult-led task, often less, and *much* less if it's passive. They re-engage fast when the activity changes or when *they* get to do something.
- They take things **literally** and find sarcasm/irony confusing.
- One-at-a-time focus: they tend to **centre** on a single feature and miss others.

### Language
- Speaking vocabulary is growing fast but **uneven**; many abstract/academic words ("compare", "predict", "estimate") are not yet owned — define them in plain words.
- Follow **short, single-clause instructions** best. Long sentences with subordinate clauses get lost.
- Love wordplay, rhyme, repetition, and being *told* things in story form.

### Social-emotional
- **Eager to please** an adult/tutor; warm praise is hugely motivating and criticism stings.
- **Big feelings, short fuse** — frustration can arrive fast and tip into giving up or tears. Emotional safety is paramount.
- Just learning to wait, take turns, and persist; self-regulation is immature.
- A character-led, friendly tutor who celebrates effort is ideal here.

### Reading
- Spans the early stages: **phonics** (decoding letter-sounds), blending sounds into words, growing **sight words**. By end of Year 2 many read simple texts with some fluency, but **decoding still costs effort** — which eats the working memory needed to *comprehend*.
- Don't assume they can read on-screen text fluently — **lean on the spoken/audio channel and pictures.**

### Number / quantity
- Counting reliably, understanding that the last number counted = the quantity (cardinality), reading/writing numerals, place value to ~100 (tens and ones), addition/subtraction within 20 moving toward fluency, number bonds to 10, doubling/halving, the very start of multiplication as "groups of", simple fractions as "half/quarter of a shape or amount".
- **Counts on fingers and objects — and should.** This is healthy concrete reasoning, not a problem to fix.
- "Equals" is often read as "the answer comes next" rather than "the same as" (a misconception that matters later).

### Practical implications
- **Lesson length:** short — aim ~**10–15 minutes** of actual focused content. Better a tight 12 minutes than a sagging 25.
- **Pacing:** change activity every few minutes; keep them *doing*, not listening. One idea, one step, one instruction at a time.
- **Examples:** maximally concrete and real — toys, animals, food, the child's own interests. Use objects/`emojiViz` for everything quantitative. Stay literal.
- **Tone:** warm, playful, encouraging, lots of honest specific praise. Celebrate effort loudly. Never make a mistake feel like a failure. Short, simple sentences; define any non-everyday word.
- **Blocks that fit:** `emojiViz`, `numberLine`, `flashcards`, `multipleChoice` with few options, `trueFalse`, `matchPairs`, `speak`, picture-led `slideshow`. Keep text minimal — they're still learning to read it.

## Band 2 — Ages 7–9 (Lower Key Stage 2, Years 3–4)

### Cognitive
- Solidly **concrete-operational** and getting good at it: logical reasoning about real situations, **conservation secure**, can hold and compare several features at once, can reverse operations mentally ("if 3 + 4 = 7 then 7 − 4 = 3").
- **The very beginnings of abstract reasoning** — but it's fragile and needs concrete anchors. Hypotheticals work *if* grounded in something familiar.
- **Working memory has grown** but is still limited — roughly **3–4 items**; multi-step procedures need to be chunked and scaffolded.
- **Attention span:** roughly **15–25 minutes** on an engaging task, longer when actively involved, shorter when passive.
- Increasingly able to plan a couple of steps ahead and follow a two-part instruction.

### Language
- Vocabulary expanding rapidly; can handle longer, more complex sentences and is starting to own **academic vocabulary** — but introduce subject terms explicitly with a `keyTerm` and an example.
- Can explain their reasoning in a sentence or two when prompted ("How did you work that out?") — a key window to develop.
- Beginning to understand figurative language, simple idioms, and humour.

### Social-emotional
- **Peer comparison and self-image emerge** — they start to notice "I'm good/bad at maths" and can form a fixed self-label. This is the critical window for **growth-mindset** framing (praise process, normalise struggle) to prevent "I'm just not a maths person."
- Can persist longer and tolerate some productive struggle, *if* it feels safe and success is reachable.
- Want to feel competent and a bit grown-up; respond well to being trusted with a "challenge" and to genuine, specific praise (empty praise starts to ring hollow).

### Reading
- Most are transitioning from **"learning to read" to "reading to learn"** — decoding is becoming automatic, freeing capacity for comprehension. Fluency and comprehension are now the growth edge: inference, sequencing, finding meaning.
- Can read on-screen instructions and short texts, but still benefit from audio support and shouldn't be given dense text.

### Number / quantity
- Place value to 1,000+ (Y3) and beyond; mental and written addition/subtraction with exchanging; **multiplication and division facts** (working toward the times tables, a major Y3–Y4 push); fractions as numbers (not just shapes) and equivalence beginning; tenths; multi-step word problems with support.
- Can work with the **abstract symbols** more, but understanding still rests on the concrete/pictorial — bar models, arrays and number lines remain essential, not babyish.

### Practical implications
- **Lesson length:** ~**15–25 minutes** of content works well, with at least one clear activity shift partway.
- **Pacing:** can sustain a short explanation, but still check understanding often and keep them doing. Chunk multi-step methods explicitly ("step 1… now step 2…").
- **Examples:** real and relatable, tied to interests, but can now include a step of abstraction *after* a concrete anchor. Introduce subject vocabulary deliberately.
- **Tone:** warm and encouraging, but treat them as capable — invite reasoning ("convince me", "why?"), offer "challenge" versions, and be scrupulous about **process praise** because self-labels are forming now. Mistakes = useful information.
- **Blocks that fit:** the full belt — `whiteboard` (bar models, arrays, diagrams), `steps`, `ordering`, `categorize`, `fillBlank`, `numberEntry`, `shortText` for reasoning, `keyTerm` for vocabulary, `slideshow`.

## Band 3 — Ages 9–11 (Upper Key Stage 2, Years 5–6)

### Cognitive
- Strong concrete-operational reasoning, and **emerging formal-operational thinking**: can handle more genuinely **abstract and hypothetical** ideas (variables, generalised rules, "what if…"), reason about reasoning, and follow multi-step logical arguments — *but it's still developing and benefits from concrete grounding when a concept is new.*
- **Working memory larger** — roughly **4–5 items** — but multi-step problems (e.g. multi-stage word problems, long division) still tax it; teach a clear procedure and let it become fluent so it stops consuming capacity.
- **Attention span:** roughly **20–30+ minutes** on engaging, meaningful work, especially when they have some ownership of it.
- Can plan, self-monitor, and check their own work to a degree; can hold a goal across several steps.

### Language
- Rich vocabulary; comfortable with complex sentences, abstract and academic language, nuance, figurative language, and explaining/justifying at length.
- Can articulate reasoning, argue a point, evaluate, and compare — push these higher-order moves ("justify", "evaluate", "what's the general rule?").

### Social-emotional
- **Self-concept and identity strengthening**; acutely aware of being good/bad at things, and increasingly self-conscious about being wrong. Fear of looking foolish can suppress risk-taking — keep it private, safe, and low-stakes (1:1 is a gift here).
- Greater independence and persistence; capable of real ownership of their learning and motivated by **purpose, mastery, and autonomy** (why does this matter? am I getting better? do I get a say?).
- Approaching pre-adolescence — respond to being treated with respect and not talked down to; bristle at babyish framing.

### Reading
- Generally fluent readers "reading to learn": comprehension, **inference**, summarising, comparing sources, detecting author's intent, navigating longer and information-dense texts. Strong readers can self-teach from text; struggling readers at this age need careful support and shouldn't be exposed in a 1:1.
- Comfortable with on-screen text — but a wall of text is still poor pedagogy (cognitive load applies at every age).

### Number / quantity
- Place value to millions and decimals; all four operations with larger numbers and decimals (long multiplication/division); **fractions, decimals, percentages and the links between them**; ratio and proportion; the very beginnings of **algebra** (using a letter for an unknown, simple formulae); area/perimeter/volume; primes, factors, multiples; negative numbers.
- Genuinely abstract for the first time — yet a quick concrete/pictorial anchor (a bar model, a diagram) still rescues a struggling moment. Don't assume abstraction is automatic; verify understanding underneath the procedure.

### Practical implications
- **Lesson length:** ~**20–30 minutes** of content is sustainable; can handle a meatier single problem or a short sequence with a shift partway.
- **Pacing:** can take a slightly longer explanation and a multi-step method, but *still* check understanding regularly and still aim for the ~80% success band; don't mistake fluency in one step for understanding of the whole.
- **Examples:** real-world relevance and a clear "why this matters", connected to genuine interests and to abstract/general patterns; can explore "what's the rule?" and "would that always work?". Respect their growing sophistication.
- **Tone:** respectful, a bit more grown-up, collaborative — a thinking partner, not a babysitter. Invite justification and evaluation. Keep being wrong safe and private; praise strategy and perseverance honestly (process praise still matters — self-labels are entrenched by now).
- **Blocks that fit:** everything, including the more demanding interactives — multi-step `steps`, `categorize`, `multiSelect`, `ordering`, `shortText` for extended reasoning, `whiteboard` for diagrams/graphs/algebra, `custom` builds, plus `video`/`slideshow` for richer explanation.

## Cross-band cheat sheet

| | 5–7 (KS1) | 7–9 (LKS2) | 9–11 (UKS2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Thinking** | concrete only | concrete, abstract emerging (fragile) | abstract emerging, still needs anchors |
| **Working memory** | ~2–3 items | ~3–4 items | ~4–5 items |
| **Attention (focused task)** | ~10–15 min | ~15–25 min | ~20–30+ min |
| **Reading** | learning to read; lean on audio + pictures | becoming fluent; "reading to learn" begins | fluent; reading to learn, inference |
| **Number** | count within 20; concrete; "groups of" | times tables; fractions as numbers; bar models | decimals/%/ratio; start of algebra |
| **Lesson length (content)** | ~10–15 min | ~15–25 min | ~20–30 min |
| **Tone** | warm, playful, literal, lots of praise | warm but capable; invite reasoning | respectful, collaborative, justify/evaluate |
| **Watch for** | conservation, big feelings, literalness | "I'm bad at X" labels forming | fear of being wrong; abstraction assumed too soon |

**The override rule again:** these are starting calibrations. Use the live evidence — if the child is breezing through, raise the band; if they're floundering, drop concrete supports back in and shorten the step. Development is the *default*, the child in front of you is the *truth*.
