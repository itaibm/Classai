# Questioning & dialogue — talk methods that make thinking happen

This family covers **how a teacher uses questions and talk** to make a child think, surface their reasoning, and consolidate learning. Many famous techniques (cold call, think-pair-share) were invented for *classrooms of 30* to spread thinking across many children; in 1:1 the child is *always* called on, so the goal shifts to **genuine thinking, surfaced reasoning, and emotional safety**. Each entry below notes its 1:1 adaptation explicitly. Don't duplicate [`pedagogy-core.md` §10](../teaching/pedagogy-core.md); this is the deeper catalogue.

---

## Socratic questioning

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** moderate — (long pedigree; effective when child has knowledge to reason with; weak as a substitute for teaching novices) · **Best for:** deepening understanding, probing reasoning, ethics/discussion; ages 8–11 more than 5–7.

**What it is.** A disciplined series of probing questions that lead the child to examine their own thinking — clarifying, challenging assumptions, seeking evidence, testing implications — rather than being told the answer.

**Why it works.** Articulating and defending reasoning forces deeper processing and exposes gaps the child (and tutor) can then fix. The child constructs the conclusion, which makes it stick.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use when the child *has* relevant knowledge to reason from, and for "why/how" thinking. NOT for introducing brand-new facts or procedures — Socratic questioning a 6-year-old toward an unknown algorithm is just disguised guessing and breeds frustration. Don't use it to "gotcha."

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Ask an opening question that has reasoning behind it.
2. Probe: "Why?" "How do you know?" "What if…?"
3. Challenge gently: offer a counter-case.
4. Ask them to test their conclusion.
5. Summarise what they reasoned out.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Use in *check*/*practice* once the core idea is taught — capture reasoning with `shortText`/`speak`. Keep it one question at a time, with real wait time. Reserve it for consolidation and transfer, never for first acquisition (that's the explain beat's job). The tutor must stay warm: probing is curiosity, not interrogation.

**Primary example.** Maths, Year 5 — "You said the bigger fraction is 1/8 because 8 is bigger. What if we drew both? … What do you notice about the slices now?"

**Pitfalls.** (1) Socratic-questioning toward unknown facts. (2) Stacking questions. (3) A tone that feels like a trap.

**Related.** [[Probing vs funnelling vs focusing questions]], [[Elaborative interrogation]], [[Self-explanation prompts]], [[Dialogic teaching (Alexander)]].

## Bloom-levelled questioning

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** moderate — (Bloom's taxonomy, 1956; Anderson & Krathwohl revision 2001; useful planning frame, not a strict hierarchy of difficulty) · **Best for:** deliberately varying cognitive demand across a lesson; all ages.

**What it is.** Pitching questions at different cognitive levels — **remember, understand, apply, analyse, evaluate, create** — so the lesson moves from recall toward higher-order thinking, instead of staying at "what's the answer?"

**Why it works.** Mixing levels ensures both retrieval (which builds memory) and the analysis/application that builds transfer. Naming the levels helps a teacher *notice* when they're stuck at recall.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use to plan a question sequence with a deliberate climb. NOT as a rigid "higher is always better" ladder — secure recall *underpins* higher-order thinking, so don't skip it. Young children need plenty of the lower levels first.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Open with remember/understand (retrieval, in own words).
2. Move to apply (use it on a new case).
3. Reach for analyse/evaluate/create where the topic allows.
4. Match the level to what the child can currently handle.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The tutor sequences checks up the levels within and across beats: a recall check in the hook, an "apply it" check in practice, an "explain why/what if" probe at the end. Question stems below give ready phrasing per level. Don't force "create" onto a child still shaky at "understand" — the director's success band governs the climb.

**Primary example.** Science, Year 4 — habitats: remember "name a desert animal," understand "why does a camel suit the desert?", apply "would a polar bear cope here?", create "design an animal for the desert."

**Pitfalls.** (1) Treating it as a strict difficulty hierarchy. (2) Spending the whole lesson at recall. (3) Reaching for "create" with no foundation.

**Related.** [[Question stems]], [[Socratic questioning]], [[../methods/retrieval-and-memory|retrieval practice]].

## Wait time (think time)

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** strong — (Mary Budd Rowe 1972/1986; extending wait time to ~3s+ raises response length, quality, and confidence) · **Best for:** every question, every age — disproportionately important for younger and less confident children.

**What it is.** The deliberate pause **after** asking a question (Wait Time I) and **after** the child's answer (Wait Time II), giving the child seconds to formulate and extend, instead of rushing on.

**Why it works.** Formulating an answer takes real time, especially for children. Filling the silence robs them of the thinking. Pausing after their answer often yields the best part — the elaboration they were still reaching for.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use after essentially every genuine question. The only "when not" is true quick-fire fluency drills where speed *is* the goal. Never mistake a child's pause for "doesn't know."

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Ask one clear question.
2. Pause — count ~3 seconds silently; resist filling it.
3. Let them answer.
4. Pause again before responding; often they'll add more.
5. Only then respond.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Build genuine wait time into checks — present the question, then *stop*; don't auto-advance or re-prompt instantly. The watchdog/timing logic should give the child real seconds (and via the answer bar, time to type or speak). After their answer, a beat of pause before judging invites elaboration. Never let TTS barrel into the next turn before the child has thought.

**Primary example.** Any subject — after "Why do you think the ice melted?", the tutor waits in silence rather than offering hints at second one.

**Pitfalls.** (1) Filling the silence with hints/rephrasing. (2) Reading a pause as failure. (3) No Wait Time II (cutting off the elaboration).

**Related.** [[Pose–Pause–Pounce–Bounce]], [[Socratic questioning]], [[../teaching/pedagogy-core|questioning techniques]].

## Cold call — and its 1:1 adaptations

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** moderate — (Lemov, *Teach Like a Champion*; raises whole-class accountability/attention) · **Best for:** classrooms; in 1:1, the *spirit* (everyone is always accountable) is automatic — adapt the *technique*.

**What it is.** Calling on a child who hasn't volunteered, so every child must stay ready to answer. Its purpose is *universal accountability* in a group.

**Why it works (in groups).** Knowing you might be called keeps all 30 children thinking, not just the hand-raisers. It also lets the teacher choose *who* to check.

**When to use / when NOT.** In 1:1 there's only one child, so cold call's group function disappears — the child is always "called." Don't import its sometimes-anxious framing. NOT a 1:1 method as-is.

**1:1 adaptation.** Keep the *accountability* (constant, gentle checking — the child can't hide at the back of a class of one) but drop the surprise/pressure. Make every check feel safe and expected: "Quick one for you…", "Have a go even if you're unsure." The equivalent virtue is *constant low-stakes checking* with warmth.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Classai already realises the good half of cold call: constant checks, no hiding. The tutor's job is to keep those checks emotionally safe (process praise, "mistakes are information") so accountability never tips into anxiety. Every turn ends with the child doing something — the benign version of "you might be called."

**Primary example.** Instead of "Anyone know…?", the tutor says "Your turn — what's 3 × 4?" warmly, then waits.

**Pitfalls.** (1) Importing the high-pressure framing into 1:1. (2) Letting checking feel like testing. (3) Forgetting the safety net.

**Related.** [[Wait time (think time)]], [[No-hands questioning]], [[../teaching/pedagogy-core|formative assessment]].

## No-hands questioning

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** moderate — (Wiliam, formative assessment; stops hand-raisers dominating) · **Best for:** classrooms; in 1:1, becomes "the child always responds."

**What it is.** The teacher stops taking volunteered hands and instead selects who answers, so participation isn't dominated by the eager few.

**Why it works (in groups).** It samples understanding across *all* children, not just the confident ones, giving truer formative data.

**When to use / when NOT.** A group equity technique. In 1:1 it's moot — collapse it to "every question gets a response from this child."

**1:1 adaptation.** The principle that survives is **no opting out**: the tutor doesn't accept "I don't know" as the end — it scaffolds toward an attempt ("let's start it together"). Every question is answered, supported as needed.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** On "I don't know," the tutor doesn't move on or reveal the answer; it drops a scaffold (a hint, a CPA stage back, a `wordBank`) and re-asks, per the wrong-answer→remedy loop. No question is abandoned unanswered.

**Primary example.** Child says "dunno" to "half of 8." Tutor: "Let's deal 8 counters into two piles together — how many in each?"

**Pitfalls.** (1) Accepting "don't know" and revealing the answer. (2) Over-scaffolding so the child never strains. (3) Treating it as a group-only idea with no 1:1 lesson.

**Related.** [[No-hands questioning]] → [[Cold call — and its 1:1 adaptations]], [[../methods/instruction-models|scaffolding]], [[Probing vs funnelling vs focusing questions]].

## Think–Pair–Share (and its 1:1 adaptation)

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** moderate — (Lyman 1981; boosts participation and rehearsal of answers) · **Best for:** classrooms; 1:1 keeps the *think* and *share*, simulates the *pair*.

**What it is.** Children **think** alone, **pair** to discuss, then **share** with the class — giving rehearsal time and a low-risk first audience before public sharing.

**Why it works (in groups).** The think phase is private retrieval; the pair phase rehearses and refines the answer with a safe partner, raising the quality and confidence of the share.

**When to use / when NOT.** A group structure. In 1:1 there's no peer to pair with — but the valuable phases (private thinking, then articulating) transfer.

**1:1 adaptation.** Keep **Think** (give wait time / a moment to jot or whisper an idea) → then **Share** (child explains to the tutor). The tutor can play a light "pair" by offering a tentative idea to react to ("I might say it's 6 — what do you think?"), giving the child something to refine against without doing their thinking for them.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Implement as: pose question → wait-time "think" → child shares via `speak`/`shortText`. For richer prompts, the tutor can voice a *deliberate hypothesis* (sometimes a wrong one, flagged playfully) for the child to evaluate — the "pair" stand-in — then have them commit to an answer.

**Primary example.** English, Year 3 — "Think for a moment: is *jumped* past or present? … Now tell me, and how you know."

**Pitfalls.** (1) Skipping the think phase (no rehearsal). (2) The tutor's "pair" idea overriding the child's. (3) Always sharing immediately, no private think.

**Related.** [[Wait time (think time)]], [[Accountable Talk]], [[Self-explanation prompts]].

## Dialogic teaching (Alexander)

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** promising — (Robin Alexander; EEF dialogic teaching trial showed gains in English/maths/science at KS2) · **Best for:** extended reasoning and oracy; ages 7–11.

**What it is.** Teaching through cumulative, reciprocal talk where ideas are built on, challenged, and chained — not a series of disconnected Q&A. Its principles: collective, reciprocal, supportive, cumulative, purposeful.

**Why it works.** Sustained, connected talk makes reasoning explicit and lets the teacher build on the child's contributions (rather than just evaluating them), deepening understanding and oracy together.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use for reasoning-rich topics with enough prior knowledge to discuss. NOT a fast route to teach new facts; not for pure fluency. Needs a child willing to talk — build that safety first.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Pose a genuine, open question (not one with a single recall answer).
2. Build on the child's answer ("you said X — so would Y also…?").
3. Chain talk cumulatively toward an understanding.
4. Keep it supportive and purposeful (toward the learning goal).

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Across a *check*/*practice* exchange the tutor *builds on* the child's last answer instead of resetting each turn — using working memory of what they just said. Keep the dialogue cumulative and goal-directed, not chit-chat; cap it so it doesn't crowd out doing. The two-tier memory lets the tutor reference earlier reasoning ("earlier you said…").

**Primary example.** History, Year 5 — "Why might people *want* a castle?" → child: "to be safe" → "safe from what? … so where would you build it?" building a reasoned account.

**Pitfalls.** (1) Reverting to IRE (evaluate-and-move-on). (2) Talk that drifts off-goal. (3) Using it before the child has knowledge to discuss.

**Related.** [[IRE vs dialogic talk]], [[Accountable Talk]], [[Socratic questioning]].

## Accountable Talk

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** promising — (Michaels, O'Connor & Resnick; raises rigour and equity of discussion) · **Best for:** holding talk to standards of reasoning and evidence; ages 7–11.

**What it is.** Discussion held to three accountabilities: to the **learning community** (listen, build on others), to **knowledge** (use accurate facts), and to **reasoning** (justify with logic/evidence). Sentence stems support it ("I agree because…", "My evidence is…").

**Why it works.** It turns loose talk into reasoned argument: the child must ground claims in knowledge and logic, which deepens understanding and models how a discipline argues.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use in reasoning discussions where claims should be justified. NOT for quick recall. In 1:1 the "community" accountability is to the tutor as a thinking partner.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Ask for a claim.
2. Require accountability to knowledge: "what do you know that supports that?"
3. Require accountability to reasoning: "why does that follow?"
4. Model the stems and have the child use them.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The tutor supplies sentence stems ("I think ___ because ___") via `wordBank`/sentence starters, then insists gently on the *because*. It demands accuracy (correcting wrong facts) and logic (asking "why does that follow?"), modelling disciplined talk. Captures justifications in `shortText` for the learner model.

**Primary example.** Science, Year 4 — "Metal sinks." → "I agree/disagree because…" → child grounds it in "metal is heavy/dense."

**Pitfalls.** (1) Stems without the reasoning behind them. (2) Letting inaccurate "facts" stand. (3) Over-formalising for a 5-year-old.

**Related.** [[Dialogic teaching (Alexander)]], [[../methods/literacy-across-curriculum|sentence stems & oracy scaffolds]], [[Self-explanation prompts]].

## IRE vs dialogic talk

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** moderate — (Mehan; Cazden; IRE is the default classroom pattern, dialogic talk is the richer alternative) · **Best for:** knowing *which* talk pattern fits the moment.

**What it is.** **IRE** = Initiate (teacher asks) → Respond (child answers) → Evaluate (teacher says right/wrong). It's fast and fine for checking recall. **Dialogic** talk instead *builds on* the response (Initiate → Respond → **Feedback that extends**), opening up thinking.

**Why it matters.** IRE is efficient for fact checks but, used for everything, keeps thinking shallow ("guess what's in my head"). Switching to dialogic feedback ("say more / why?") deepens reasoning. Knowing when to use each is the skill.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use IRE for quick fluency/recall checks. Switch to dialogic for understanding, reasoning, and misconceptions. Don't run an entire lesson as relentless IRE.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. For recall: IRE is fine — ask, hear, confirm, move on.
2. For understanding: replace the "E" with a build ("why?", "say more", "what if?").
3. Choose deliberately based on the goal of the question.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The tutor uses IRE-speed checks for fluency items (quick `multipleChoice`/`numberEntry`) but switches to dialogic follow-ups on the *important* checks and on every wrong answer — never just "wrong, the answer is X," but "what made you pick that?" then remedy. The director's stuck-state forces the dialogic mode.

**Primary example.** IRE: "7×8? … 56, yes." Dialogic: "How did you get 56?" → reveals strategy or luck.

**Pitfalls.** (1) IRE for everything (shallow). (2) Dialogic for trivial recall (drags). (3) "Evaluate" with no feedback the child can use.

**Related.** [[Dialogic teaching (Alexander)]], [[Pose–Pause–Pounce–Bounce]], [[../teaching/pedagogy-core|formative assessment]].

## Probing vs funnelling vs focusing questions

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** moderate — (Wood; Herbel-Eisenmann & Breyfogle on funnelling vs focusing) · **Best for:** maths/reasoning talk; choosing *how* to follow up; ages 6–11.

**What it is.** Three follow-up styles. **Probing:** dig into the child's reasoning ("how did you get that?"). **Funnelling:** a chain of narrow questions that lead the child step-by-step to *your* answer (low thinking). **Focusing:** questions that turn attention to a key feature and let the child reason ("what do you notice about the denominators?") — high thinking.

**Why it matters.** Funnelling feels helpful but does the thinking *for* the child (they just follow the breadcrumb). Focusing keeps the cognitive work with the child. Probing surfaces what they actually understand.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use **focusing** and **probing** as the default. Use **funnelling** only as a last-resort scaffold when the child is truly stuck — and recognise it's a crutch, not teaching.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. First **probe**: "how did you work it out?"
2. If stuck, **focus**: direct attention to the key feature, then let them reason.
3. Only if still stuck, briefly **funnel** to rescue, then re-pose a focusing question.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The remedy ladder should prefer focusing/probing prompts over funnelling: on a wrong answer, first ask *why*, then point at the key feature ("look at the bottom numbers"), and only funnel as the final scaffold before re-modelling. This keeps the child thinking instead of being walked to the answer.

**Primary example.** Fractions: funnelling = "what's 1×2? 2. what's below? 8. so?" (child just arithmetic-follows). Focusing = "what do you notice about how many slices each has?"

**Pitfalls.** (1) Defaulting to funnelling (it feels efficient). (2) Probing only when the child is *wrong* (teaches "why = you erred"). (3) Vague focusing that doesn't actually direct attention.

**Related.** [[Socratic questioning]], [[No-hands questioning]], [[../methods/instruction-models|scaffolding & fading]].

## Hinge questions

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** promising — (Dylan Wiliam; a well-designed diagnostic check at a lesson's pivot) · **Best for:** deciding whether to move on or reteach; all ages, especially maths/science.

**What it is.** A single, carefully designed question at a **hinge point** in the lesson whose answer tells you whether the child is ready to move on — with wrong options that each reveal a *specific* misconception.

**Why it works.** It compresses formative assessment into one high-information question: the *particular* wrong answer diagnoses *which* misconception, so the response can be precise, not generic.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use at the pivot before building further. NOT for every check (designing them is costly) — reserve for the make-or-break understanding. A weak hinge question (no diagnostic distractors) wastes the moment.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Identify the lesson's pivotal understanding.
2. Write one question whose wrong answers each map to a known misconception.
3. Pose it; read *which* wrong answer they give.
4. Reteach the specific misconception, or advance.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** This is exactly Classai's `BeatCheck` with `wrongAnswers: [{ answer, why, remedy }]` — each distractor is pre-mapped to a misconception and its remedy. The grounding files supply these. The tutor poses the hinge check, matches the child's answer to the right `remedy`, and the director advances only on the correct one.

**Primary example.** Maths, Year 3 — "Which is bigger, 0.5 or 0.25?" Picking 0.25 reveals "longer decimal = bigger" → remedy with a number line.

**Pitfalls.** (1) Distractors that don't diagnose anything. (2) Using it everywhere. (3) Ignoring *which* wrong answer was given and giving a generic correction.

**Related.** [[IRE vs dialogic talk]], [[Bloom-levelled questioning]], [[../methods/instruction-models|Concept Attainment]].

## Pose–Pause–Pounce–Bounce

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** promising — (popular UK AfL routine; bundles wait time + dialogic build) · **Best for:** structuring a questioning exchange; ages 7–11.

**What it is.** A four-move routine: **Pose** a question to all; **Pause** (wait time); **Pounce** (get an answer from one child); **Bounce** (pass it on — "do you agree? can you add?"). In groups the bounce spreads thinking; the first three are universal.

**Why it works.** It builds in wait time and stops the teacher answering their own question, and the bounce turns one answer into shared reasoning.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use for discussion-worthy questions. The "bounce" needs a second person — adapt in 1:1.

**1:1 adaptation.** Pose–Pause–Pounce works as-is (ask, wait, child answers). **Bounce** becomes a self-bounce or tutor-bounce: "Now convince me," "What if I said it was 7 instead?", or "Would that still work for 100?" — bouncing the child's answer back for them to defend or extend, rather than to a peer.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Implement the first three with genuine wait time, then "bounce" by asking the child to justify/extend their own answer (`speak`/`shortText`) or by tossing a counter-case for them to evaluate. Keeps a single answer from ending the thinking.

**Primary example.** Maths: pose "is 17 prime?", pause, child "yes", bounce "convince me — what could divide it?"

**Pitfalls.** (1) Dropping the pause. (2) No bounce (thinking stops at first answer). (3) Bouncing into a "gotcha."

**Related.** [[Wait time (think time)]], [[Dialogic teaching (Alexander)]], [[Elaborative interrogation]].

## Elaborative interrogation

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** strong — (Dunlosky et al. 2013 rated it *moderate-to-high* utility; Pressley) · **Best for:** building understanding of facts by asking "why is this true?"; ages 8–11.

**What it is.** Prompting the child to explain *why* a stated fact is true or makes sense ("Why would a desert animal have thin fur?"), connecting the new fact to prior knowledge rather than memorising it flat.

**Why it works.** Generating a reason integrates the fact into existing knowledge networks, making it more meaningful and memorable than rote storage. It's the "why" engine of comprehension.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use when facts have an underlying logic the child can reason to. NOT for arbitrary facts with no "why" (a sound's spelling, a date) — there the "why" frustrates. Needs enough prior knowledge to answer.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. State (or have the child read) a fact.
2. Ask "why might that be true/make sense?"
3. Let them reason; supply the link if they can't reach it.
4. Confirm and connect to what they know.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** After teaching a fact in *explain*, the tutor adds a "why do you think…?" check rather than only "what is…?". Pairs with the learner model's recorded interests to anchor the "why" in something familiar. Skip it for arbitrary facts (use mnemonics there instead).

**Primary example.** Science, Year 5 — "Polar bears have black skin. Why might that help?" → "to soak up heat."

**Pitfalls.** (1) Using it on arbitrary facts. (2) Accepting "because it is." (3) Asking "why" before the fact is even understood.

**Related.** [[Self-explanation prompts]], [[Socratic questioning]], [[../methods/retrieval-and-memory|generation effect]].

## Self-explanation prompts

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** strong — (Chi et al.; Dunlosky moderate utility; self-explainers learn more from examples) · **Best for:** learning from worked examples and texts; ages 7–11.

**What it is.** Prompting the child to explain *to themselves* what a step means or how a new idea connects ("Why did I split the 5 into 3 and 2 there?"). Unlike elaborative interrogation (why a *fact* is true), this targets *one's own understanding of a step or process*.

**Why it works.** Explaining a step forces the child to make the reasoning explicit, catching gaps and integrating the procedure with understanding — turning passive watching of a worked example into active sense-making.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use during/after worked examples and modelled steps. NOT so often it becomes a chore that slows fluency drills. Best when there's reasoning worth surfacing.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. After a modelled step, ask "why did that step work?" / "what did I just do and why?"
2. Have the child put the reasoning in their own words.
3. Correct or extend the explanation.
4. Move to the next step.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Insert self-explanation checks between the `steps` of a worked example: reveal a step, then "why did I do that?" via `speak`/`shortText`. This converts the worked-example beat from watching into thinking and gives the tutor a read on understanding before practice.

**Primary example.** Maths, Year 4 — column subtraction: after "I borrowed a ten," ask "why did I cross out the 4 and write 3?"

**Pitfalls.** (1) Asking only at the end (misses step-level gaps). (2) Accepting a restatement instead of a reason. (3) Overusing it until it kills momentum.

**Related.** [[Elaborative interrogation]], [[../methods/instruction-models|worked-example & faded guidance]], [[../methods/representation-and-modelling|think-alouds]].

## Question stems (sentence frames for questions)

**Family:** Questioning & dialogue · **Evidence:** moderate — (practical scaffold; supports Bloom-levelled and dialogic questioning) · **Best for:** giving the tutor ready phrasing at the right cognitive level; all ages.

**What it is.** A bank of reusable question openers sorted by purpose/level — recall ("What is…?"), understanding ("Can you say that in your own words?"), apply ("What would happen if…?"), analyse ("How is X different from Y?"), evaluate ("Which is better, and why?"), reasoning ("How did you work it out?", "What's your evidence?").

**Why it works.** Good questions are hard to improvise; stems ensure the tutor reaches beyond recall and phrases prompts clearly and consistently at the intended level.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use as a generation aid for checks and probes. NOT to fire mechanically — match the stem to the moment and the child's level.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Decide the question's purpose (check recall? probe reasoning? push to transfer?).
2. Pick a stem at that level.
3. Fill it with the lesson content.
4. Keep it to one question.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** These stems are the tutor's phrasing library for generating `BeatCheck.question` text and follow-up probes at a chosen Bloom level — keeping checks varied (not always "what is…?") and reasoning prompts ("how do you know?") frequent on both right and wrong answers. Pair with `wordBank`/sentence starters to give the *child* answer stems too.

**Primary example.** Same fact, different stems: "What's a verb?" (recall) → "Find the verb in this sentence" (apply) → "How is a verb different from a noun?" (analyse).

**Pitfalls.** (1) Always reaching for recall stems. (2) Firing stems without matching the level. (3) Stacking several stems in one turn.

**Related.** [[Bloom-levelled questioning]], [[../methods/literacy-across-curriculum|sentence stems & oracy scaffolds]], [[Accountable Talk]].
