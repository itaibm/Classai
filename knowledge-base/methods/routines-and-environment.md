# Routines & environment methods

Methods that treat **habits, climate, and the learning environment as teaching tools** in their own right — adapted for a *solo child at home with a parent and an AI tutor*, not a class of thirty. Routines free up attention, a safe-to-be-wrong climate frees up risk-taking, good pacing protects focus, and the physical/screen environment shapes whether learning can happen at all.

**Reframe for the home 1:1 setting.** Most "behaviour management" advice assumes a class to control. Here there is one willing (or sometimes reluctant) child, a parent nearby, and the AI tutor. So this family is less about *crowd control* and more about: building **rapport and a calm climate**, establishing light **routines and rituals** that lower friction, managing the **single child's attention, frustration and energy**, and shaping the **home + screen environment**. The AI tutor can carry much of the routine; the parent owns the physical setup and the relationship backstop.

## Learning routines & "Do Now" starters

**Family:** Routines & environment · **Evidence:** moderate–strong — (routines reduce extraneous load; "Do Now"/bell-work, Lemov; retrieval starters, EEF) · **Best for:** the opening of every session, ages 5–11.

**What it is.** A predictable opening routine — often a short **"Do Now"** task the child starts immediately (a quick retrieval warm-up, a puzzle) — that signals "we're learning now" and gets the brain into gear without dead time or negotiation.

**Why it works.** Predictable routines become automatic, freeing working memory and attention for *learning* rather than figuring out what's happening. A retrieval-based "Do Now" also strengthens prior memory (the testing effect) and gives an instant readiness read. It removes the friction of "starting."

**When to use / when NOT.** Use to open *every* session — consistency is the point. NOT a long or hard starter that becomes a barrier; NOT busywork unrelated to learning. Keep it to a couple of quick, gettable items.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Same predictable opening each time (greeting → quick warm-up).
2. The warm-up is a short retrieval of *prior* learning (2–4 easy items).
3. Read the result; transition smoothly into today's goal.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** This is the **hook beat as a "Do Now" retrieval starter**: the tutor opens consistently and poses 2–4 quick items drawn from `LearnerModel` topics due for spaced review — getting the child going while strengthening memory and reading readiness. The same warm greeting + warm-up each session builds the routine that lowers start-up friction (especially valuable for autistic learners and those with ADHD).

**Primary example.** Every maths session starts the same way: "Hi Maya! Quick warm-up first — 6 + 7? What's half of 10? What did we call the bottom of a fraction?" Then into today's learning.

**Pitfalls.** No routine (every session a cold, negotiated start); a starter too hard (barrier, not warm-up); the same items forever (no spacing).

## Establishing attention & focus

**Family:** Routines & environment · **Evidence:** strong — (attention as the gateway to learning; cognitive load; "no learning without attention") · **Best for:** the start of any focused work, all ages, especially ADHD/young children.

**What it is.** Deliberately securing the child's attention *before* teaching, and structuring the session to *sustain* it — clear single focus, removing competing distractions, short engaging segments, and re-gaining attention gently when it drifts.

**Why it works.** Nothing enters memory without attention. Young children and those with ADHD have shorter, more fragile attention; competing stimuli (notifications, toys, noise) win easily. Securing and protecting attention is the precondition for everything else.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use at the start and continuously. NOT by demanding sustained attention beyond the child's span (set realistic segment lengths); NOT by treating drift as defiance.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Gain attention first ("ready? eyes here / listen to this…") before key content.
2. One clear focus at a time; remove competing distractions.
3. Keep segments within the child's span; vary activity to refresh attention.
4. Re-gain drift warmly with a hook, a question, or a break — not a reprimand.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The talking character + speech bubble naturally orient attention; the director keeps **one focus per beat** and varies block types to refresh it. When `WorkingMemory` signals disengagement (slow/empty responses), the tutor re-hooks with the child's interest, a quick interactive block, or a brain break — never a telling-off. Segment length adapts to the child's profile in `LearnerModel`.

**Primary example.** A Year 1 child drifting during phonics: the tutor pauses, says "Watch this!", animates the letter forming, and turns it into a quick "your turn — trace it" — attention recaptured through novelty, not scolding.

**Pitfalls.** Teaching the key point before attention is secured; sessions longer than the child's span; reprimanding drift; cluttered, distracting displays.

## Behaviour-for-learning basics (home 1:1)

**Family:** Routines & environment · **Evidence:** moderate — (clear expectations, positive reinforcement; EEF "Improving Behaviour"; PBIS principles, adapted) · **Best for:** keeping a solo learner engaged and cooperative, ages 5–11.

**What it is.** The minimal set of behaviour supports for productive learning at home: a few clear, positively-framed expectations; warm, specific encouragement of effort and on-task behaviour; consistent gentle responses to off-task behaviour; and protecting the learning relationship throughout. (For one child, this is "engagement support," not crowd control.)

**Why it works.** Clear expectations remove ambiguity; catching and naming *good* behaviour/effort reinforces it far better than punishing the bad. Consistency builds trust and predictability. For a solo child, the *relationship* is the main lever — a child works for someone they like and trust.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use as a quiet backdrop, mostly through positive reinforcement. NOT heavy sanctions or a points-economy that turns learning into compliance-for-reward (undermines intrinsic motivation). The *parent* owns real behaviour boundaries; the AI encourages and re-engages.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Agree a few simple, positive expectations ("we try, we're kind to mistakes, we have a go").
2. Notice and name effort/on-task behaviour specifically ("you stuck with that tricky one").
3. Respond to off-task calmly: re-engage, adjust difficulty, or break — diagnose *why*.
4. Keep the relationship warm; never shame.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The tutor's encouragement is **process-praise** ("you didn't give up on that one") woven into feedback; off-task signals trigger *diagnosis* (too hard? too easy? tired?) and an adjustment, not a reprimand. Persistent disengagement is surfaced to the **parent dashboard** — the AI re-engages and informs; the parent handles genuine behaviour limits. No points/reward economy that crowds out intrinsic interest.

**Primary example.** A child starts giving silly answers. Rather than scold, the tutor reads it as boredom or fatigue, says "I think these got too easy — want a real challenge, or a quick break?" — re-engaging through respect.

**Pitfalls.** Sanction-heavy approaches with one child (damages the relationship); reward economies that kill intrinsic motivation; shaming; missing that "misbehaviour" is usually a signal (bored/lost/tired).

**Related.** [[building-rapport-and-a-safe-to-be-wrong-climate]], [[managing-frustration-and-the-struggle-moment]], [[behaviour-for-learning-basics]] pairs with motivation methods (`methods/` part 2).

## Building rapport & a safe-to-be-wrong climate

**Family:** Routines & environment · **Evidence:** strong — (teacher-student relationships, Hattie d≈0.5+; psychological safety; error climate) · **Best for:** the foundation of *all* learning, all ages — the single highest-leverage climate factor.

**What it is.** Deliberately building a warm relationship and a classroom-of-one culture where **mistakes are welcome, expected, and useful** — so the child takes the risks (attempting, guessing, admitting confusion) that learning requires. "Safe to be wrong" is the precondition for honest formative assessment and for productive struggle.

**Why it works.** Learning requires risk; a child who fears looking stupid hides confusion, guesses safe, and disengages. A warm relationship and a blameless error climate remove that fear, so the child *reveals* their real thinking (which you can then teach to) and persists through difficulty. Relationship quality predicts engagement and outcomes.

**When to use / when NOT.** Always — it's foundational, built from session one and protected continuously. NOTHING about it is optional. The only "when not": don't let "safe" tip into *no challenge* — safety enables challenge, it doesn't replace it.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Be warm, interested in the child as a person (use their interests/name).
2. Frame mistakes as information and normal: "great mistake — it shows exactly what to practise."
3. Never shame a wrong answer; respond with curiosity ("how did you get that?").
4. Celebrate effort and recovery, not just correctness; keep it patient and unhurried.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The character's warm, patient persona and the `LearnerModel` interests build rapport ("I remembered you love space — let's count with rockets"). The wrong-answer→remedy loop is *inherently* blameless: errors trigger a guiding hint and a "good mistake," never a "wrong." The tutor probes reasoning with curiosity, praises effort/strategy, and never rushes or shames — making it genuinely safe to be wrong, which makes the formative loop honest.

**Primary example.** A child whispers "I don't get it." The tutor: "That's a *great* thing to tell me — it means we found the exact bit to work on together. Let's look at it a different way." Confusion becomes welcome, not shameful.

**Pitfalls.** Empty/dishonest praise (erodes trust); any hint of "wrong!" or impatience; safety with no challenge (comfortable but no learning); forgetting the child's interests (cold, generic).

**Related.** [[managing-frustration-and-the-struggle-moment]], [[behaviour-for-learning-basics]]; feedback-that-moves-learning-forward (assessment methods); motivation methods (`methods/` part 2).

## Managing frustration & the "struggle" moment

**Family:** Routines & environment · **Evidence:** moderate–strong — (productive struggle / desirable difficulty, Bjork; growth mindset, Dweck — with honest caveats) · **Best for:** the moment a child hits genuine difficulty, all ages — the make-or-break point.

**What it is.** Recognising and skilfully handling the moment a child meets real difficulty: keeping them in **productive struggle** (effortful but progressing) and out of **destructive frustration** (overwhelmed, shutting down) — through encouragement, the right-sized hint, normalising difficulty, and knowing when to ease off.

**Why it works.** Learning lives at the edge of difficulty — too easy is no learning, too hard is collapse. The skill is keeping the child *in the productive band*: enough challenge to grow, enough support and morale to persist. How a child experiences struggle (as "I can't" vs "I can't *yet*") shapes whether they push on.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use at every point of difficulty. NOT to leave a child floundering in the name of "struggle" (that's just frustration — back off, scaffold, shrink the step); NOT to rescue them instantly from *productive* difficulty (robs the learning). Read which it is.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Distinguish productive struggle (engaged, trying) from destructive frustration (distressed, shutting down).
2. If productive: encourage, give wait time, a small hint at most — let them push.
3. If destructive: ease off — shrink the step, scaffold, switch representation, or take a break; restore success and morale.
4. Normalise it: "this is the hard, brain-growing part — everyone finds it tricky."

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The director's **struggle streak** is exactly this gauge: a *little* struggle → continue with encouragement; **two misses in a row → "stuck"** → it stops advancing, shrinks to the smallest next step, scaffolds, or changes representation (`assessment-and-feedback.md` §6). The tutor reframes difficulty positively ("this is the brain-growing bit"), gives wait time, and can suggest a brain break when frustration (not productive struggle) is detected. It never abandons a child to flounder, nor rescues them from healthy effort.

**Primary example.** A child stuck on a subtraction with exchange, getting visibly upset: the tutor says "Let's make this easier for a sec — here are ten cubes," drops to a concrete model and a smaller step, rebuilds a win, *then* climbs back up — frustration defused, learning preserved.

**Pitfalls.** Mistaking frustration for productive struggle (child drowns); rescuing too soon (no learning); "you can do it!" with no actual support (hollow); ignoring the emotional state.

**Related.** [[building-rapport-and-a-safe-to-be-wrong-climate]], [[brain-breaks-and-pacing]]; scaffolding-and-fading & cognitive-load reduction (differentiation methods); motivation/growth-mindset (`methods/` part 2).

## Brain breaks & pacing

**Family:** Routines & environment · **Evidence:** moderate — (attention spans, spacing/breaks; physical activity & cognition; against marathon sessions) · **Best for:** sustaining focus and energy across a session, ages 5–11, especially ADHD/early years.

**What it is.** Deliberately varying intensity and inserting short **brain breaks** (a stretch, a wiggle, a quick game, a drink) to reset attention and energy — and **pacing** the session so it's neither a sluggish crawl nor a relentless sprint. Short, focused work beats long, fading work.

**Why it works.** Attention and working memory fatigue; a brief break (especially with movement) restores them, and *distributed* effort beats massed (spacing). For young and ADHD learners, breaks aren't a luxury — they're what makes the *next* chunk productive. Good pacing keeps the child in the zone between bored and overwhelmed.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use breaks before attention collapses (proactively, not only after meltdown); pace to the child's energy. NOT breaks so long/frequent they fragment learning; NOT pushing through obvious fatigue ("just finish this page") — it's wasted time.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Keep work segments within the child's span; watch for fatigue signals.
2. Insert a short, ideally active, break *before* focus collapses.
3. Vary pace and activity type to match energy (livelier when flagging, calmer when overexcited).
4. Return with a quick re-hook to the goal.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The director paces in **short beats** and can **prompt a brain break** when `WorkingMemory` shows fading responses or a frustration spike — "Let's stretch and wiggle for a minute, then finish strong." It varies block types to modulate energy and adapts segment length to the child's `LearnerModel` profile. A break is reframed as part of learning, not a reward for enduring it.

**Primary example.** Mid-session a Year 2 child is fidgeting and slowing: the tutor calls "Brain break! Stand up, touch your toes 5 times, shake it out — then we'll do the fun last bit." Two minutes later, refocused.

**Pitfalls.** Breaks only after a meltdown (too late); breaks so frequent learning fragments; marathon sessions ignoring fatigue; not re-hooking after the break.

**Related.** [[establishing-attention-and-focus]], [[managing-frustration-and-the-struggle-moment]], [[screen-time-aware-design]]; spacing (retrieval methods).

## Session opening & closing rituals

**Family:** Routines & environment · **Evidence:** moderate — (routines/rituals reduce load & anxiety; primacy/recency; closure aids consolidation) · **Best for:** bookending every session predictably, all ages, especially autistic learners.

**What it is.** Small, consistent **rituals** that begin and end every session — a familiar greeting and warm-up to open; a recap, a celebration of progress, and a clear "we're finished" to close. The predictability itself is the support.

**Why it works.** Predictable bookends reduce anxiety and start-up friction (the child knows what's coming), exploit primacy/recency (strong openings and closings are best remembered), and the closing recap consolidates learning while the celebration builds positive association with the subject. Especially powerful for autistic children, who thrive on predictability.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use *every* session — consistency is the whole point. NOT rigidly identical to the point of tedium (keep the *shape* constant, vary the content). Always signal the *end* clearly (don't trail off).

**How to run it (steps).**
1. **Open** the same way: warm greeting → goal-of-the-day → warm-up.
2. Teach the lesson.
3. **Close** the same way: recap the core idea, celebrate a specific win, signal completion ("great work — we're done for today").
4. Optionally preview next time ("next we'll…").

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Classai's lesson shape *is* the ritual: a consistent **hook (greeting + warm-up + goal)** and a consistent **recap beat (core idea + specific praise + clear close)**. The recap is the exit-ticket consolidation; the celebration ("you nailed regrouping today!") draws on the session's `WorkingMemory` wins. Advance-warning of the end ("two more, then we finish") supports transitions for autistic and anxious children.

**Primary example.** Every session: "Hi Sam! Today we're learning about adding fractions. First, a warm-up…" → lesson → "Let's recap: the bottom number stays the same. You did brilliantly with that tricky one. We're all done — see you next time!"

**Pitfalls.** No clear ending (child left hanging); openings/closings skipped when rushed (lose the consolidation); rituals so fixed they bore; no advance warning of transitions.

**Related.** [[learning-routines-and-do-now-starters]], [[goal-of-the-day]]; exit tickets (assessment methods); spaced review (retrieval methods).

## Goal-of-the-day

**Family:** Routines & environment · **Evidence:** moderate–strong — (goal clarity / learning intentions, Hattie d≈0.5+; sharing the objective) · **Best for:** orienting the child to *what* and *why* at the start, ages 6–11.

**What it is.** Stating, in child-friendly language, the *one* thing this session is about and (briefly) why it matters — so the child knows where they're going and can recognise when they've arrived. A learning intention plus a sense of purpose.

**Why it works.** A clear goal orients effort and lets the child self-monitor ("am I getting closer?"); a touch of *why* ("so you can split a pizza fairly") supplies relevance and motivation. Goal clarity is among the better-evidenced teaching moves. It turns a session from "stuff happening" into "we're achieving X."

**When to use / when NOT.** Use at the start of every session, kept short and concrete. NOT a vague or jargon goal ("explore fractions"); NOT a list of five goals (pick one core). Tie to success criteria so "done" is recognisable.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. State the single core goal in plain, child language.
2. Add a quick, real *why* (relevance/usefulness).
3. Link to the success criteria ("you'll know you've got it when…").
4. Refer back at the close ("we set out to… and you did!").

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The hook beat states the **goal-of-the-day** drawn from the lesson plan, in child language, with a relevance hook tied to the child's `LearnerModel` interests where possible. The recap beat closes the loop ("we wanted to… and you nailed it"). The goal connects to the beat `successCriteria`, so the child knows the target throughout.

**Primary example.** "Today's mission: by the end, you'll be able to tell the time on a clock with hands — handy for knowing when football starts! You'll know you've got it when you can read three clocks in a row."

**Pitfalls.** Vague/jargony goals; too many goals; never referring back (the goal evaporates); no "why" (no motivation).

**Related.** [[session-opening-and-closing-rituals]], [[learning-routines-and-do-now-starters]]; success criteria & rubrics, feed-up (assessment methods).

## The physical / home learning environment

**Family:** Routines & environment · **Evidence:** moderate — (environment & distraction effects; home learning environment, EEF "Parental engagement") · **Best for:** the setup *around* the learning, all ages — owned by the parent.

**What it is.** Shaping the *physical* space and conditions for learning at home: a consistent, reasonably quiet spot with the needed materials, minimal distractions (TV off, toys away, notifications silenced), comfortable seating and lighting, and a parent positioned as a supportive backstop. The environment enables or sabotages everything else.

**Why it works.** Distractions compete with attention and win; a dedicated, prepared space signals "learning happens here" (a routine cue) and removes friction. The home environment and parental engagement are real predictors of learning. This is mostly the *parent's* lever, and a high-leverage one.

**When to use / when NOT.** Set up before sessions; maintain consistently. NOT a perfect study (any consistent, low-distraction corner works); NOT a parent hovering and answering for the child (supportive presence, not interference).

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Choose a consistent, low-distraction spot with good light and the materials ready.
2. Remove competing stimuli (TV, toys, phone notifications) before starting.
3. Position the parent as available backstop, not answer-giver.
4. Keep the device/screen at comfortable distance and brightness.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The AI can't control the room, but Classai can **prompt good setup** (a friendly "all set up somewhere comfy and quiet?") and is designed for **minimal on-screen distraction** (focused activity-forward layout, one thing at a time — see screen-time-aware design). The **parent dashboard** guides the adult on environment and on being a supportive backstop. Recognising that the parent owns the physical space, the app makes its part (the screen) calm and focused.

**Primary example.** Before a session, the tutor (or app onboarding) suggests: "Find a quiet spot at the table, get a pencil and paper handy, and pop the TV off — then we'll start." A consistent kitchen-table corner becomes the child's "school place."

**Pitfalls.** Learning amid TV/toys/notifications; a different chaotic spot each time; a parent who interferes or answers for the child; uncomfortable/eye-straining setup.

**Related.** [[screen-time-aware-design]], [[establishing-attention-and-focus]], [[behaviour-for-learning-basics]]; parental engagement.

## Screen-time-aware design

**Family:** Routines & environment · **Evidence:** moderate — (screen-time/eye-health guidance, e.g. 20-20-20; quality-over-quantity consensus; not all screen time equal) · **Best for:** any device-based learning, all ages — designing *with* screen-health in mind.

**What it is.** Designing and running device-based learning so it's healthy and focused: bounded session lengths, eye-care habits (breaks, distance), avoiding overstimulating/addictive design, prioritising *active/interactive* screen use over passive watching, and treating the screen as a tool with an off-switch, not an end in itself.

**Why it works.** Not all screen time is equal — *interactive, purposeful* use beats passive consumption; bounded sessions with breaks protect eyes and attention; calm, non-addictive design keeps the focus on learning rather than on engagement-maximising hooks. Modelling healthy screen habits matters for children.

**When to use / when NOT.** Apply to all device learning. NOT marathon screen sessions; NOT slot-machine gamification that hooks rather than teaches (see play methods' gamification *risks*). Favour active interaction and real breaks.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Keep sessions age-appropriately bounded; build in screen breaks (e.g. 20-20-20: every 20 min, look 20 ft away for 20 s).
2. Prefer *active* interaction (answering, creating) over passive watching.
3. Use calm, focused design — avoid overstimulating, attention-hijacking elements.
4. Make stopping clean and positive; the screen turns off when learning's done.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Classai is built for **active, focused** screen use: the child *does* something nearly every beat (answers, sorts, draws, speaks) rather than passively watching; the layout is calm and uncluttered (no engagement-maximising dark patterns); brain breaks include looking away from the screen; sessions are bounded with a clean close. Voice answers (Whisper) even let the child look *away* from the screen to respond. The design treats screen-health as a constraint, not an afterthought.

**Primary example.** During a 20-minute session, after a focused chunk the tutor says "Quick eye break — look out the window at something far away while you tell me your answer," combining a brain break, eye-care, and voice response.

**Pitfalls.** Long unbroken screen time; passive video-heavy sessions; addictive gamification; no eye breaks; a session that never cleanly *ends*.

**Related.** [[brain-breaks-and-pacing]], [[the-home-learning-environment]]; multimedia & video-done-well, gamification *risks* (technology and play methods).

## The routines & environment principle in one line

**Make the structure predictable (routines, rituals, clear goals) so attention is free for learning; make the climate warm and safe-to-be-wrong so the child takes the risks learning needs; pace with breaks so energy lasts; and shape the space and screen — calm, focused, bounded — so the environment helps rather than fights the lesson.**
