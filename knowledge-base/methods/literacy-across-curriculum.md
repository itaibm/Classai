# Literacy across the curriculum — reading, vocabulary & writing as methods

This family covers **literacy methods usable in *any* subject**, not just English lessons. A child who can't access the vocabulary of a science topic, or read its text, or write to consolidate it, is locked out — so explicit vocabulary teaching, comprehension strategies, oracy scaffolds, and writing-to-learn are general teaching tools every subject borrows. (Subject-specific phonics/reading-scheme detail lives in the English subject files; this is the *cross-curricular method* layer.) Don't duplicate [`pedagogy-core.md`](../teaching/pedagogy-core.md); this is the deeper catalogue.

> Cross-curricular truth: **vocabulary is the bottleneck.** The gap between children is largely a word gap; closing it is the highest-leverage literacy move in every subject.

---

## Explicit vocabulary instruction (Tier 2/3, Beck's robust vocabulary)

**Family:** Literacy across the curriculum · **Evidence:** strong — (Beck, McKeown & Kucan *Bringing Words to Life*; EEF "oral language" & vocabulary) · **Best for:** the words a topic *depends on*, in every subject; all ages.

**What it is.** Deliberately teaching chosen words rather than hoping children absorb them. Beck's three tiers: **Tier 1** everyday words (need no teaching), **Tier 2** high-utility cross-topic words (*compare, predict, represent, cause*) — the richest investment, and **Tier 3** subject-specific terms (*photosynthesis, denominator, peninsula*). "Robust" instruction means multiple rich, active encounters, not a definition copied once.

**Why it works.** Word knowledge is the strongest correlate of comprehension; you can't understand a text or a topic whose key words are opaque. Multiple meaningful encounters (definition + examples + using it) build deep, usable word knowledge, unlike a one-off dictionary look-up.

**When to use / when NOT.** Pre-teach and revisit the Tier 2/3 words a lesson hinges on, in any subject. Prioritise **Tier 2** (high payoff across topics) and the topic's essential **Tier 3** terms. NOT every unfamiliar word (overload) — pick the load-bearing few. A copied definition alone is not enough.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Select the few words the topic depends on (Tier 2 + essential Tier 3).
2. Give a child-friendly explanation (not a dictionary definition).
3. Show examples and non-examples; use it in context.
4. Have the child use it (say it, apply it) — multiple active encounters.
5. Revisit it (spaced) in later lessons.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The tutor pre-teaches a topic's key words in the *hook/explain* with a child-friendly explanation + example (often a Frayer model on `whiteboard`/`mindMap`), then *requires the child to use the word* in a check (`shortText`/`speak`), and the spaced-review system resurfaces it. Grounding files name the Tier 2/3 words per topic, so the tutor teaches the right ones rather than guessing.

**Primary example.** Science, Year 3 — before a lesson on plants, teach "absorb": "to soak up, like a sponge soaks up water — roots *absorb* water," then "use *absorb* in a sentence about roots."

**Pitfalls.** (1) Teaching too many words (overload). (2) Definition-copying with no use. (3) Teaching the word once and never revisiting.

## Frayer model & word-learning organisers

**Family:** Literacy across the curriculum · **Evidence:** strong — (Frayer 1969; structures deep word knowledge) · **Best for:** giving a single important word deep, four-sided treatment; ages 7–11.

**What it is.** A four-quadrant organiser for one word: **definition**, **characteristics/facts**, **examples**, **non-examples** (with the word in the centre). Related light forms: word maps, the "word in my own words / picture / sentence" frame.

**Why it works.** It forces the multiple, varied encounters robust vocabulary needs — especially the *non-examples*, which sharpen the word's boundary (knowing what a "peninsula" is *not* defines what it is). Concrete and visual, it suits children.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use for a topic's most important word(s). NOT for every word (too slow) — reserve for the load-bearing terms. Needs the child to *generate* parts, not just receive them.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Put the word in the centre.
2. Co-create a child-friendly definition.
3. List characteristics/facts.
4. Generate examples *and* non-examples (the key sharpening step).
5. Use the word in a sentence.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Build the Frayer on a `whiteboard`/`mindMap`; the tutor models one quadrant then the child generates the rest (generation effect), especially the non-examples (which double as a Concept-Attainment check). Stored in the learner model so revisits reuse it. This is the operational form of "robust vocabulary" for one key word.

**Primary example.** Geography, Year 5 — "island": definition (land surrounded by water), characteristics, examples (Britain), non-examples (a peninsula, a lake).

**Pitfalls.** (1) Filling it *for* the child (no generation). (2) Skipping non-examples (the most diagnostic quadrant). (3) Frayer-ing every word.

## Pre-teaching vocabulary

**Family:** Literacy across the curriculum · **Evidence:** strong — (EEF; front-loading key words removes the access barrier) · **Best for:** any lesson/text with words that would otherwise block comprehension; all ages.

**What it is.** Teaching the essential words *before* the lesson or text where they'll appear, so the child meets them already understanding the gatekeeper terms.

**Why it works.** Encountering an unknown key word *inside* new content forces the child to decode the word *and* grasp the concept at once — overloading working memory. Front-loading the words removes that double-load so attention goes to the new idea.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use before a text or lesson dense with unfamiliar essential terms — especially valuable for children with smaller vocabularies (closes the word gap). NOT for every word (only the gatekeepers). Don't let pre-teaching become a long vocabulary lesson that crowds out the content.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Scan the upcoming lesson/text for gatekeeper words.
2. Teach those few briefly *before* (child-friendly explanation + example).
3. Then meet them in context; the child now reads/listens with comprehension.
4. Revisit afterward.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** In the *hook*, the tutor front-loads the one or two gatekeeper words a topic needs before the *explain* beat, so the explanation lands. The analysis pass / grounding files supply `priorKnowledge` including key vocabulary. Keep it to seconds, not a sub-lesson.

**Primary example.** History, Year 4 — before a passage on the Romans, pre-teach "invade" and "conquer" so the passage is readable.

**Pitfalls.** (1) Pre-teaching too many words. (2) Front-loading so long the content gets squeezed. (3) Teaching words the child already knows.

**Related.** [[Explicit vocabulary instruction]], [[Reading comprehension strategies]], [[../methods/instruction-models|Advance Organisers]].

## Reciprocal teaching

**Family:** Literacy across the curriculum · **Evidence:** strong — (Palincsar & Brown 1984; EEF "reading comprehension strategies" +6mo) · **Best for:** building reading comprehension via four strategies; ages 8–11.

**What it is.** A structured dialogue around a text using four roles/strategies the child gradually takes over: **predict**, **clarify** (unclear words/ideas), **question** (generate questions about the text), and **summarise**. Classroom version rotates roles among pupils; in 1:1 the tutor and child share them.

**Why it works.** It makes the invisible strategies of skilled readers explicit and practised, and the gradual handover (teacher-led → child-led) builds the child's independent self-monitoring of comprehension — a strong driver of reading gains.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use with any text in any subject once the child can decode reasonably (it targets comprehension, not decoding). NOT for pre-readers (decode first). Needs modelling before handover, or it collapses.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Model all four strategies on a short text (think-aloud).
2. **Predict** what's coming.
3. Read a chunk; **clarify** anything unclear.
4. **Question** — generate a question about the chunk.
5. **Summarise** the chunk.
6. Gradually hand each strategy to the child.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The tutor runs the four-strategy cycle on a text (an `image`/passage), modelling each via think-aloud, then handing them to the child through `speak`/`shortText` prompts ("what do you predict?", "summarise that bit"). Maps onto gradual release: tutor-modelled → shared → child-led. The learner model tracks which of the four strategies the child still needs scaffolded.

**Primary example.** Science, Year 5 — reading a text on the water cycle: predict the next stage, clarify "evaporation", question "why does water rise?", summarise the paragraph.

**Pitfalls.** (1) Handing over before modelling. (2) Doing it on text too hard to decode (comprehension can't happen). (3) Mechanical role-play with no real thinking.

## Reading comprehension strategies (predict / clarify / question / summarise / infer / visualise)

**Family:** Literacy across the curriculum · **Evidence:** strong — (EEF "reading comprehension strategies" +6mo; National Reading Panel) · **Best for:** understanding texts in every subject; ages 7–11 (some from 6).

**What it is.** A toolkit of explicit strategies skilled readers use: **predicting**, **clarifying** unknowns, **questioning** the text, **summarising**, **inferring** (reading between the lines), **visualising** (making a mental picture), and **activating prior knowledge**. Reciprocal teaching bundles four of them; this is the broader set.

**Why it works.** Comprehension isn't automatic once you can decode — these strategies are what good readers *do*, and teaching them explicitly (with modelling and practice) lifts comprehension, especially for weaker readers. Inference in particular relies on background knowledge, linking comprehension to the wider curriculum.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use with texts across subjects once decoding is reasonable. NOT a replacement for *knowledge* — a strategy can't extract meaning a child has no background for (so build knowledge too). Teach one strategy at a time before combining.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Pick one strategy; name it.
2. Model it on a text (think-aloud).
3. Practise it together, then independently.
4. Combine strategies once each is secure.
5. Watch for the knowledge gaps inference exposes.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The tutor teaches one strategy per mini-lesson, modelling on a passage then prompting the child (`shortText`/`speak`) — "what can you infer about the character?", "picture the scene — describe it." Inference misses often reveal a *knowledge* gap the tutor then fills (tie to the subject knowledge base). Strategies are spaced and revisited.

**Primary example.** English, Year 4 — inference: "It says she grabbed her umbrella and sighed. What can you tell about the weather *and* her mood?"

**Pitfalls.** (1) Teaching strategies as a substitute for knowledge. (2) Doing all strategies at once. (3) Strategy drills on texts too hard to decode.

**Related.** [[Reciprocal teaching]], [[Close reading]], [[Pre-teaching vocabulary]], [[../methods/questioning-and-dialogue|Bloom-levelled questioning]].

## Close reading

**Family:** Literacy across the curriculum · **Evidence:** moderate — (careful re-reading of a short rich text; strong for analysis when text is well-chosen) · **Best for:** digging deep into a short, worthwhile text; ages 9–11.

**What it is.** Repeated, careful reading of a *short* text with text-dependent questions, attending closely to word choice, structure, and meaning — re-reading for different purposes each pass (gist → detail → author's craft).

**Why it works.** Multiple focused passes let the child notice what one reading skims past, and text-dependent questions force evidence *from the text* rather than vague opinion — building analytical reading.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use on a short, rich passage worth the attention (a poem, a key paragraph, a primary source). NOT on long or simple texts (tedious and pointless). Best for older primary; younger children tire of re-reading.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Choose a short, rich text.
2. First read: gist ("what's it about?").
3. Second read: detail / vocabulary.
4. Third read: craft / inference ("why this word?").
5. Ask text-dependent questions (answers grounded in the text).

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The tutor displays a short passage (`image`/text) and runs purposeful re-reads with text-dependent checks (`shortText`/`speak`), insisting answers cite the text ("which word tells you that?"). Pairs with Accountable Talk's "what's your evidence?". Keep texts short — re-reading a long passage overloads.

**Primary example.** History, Year 6 — close-read a 4-line eyewitness account of the Great Fire of London: gist, then "which words show fear?", then inference about the writer.

**Pitfalls.** (1) Close-reading long texts (exhausting). (2) Questions answerable without the text (defeats the point). (3) Over-analysing a thin text.

**Related.** [[Reading comprehension strategies]], [[Reciprocal teaching]], [[../methods/questioning-and-dialogue|Accountable Talk]].

## Writing-to-learn

**Family:** Literacy across the curriculum · **Evidence:** strong — (writing to consolidate is a form of retrieval + elaboration; effective across subjects) · **Best for:** consolidating understanding in *any* subject through short writing; ages 7–11.

**What it is.** Using brief, low-stakes writing to *think* and *consolidate* — explaining a concept in your own words, an exit ticket ("what did you learn?"), a quick paragraph summarising a science result — where the writing is a learning tool, not a polished product.

**Why it works.** Putting an idea into your own written words forces retrieval, elaboration, and the discovery of gaps (you can't write clearly what you don't understand). It's the generation effect and self-explanation in written form, and it works in every subject.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use to consolidate after teaching, in any subject. NOT as a graded writing-quality exercise (that flips it from thinking-tool to performance and adds anxiety). Keep it short; for the youngest, oral "writing-to-learn" (saying it) substitutes.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. After teaching, prompt a short write: "explain ___ in your own words."
2. Make it low-stakes (ideas, not spelling/neatness).
3. Read it for *understanding* and gaps.
4. Respond to the gaps next.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** Use a `shortText` exit-ticket or "explain it back" in the *recap* — the child writes/says the idea in their own words; the tutor judges *understanding* (not spelling) and the learner model logs gaps for spaced follow-up. For young children, `speak` (oral) substitutes. This is retrieval + self-explanation as a closing beat.

**Primary example.** Science, Year 4 — recap: "Write one sentence explaining why the puddle disappeared." reveals whether evaporation landed.

**Pitfalls.** (1) Grading it on writing quality (kills the thinking purpose). (2) Making it long. (3) Not acting on the gaps it reveals.

**Related.** [[../methods/retrieval-and-memory|the generation effect]], [[../methods/questioning-and-dialogue|self-explanation prompts]], [[Sentence stems & oracy scaffolds]].

## Sentence stems & oracy scaffolds (talk frames)

**Family:** Literacy across the curriculum · **Evidence:** strong — (oracy/EEF oral language; scaffolds extended, accurate talk and writing) · **Best for:** helping a child express reasoning in full, subject-appropriate language; all ages.

**What it is.** Provided sentence openers and frames that scaffold *how* to say/write an idea — "I think ___ because ___", "I agree with ___ because ___", "First… then… finally…", "The evidence is ___" — supporting both spoken reasoning (oracy) and writing.

**Why it works.** Children often *have* the thinking but lack the language structure to express it; a frame supplies the structure so the cognitive effort goes to the *idea*, not to constructing the sentence. It also models the academic register of each subject ("the evidence suggests…").

**When to use / when NOT.** Use whenever you want extended reasoning, justification, or subject-register talk/writing — and *fade* the frames as the child internalises them. NOT permanently (that creates dependence and formulaic talk). Match the frame to the thinking (a "because" frame for justification, a sequence frame for explaining a process).

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Choose a frame matching the thinking required.
2. Model using it.
3. Have the child speak/write with the frame.
4. Fade the frame as the child no longer needs it.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** The tutor supplies stems via `wordBank` / sentence starters on `fillBlank`/`shortText`/`speak` checks ("I think ___ because ___"), insisting on the *because* (ties to Accountable Talk), then strips the scaffold across sessions as the learner model shows the child can reason unaided. Frames also give the academic vocabulary of the subject.

**Primary example.** Science, Year 3 — "I predict the ice will ___ because ___" frames a reasoned prediction the child couldn't yet phrase alone.

**Pitfalls.** (1) Never fading (formulaic, dependent talk). (2) A frame mismatched to the thinking. (3) Frames that let the child fill blanks without real reasoning.

**Related.** [[../methods/questioning-and-dialogue|Accountable Talk]], [[Writing-to-learn]], [[../methods/instruction-models|scaffolding & fading]].

## Etymology & morphology

**Family:** Literacy across the curriculum · **Evidence:** strong — (morphological awareness aids spelling, vocabulary & reading; valuable for Tier 3 science/maths terms) · **Best for:** unlocking and remembering longer/technical words by their parts; ages 8–11.

**What it is.** Teaching the meaningful parts of words — **prefixes, roots, suffixes** (morphology) and word **origins** (etymology) — so the child can decode and remember unfamiliar words by understanding their structure (*tri-* = three, *-graph* = writing, *photo-* = light).

**Why it works.** Most longer/academic words are built from recurring parts; knowing the parts lets a child infer meaning and spelling of *new* words (a generative skill), and the meaning-link makes Tier 3 vocabulary far stickier than rote memorising. Especially powerful for science/maths/geography terms.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use for longer words with transparent, high-utility parts (especially subject vocabulary) and for spelling patterns. NOT for short irregular words where parts don't help (use mnemonics there). Don't over-analyse every word.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Break a target word into its meaningful parts.
2. Teach each part's meaning (with other words sharing it).
3. Reassemble to infer the whole word's meaning.
4. Apply the part to *predict* a new word's meaning.

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** When teaching a Tier 3 term, the tutor exposes its parts ("*photo* means light, *synthesis* means making — so *photosynthesis* is making with light") and connects to known words sharing a part, deepening and spacing vocabulary. The learner model stores known roots so the tutor can reuse them. Pairs with explicit vocabulary instruction.

**Primary example.** Maths, Year 4 — "triangle": *tri-* = three (tricycle, tripod), *-angle* = corner → "three corners."

**Pitfalls.** (1) Analysing words whose parts don't help. (2) Teaching parts in isolation, never applying them to new words. (3) Etymology trivia with no comprehension payoff.

## Knowledge organisers

**Family:** Literacy across the curriculum · **Evidence:** promising — (popular UK tool; effective *when used for retrieval*, not just handed out) · **Best for:** capturing a topic's core facts/vocabulary on one page for study and retrieval; ages 8–11.

**What it is.** A single page summarising a topic's essential knowledge — key vocabulary + definitions, core facts, dates, diagrams — designed so a child (and parent/tutor) can see the whole topic at a glance and self-test from it.

**Why it works.** It defines and bounds what's worth knowing (helping sequencing and review) and, crucially, serves as a **retrieval** tool — covering it and recalling, or quizzing from it — rather than a passive handout. It also lists the Tier 3 vocabulary explicitly.

**When to use / when NOT.** Use as a reference + retrieval base for a topic across its lessons and reviews. NOT as something merely read/copied (passive = low value) — the gain is in retrieving *from* it and being quizzed *on* it. Don't overcrowd it.

**How to run it (steps).**
1. Distil the topic to its essential vocabulary, facts, and one or two diagrams.
2. Use it to *pre-teach* and frame the topic.
3. Mainly: self-test from it (cover and recall; quiz on it).
4. Revisit across the topic (spacing/cumulative review).

**1:1 AI-tutor adaptation.** A topic's knowledge organiser is essentially the grounding file's `keyConcepts` + vocabulary + `resources` rendered for the child; the tutor uses it to frame the topic (advance organiser), to source pre-taught vocabulary, and — most importantly — as the bank the spaced-review system quizzes *from* (retrieval), never just displays. Pairs with brain dumps (recall the organiser from memory).

**Primary example.** History, Year 5 — a one-page "Ancient Egypt" organiser (key words: pharaoh, pyramid, Nile; key facts; a timeline) the tutor quizzes from across the unit.

**Pitfalls.** (1) Handing it out to read (passive, low value). (2) Overcrowding it. (3) Never using it for retrieval (its main purpose).
