# Year 6 Mathematics (ages 10–11) — Upper Key Stage 2

Year 6 consolidates and extends: numbers to 10,000,000, order of operations, long division, all four operations with fractions, percentage/decimal/fraction equivalence, area and volume formulae, circles, four-quadrant coordinates, pie charts and the mean, and the new domains of **ratio & proportion** and **algebra**. This is the end of primary maths and the SATs year.

## 1. Numbers to 10,000,000 and order of operations

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Number – number & place value / four operations

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 place value to a million.

### Key concepts
- Read, write, order and round numbers to 10,000,000.
- Negative numbers are used in context and across zero.
- Order of operations (BIDMAS/BODMAS): brackets, then indices, then ×÷, then +−.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Place value to a million; rounding; the four operations.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Place value extends to **ten millions** (8 digits); group in threes. **Order of operations:** when a calculation mixes operations, do them in a set order so everyone gets the same answer — **B**rackets, **I**ndices (powers), **D**ivision/**M**ultiplication (left to right), **A**ddition/**S**ubtraction (left to right). So 2 + 3 × 4 = 2 + 12 = 14, *not* 20 — multiplication happens before addition. Brackets force a different order: (2 + 3) × 4 = 20.

**Worked example.** 5 + 2 × 3² . Indices first: 3² = 9. Then ×: 2 × 9 = 18. Then +: 5 + 18 = **23**. Compare (5 + 2) × 3 = 21.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- National population figures.
- Money in big budgets.
- Calculator/spreadsheet rules (it follows BIDMAS too).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** 2 + 3 × 4 = 20 (works strictly left to right).
  **Why:** ignores operation priority.
  **Fix:** ×/÷ before +/−; 3 × 4 = 12 first, then + 2 = 14.
- **Wrong:** treats BIDMAS as "division always before multiplication."
  **Why:** reads the order too literally.
  **Fix:** ÷ and × are equal rank — do them left to right; same for + and −.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 3 + 4 × 2 = ? **A:** 11.
- **Q:** (3 + 4) × 2 = ? **A:** 14.
- **Q:** 20 − 6 ÷ 2 = ? **A:** 17 (÷ first: 6÷2=3, then 20−3).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Apply BIDMAS to mixed calculations (with and without brackets/indices); order and round large numbers.

## 2. Long multiplication and long division

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Number – multiplication & division

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 long multiplication & short division.

### Key concepts
- Multiply 4-digit by 2-digit numbers fluently with long multiplication.
- Divide by a 2-digit number using long division, with remainders as whole numbers, fractions or decimals.
- Estimate, and check by inverse.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Long multiplication; short division; tables to 12.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Long division** by a 2-digit number: it helps to first write a small "facts list" of the divisor (e.g. for ÷15: 15, 30, 45, 60, 75…). Then divide left to right, subtracting the biggest multiple that fits, bringing down the next digit. **Remainders** can be left as r, or written as a fraction (r/divisor) or carried into decimals by adding .0s. **Estimate** to size-check (432 ÷ 12 ≈ 430 ÷ 12 ≈ 36).

**Worked example.** 432 ÷ 12. List 12s: 12, 24, 36, 48. 43 ÷ 12 = 3 (3×12 = 36), remainder 7; bring down 2 → 72; 72 ÷ 12 = 6. Answer **36**. Check: 36 × 12 = 432. ✓

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sharing a big bill among many people.
- How many full coaches (each holds 53) for 800 people?
- Cost per item from a bulk total.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** in long division, forgets to bring down the next digit, ending early.
  **Why:** stops after the first step.
  **Fix:** keep going until every digit is used; bring each one down in turn.
- **Wrong:** mishandles a remainder (ignores it, or writes r as the next quotient digit).
  **Why:** doesn't carry the remainder into the next bring-down.
  **Fix:** combine the remainder with the next digit (e.g. r7 + the 2 = 72) before dividing again.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 528 ÷ 12 = ? **A:** 44.
- **Q:** 234 × 23 = ? **A:** 5,382.
- **Q:** 437 ÷ 15 = ? (whole + remainder) **A:** 29 remainder 2.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Long multiplication (e.g. 1,234 × 24) and long division by 2-digit divisors, expressing remainders as whole, fraction and decimal.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Long division; long multiplication.**
- **Oak National Academy — Year 6 written methods units.**

## 3. Four operations with fractions

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 add/subtract/multiply fractions.

### Key concepts
- Add/subtract fractions by finding a common denominator.
- Multiply fractions by multiplying numerators and denominators.
- Divide a fraction by a whole number (and simplify answers).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Equivalent fractions; multiply fractions by whole numbers; common denominators.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Add/subtract:** common denominator, then add/subtract numerators (½ + ⅓ = 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6). **Multiply two fractions:** multiply tops and multiply bottoms (⅔ × ¾ = 6/12 = ½); a fraction of a fraction is *smaller*, which surprises children. **Divide a fraction by a whole number:** ½ ÷ 3 means split a half into 3 equal parts → 1/6 (multiply the denominator by 3). **Always simplify** the answer.

**Worked example.** ⅔ × ¾ = (2×3)/(3×4) = 6/12 = **½**. And ½ ÷ 2 = 1/4 (a half shared into 2). And ¾ − ⅖ = 15/20 − 8/20 = **7/20**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- ⅔ of ¾ of a cake left.
- Splitting half a pizza among 3 people.
- Scaling a recipe down by a fraction.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** to multiply fractions, finds a common denominator first (as for adding).
  **Why:** over-applies the addition rule.
  **Fix:** multiplying needs no common denominator — just multiply across (tops × tops, bottoms × bottoms).
- **Wrong:** thinks ⅔ × ¾ should be bigger than ¾.
  **Why:** assumes multiplying always enlarges.
  **Fix:** taking a fraction *of* something gives less — ⅔ of ¾ is part of ¾, so smaller.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** ⅖ × ½ = ? **A:** 2/10 = 1/5.
- **Q:** ⅓ ÷ 2 = ? **A:** 1/6.
- **Q:** ¾ + ⅙ = ? **A:** 9/12 + 2/12 = 11/12.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Mixed fraction operations (×, ÷ by whole numbers, + and − with unlike denominators); simplify each answer.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Multiplying and dividing fractions.**
- **Khan Academy — Multiplying & dividing fractions.**

## 4. Fractions, decimals and percentages: equivalence

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 26 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions, decimals & percentages

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 percentages and decimals.

### Key concepts
- Any proportion can be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage.
- Convert between them: fraction → decimal (divide), decimal → percentage (× 100).
- Use the form that makes a problem easiest, and find percentages of amounts.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Percentages; decimals; equivalent fractions; division.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The three notations are interchangeable. **Fraction → decimal:** divide top by bottom (¾ = 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75). **Decimal → percentage:** × 100 (0.75 = 75%). **Percentage → fraction:** over 100 and simplify (75% = 75/100 = ¾). **Memorise the common set:** ½=0.5=50%, ¼=0.25=25%, ¾=0.75=75%, 1/10=0.1=10%, 1/5=0.2=20%, ⅛=0.125=12.5%. **Percentage of an amount:** use 10%/1% building blocks or convert to a fraction.

**Worked example.** Express ⅗ as a decimal and percentage: 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6 = **60%**. Find 60% of 35: 10% of 35 = 3.5, × 6 = **21**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sale: "25% off" vs "¼ off" vs "0.25 of the price."
- Test results as fractions and percentages.
- Statistics in sport (% of shots scored).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** converts ¾ to a decimal as 0.34 (writes the digits).
  **Why:** doesn't divide; just lines up the numerator and denominator.
  **Fix:** divide numerator by denominator: 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75.
- **Wrong:** 0.6 = 6% (forgets ×100).
  **Why:** confuses decimal and percentage.
  **Fix:** to go decimal → %, multiply by 100: 0.6 = 60%.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Write ⅘ as a decimal and a percentage. **A:** 0.8 and 80%.
- **Q:** What fraction is 40%? **A:** 40/100 = ⅖.
- **Q:** Find 75% of 40. **A:** 30.

### Activity / practice
**matchPairs / numberEntry:** Match equivalent fraction/decimal/percentage triples; convert in all directions; find percentages of amounts.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Fractions, decimals and percentages.**
- **Khan Academy — Converting fractions, decimals & percents.**

## 5. Ratio and proportion

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 28 · **Subject strand:** Ratio & proportion

**Prerequisites:** Fractions; multiplication & division; FDP equivalence.

### Key concepts
- A ratio compares two quantities (3 : 2 means 3 of one for every 2 of the other).
- Proportion relates a part to the whole; scaling keeps the ratio the same.
- Share a quantity in a given ratio; use scaling for recipes and similar problems.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Multiplication and division; fractions of amounts.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **ratio** like 3 : 2 says "for every 3 of these, there are 2 of those." **Concrete:** for every 3 red counters put down 2 blue. **Sharing in a ratio:** to share 20 sweets in the ratio 3 : 2, add the parts (3 + 2 = 5 parts), find one part (20 ÷ 5 = 4), then multiply: 3 parts = 12, 2 parts = 8. **Proportion / scaling:** if 2 apples cost 60p, then 6 apples (×3) cost £1.80 — scale both by the same factor. Recipes scale this way.

**Worked example.** Share 30 marbles between Amy and Ben in the ratio 2 : 3. Parts = 2 + 3 = 5; one part = 30 ÷ 5 = 6; Amy gets 2 × 6 = **12**, Ben gets 3 × 6 = **18** (check 12 + 18 = 30 ✓).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Mixing squash (1 part juice : 4 parts water).
- Sharing winnings or sweets between friends.
- Scaling a recipe up for more people.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** shares 20 in ratio 3 : 2 as 3 and 2 (just splits off those amounts).
  **Why:** treats the ratio numbers as the actual amounts.
  **Fix:** the numbers are *parts*; find the value of one part (total ÷ total parts), then multiply.
- **Wrong:** confuses ratio 3 : 2 with the fraction "3 of the whole."
  **Why:** mixes part-to-part with part-to-whole.
  **Fix:** 3 : 2 means 3 out of every *5* — the whole is 5 parts, not 3.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Share 15 in the ratio 2 : 3. **A:** 6 and 9.
- **Q:** Squash is 1 : 4 juice to water; for 1 cup of juice, how much water? **A:** 4 cups.
- **Q:** If 3 pens cost 90p, how much for 5? **A:** £1.50 (one pen 30p).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Share amounts in given ratios; scale recipes/prices proportionally; "for every… how many…" problems.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Ratio and proportion.**
- **Khan Academy — Intro to ratios.**
- **NRICH — primary ratio and proportion problems.**

## 6. Algebra: formulae, expressions and equations

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 28 · **Subject strand:** Algebra

**Prerequisites:** The four operations; order of operations; missing-number problems.

### Key concepts
- A letter can stand for an unknown or a variable number.
- A formula is a rule written with letters (e.g. perimeter P = 2(l + w)).
- Solve simple one- and two-step equations by keeping both sides balanced; find pairs of values; continue and describe sequences.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Inverse operations; missing-number sentences; substitution into rules.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Algebra is "missing-number" maths with letters. A letter is just a number we don't know yet. **Substitute** values into a formula: if a = 3, then 2a + 1 = 2×3 + 1 = 7. **Solve an equation** by doing the same to both sides to undo operations (keep the scales balanced): x + 5 = 12 → subtract 5 from both sides → x = 7. **Two-step:** 2x + 3 = 11 → −3 both sides → 2x = 8 → ÷2 → x = 4. **Sequences:** spot the rule (e.g. +3 each time) to continue or find the nth pattern. **Enumerate** possibilities: a + b = 10 has pairs (1,9),(2,8)…

**Worked example.** Solve 3n − 4 = 11. Add 4 to both sides: 3n = 15. Divide both sides by 3: **n = 5**. Check: 3×5 − 4 = 11 ✓.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A rule for working out cost: total = 5 × items + 2 (delivery).
- Perimeter formula for a fence.
- Patterns of matchsticks/tiles growing by a fixed amount.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** reads 2a as "2 then a" (twenty-something) or as 2 + a.
  **Why:** doesn't know 2a means 2 × a.
  **Fix:** a number next to a letter means multiply: 2a = 2 × a.
- **Wrong:** solves x + 5 = 12 by doing something to only one side.
  **Why:** breaks the balance.
  **Fix:** whatever you do to one side, do to the other; subtract 5 from *both* sides.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** If y = 4, what is 3y + 2? **A:** 14.
- **Q:** Solve x − 7 = 10. **A:** x = 17.
- **Q:** Solve 2x + 1 = 9. **A:** x = 4.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / fillBlank:** Substitute into formulae; solve one- and two-step equations; continue a sequence and state its rule; list value-pairs that satisfy a + b = n.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Algebra (formulae, equations, sequences).**
- **Khan Academy — Intro to variables and equations.**

## 7. Area, perimeter and volume (formulae)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 26 · **Subject strand:** Measurement

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 area, perimeter & volume.

### Key concepts
- Use formulae: rectangle area = l × w; triangle area = ½ × base × height; cuboid volume = l × w × h.
- Calculate area of triangles and parallelograms; volume of cuboids.
- Convert between units (cm² ↔ m², ml ↔ l) and pick the right unit.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Area/perimeter of rectangles; volume of cuboids; multiplication.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A triangle is **half a rectangle**: area = ½ × base × height (the perpendicular height, not a slanted side). A **parallelogram** has area base × height (slide the triangle from one end to the other to make a rectangle). **Cuboid volume** = length × width × height. **Concrete/pictorial:** cut a rectangle diagonally → two equal triangles. **Unit conversion:** 1 cm = 10 mm, 1 m = 100 cm, 1 litre = 1000 ml; for area, 1 cm² = 100 mm². Choose sensible units (a room in m, a stamp in mm).

**Worked example.** Triangle with base 8 cm, height 5 cm: area = ½ × 8 × 5 = **20 cm²**. Cuboid 5 × 4 × 3 cm: volume = 5 × 4 × 3 = **60 cm³**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Area of a triangular flag or sail.
- Volume of a packing box (and whether items fit).
- Converting a recipe's litres to millilitres.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** triangle area = base × height (forgets the ½).
  **Why:** doesn't see a triangle as half a rectangle.
  **Fix:** two copies of the triangle make a rectangle; so area is *half* of base × height.
- **Wrong:** uses a slanted side as the "height" of a triangle/parallelogram.
  **Why:** confuses the perpendicular height with a sloping edge.
  **Fix:** height is the *perpendicular* distance (straight up from the base).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Area of a triangle, base 10 cm, height 6 cm? **A:** 30 cm².
- **Q:** Volume of a 2×3×4 cm cuboid? **A:** 24 cm³.
- **Q:** How many ml in 1.5 litres? **A:** 1500 ml.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Area of triangles and parallelograms; volume of cuboids; unit conversions (length, area, capacity).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Area of triangles; volume; units of measure.**
- **Oak National Academy — Year 6 measurement units.**

## 8. Circles, angles and nets

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Geometry – properties of shapes

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 angles; 2-D/3-D shapes.

### Key concepts
- A circle has a radius (centre to edge), a diameter (across through the centre) and a circumference (the distance around).
- The diameter is twice the radius.
- Angle facts: angles in a triangle sum to 180°; vertically opposite angles are equal; build solids from their nets.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Measuring angles; properties of shapes; faces/edges/vertices.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Circle parts:** the **radius** runs from the centre to the edge; the **diameter** crosses the whole circle through the centre and = **2 × radius**; the **circumference** is the perimeter of the circle. **Angle facts to use:** the three angles in any triangle add to **180°** (so a missing angle = 180 − the other two); where two straight lines cross, **vertically opposite** angles are equal. **Nets:** a flat pattern that folds into a 3-D shape — a cube's net is 6 squares arranged so they fold up; match nets to solids.

**Worked example.** A circle has radius 4 cm → diameter = 2 × 4 = **8 cm**. A triangle has angles 70° and 60°; the third = 180 − (70 + 60) = **50°**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Wheels and clocks (radius/diameter).
- Folding a cardboard net into a box.
- Roof and ramp angles.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks the radius is the distance across the whole circle.
  **Why:** confuses radius with diameter.
  **Fix:** radius = centre to edge; diameter = edge to edge through the centre (= 2 radii).
- **Wrong:** says a triangle's angles add to 360°.
  **Why:** confuses with angles around a point / quadrilaterals.
  **Fix:** tear off a triangle's three corners and place them on a straight line — they make 180°.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** A circle's diameter is 10 cm — what's the radius? **A:** 5 cm.
- **Q:** Two angles of a triangle are 90° and 45° — find the third. **A:** 45°.
- **Q:** How many squares are in the net of a cube? **A:** 6.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / matchPairs:** Find radius/diameter; missing angles in triangles and at crossing lines; match nets to their 3-D shapes.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Circles; angles in shapes; nets.**
- **Topmarks — net and angle activities.**

## 9. Coordinates in four quadrants

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Geometry – position & direction

**Prerequisites:** Year 4/5 coordinates; negative numbers; reflection.

### Key concepts
- A full coordinate grid has four quadrants using positive and negative x and y.
- Plot and read points anywhere, including with negative coordinates.
- Reflecting/translating shapes can move them across the axes.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- (x, y) coordinates; negative numbers; reflection across an axis.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Extend both axes through zero so x and y can be **negative**. The grid splits into **four quadrants**: top-right (+,+), top-left (−,+), bottom-left (−,−), bottom-right (+,−). To plot (−3, 2): go 3 *left* (negative x), 2 *up*. **Reflection** across the y-axis flips the x sign; across the x-axis flips the y sign. **Translation** still slides every point the same way, now possibly across zero.

**Worked example.** Plot (−2, −3): 2 left, 3 down (bottom-left quadrant). Reflect (3, 2) across the y-axis → flip x sign → **(−3, 2)**. Reflect (3, 2) across the x-axis → flip y sign → (3, −2).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Map grids with directions both ways from a centre.
- Game coordinates with negative positions.
- Temperature/profit graphs crossing zero.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** plots (−3, 2) by going right 3 (ignores the minus).
  **Why:** treats the negative as positive.
  **Fix:** negative x means go *left* of the origin; negative y means *down*.
- **Wrong:** reflecting across the y-axis, flips the y sign instead of x.
  **Why:** mixes up which coordinate changes.
  **Fix:** the y-axis is vertical; reflecting left-right swaps the *x* sign (y stays the same).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which quadrant is (−4, −1) in? **A:** Bottom-left (both negative).
- **Q:** Plot (−2, 3) — describe the move. **A:** 2 left, 3 up.
- **Q:** Reflect (4, −1) across the x-axis. **A:** (4, 1).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / multipleChoice:** Plot and read four-quadrant coordinates; reflect points across axes; find the coordinates of a missing vertex of a shape.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Coordinates (four quadrants).**
- **Topmarks — coordinate games.**

## 10. Statistics: pie charts and the mean

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Statistics

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 line graphs; fractions/percentages; angles; division.

### Key concepts
- A pie chart shows proportions of a whole as slices; the whole circle is 360° (or 100%).
- Read pie charts using fractions, percentages or angles.
- The mean (average) = total ÷ number of values.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Fractions/percentages of amounts; angles in a circle; division.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **pie chart** divides a circle into slices showing each part's share of the whole. The whole circle = **360°** = 100%. A slice that's ¼ of the chart = 90° = 25% of the data. To find how many a slice represents, take its fraction/percentage of the total. The **mean** is the "evening-out" average: add all the values and divide by how many there are. **Concrete:** share the total equally to picture the mean.

**Worked example.** 20 children surveyed; the "football" slice is half the pie (180°, 50%) → 10 children. **Mean:** scores 4, 6, 8, 6 → total 24 ÷ 4 values = **6** (the mean score).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Pie chart of how a day is spent (sleep/school/play).
- Average goals per match across a season.
- Survey results as slices of a circle.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** reads pie-chart slices as exact counts without using the total.
  **Why:** treats the slice size as the number, not a proportion.
  **Fix:** a slice is a *fraction of the total*; find that fraction of the actual number surveyed.
- **Wrong:** finds the mean by dividing by the wrong count (or forgetting to add first).
  **Why:** muddles the "add then divide by how many" steps.
  **Fix:** mean = (sum of values) ÷ (number of values); count the values carefully.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** A pie chart slice is 25% of 40 people — how many? **A:** 10.
- **Q:** Find the mean of 3, 7, 5. **A:** 5 (15 ÷ 3).
- **Q:** A full pie chart is how many degrees? **A:** 360°.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / multipleChoice:** Read counts from pie-chart slices (using % or angle of 360°); calculate the mean of a data set; compare two sets by their means.

- **Khan Academy — Mean (average); reading pie charts.**
