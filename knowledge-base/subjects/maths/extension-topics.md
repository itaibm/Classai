## 1. Long division: the deep dive (the "bus stop" with remainders and decimals)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Calculation – division

**Prerequisites:** Long multiplication and short division (Y5), Numbers to 10,000,000 and order of operations (Y6).

### Key concepts
- Long division is short division written out, with the subtractions shown so you can divide by a **two-digit** number.
- At each step: "How many times does the divisor go in? Multiply, subtract, bring down the next digit."
- A remainder can be left as `r`, written as a **fraction** (remainder over divisor), or carried into **decimals** by adding `.0`s.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Times tables to 12×12, column subtraction, and place value into decimals.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Long division for a 2-digit divisor uses the same four-step loop every time: **Divide, Multiply, Subtract, Bring down** (D-M-S-B). Write the divisor outside the "bus stop", build the answer on top, and show every subtraction underneath. If it does not divide exactly and you want a decimal, add a decimal point and zeros and keep going.

**Worked example.** `432 ÷ 15`.
- 15 into 4 → 0 (too small), so look at 43. 15 into 43 → **2** (2×15 = 30). Write 2. 43 − 30 = 13.
- Bring down the 2 → 132. 15 into 132 → **8** (8×15 = 120). Write 8. 132 − 120 = 12.
- No more whole digits, remainder 12. So `432 ÷ 15 = 28 r 12`, or `28 12⁄15 = 28 ⅘`.
- As a decimal: bring down `.0` → 120. 15 into 120 = **8** exactly. So `432 ÷ 15 = 28.8`. (Check: 28.8 × 15 = 432. ✓)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sharing 432 football stickers fairly between 15 friends — how many each, how many left over?
- A 432 cm ribbon cut into 15 equal bows.
- £4.32 split 15 ways (links remainder → pence).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** forgets to bring the next digit down and stops early. **Why:** loses the loop. **Fix:** chant "Divide, Multiply, Subtract, Bring down" as a rhythm; tick each digit as it is brought down.
- **Wrong:** writes the remainder as the answer (e.g. says "12"). **Why:** confuses leftover with quotient. **Fix:** label the answer line vs the remainder line explicitly.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** `196 ÷ 14`. **A:** 14 (14×14 = 196 exactly).
- **Q:** `500 ÷ 16` as a decimal. **A:** 31.25 (16×31 = 496, remainder 4 → 40÷16 = 2.5).
- **Q:** `247 ÷ 13`. **A:** 19 (13×19 = 247).

### Activity / practice
numberEntry: a ladder of three divisions — one exact, one with whole remainder, one carried to a decimal — each checked by inverse multiplication.

## 2. Negative numbers in context (temperature, money and number-line jumps)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Number – negative numbers

**Prerequisites:** Negative numbers and Roman numerals (Y4), Numbers and place value to 1,000,000 (Y5).

### Key concepts
- Negative numbers are values **below zero**; the further left on the number line, the **smaller** the number (−8 < −3).
- To find a **difference across zero**, count from one number up to zero, then on to the other, and add the jumps.
- Real contexts: temperature, money owed/overdrawn, floors below ground, sea level.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Reading a number line, counting on, ordering whole numbers.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Draw a horizontal number line with 0 in the middle. Going right adds, going left subtracts. To find the **gap** between a negative and a positive, do it in two hops: up to zero, then up to the target. −5 is *colder/smaller* than −1, even though 5 is bigger than 1 — the minus flips the order.

**Worked example.** The temperature is −6 °C and rises by 9 degrees. New temperature? Start at −6, jump 6 right to reach 0 (uses 6 of the 9), then 3 more right → **+3 °C**. And the difference between −6 °C and 4 °C is 6 + 4 = **10 degrees**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Winter weather: overnight −4 °C, afternoon 7 °C — how much warmer?
- A bank balance of −£20 (overdrawn); paying in £30 leaves +£10.
- A lift: 3 floors up from basement level −2 reaches floor +1.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** says −8 is bigger than −3. **Why:** judges by the digit, ignoring the sign. **Fix:** a number line — "further left = smaller / colder."
- **Wrong:** difference between −6 and 4 is 2. **Why:** subtracts the digits. **Fix:** count the two hops across zero (6 + 4 = 10).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Order: 3, −7, 0, −2, 5. **A:** −7, −2, 0, 3, 5.
- **Q:** −3 °C falls by 5 degrees. **A:** −8 °C.
- **Q:** Difference between −9 and 6. **A:** 15.

### Activity / practice
ordering: drag temperatures (−7, −2, 0, 3, 5) coldest → warmest, then a numberEntry "rise/fall" puzzle across zero.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Negative numbers** (KS2, temperature contexts).
- **Topmarks — Tommy's Trek / number line** for jumps across zero.

## 3. Ratio problem-solving (scaling recipes, sharing in a ratio)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Ratio and proportion

**Prerequisites:** Ratio and proportion (Y6), Multiply fractions; decimals (Y5).

### Key concepts
- A ratio `a : b` compares **parts**; to share a total, find the **value of one part** by dividing by the total number of parts.
- **Scaling**: multiply both sides by the same number keeps the ratio equal (2:3 = 4:6 = 8:12).
- Proportion problems ("if 3 cost 90p, what do 7 cost?") use the **unitary method** — find one, then many.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Division, simplifying fractions, multiplication facts.

### Teach it (the explanation)
For "share £40 in the ratio 3 : 5", add the parts (3 + 5 = 8), divide the total by the parts (40 ÷ 8 = £5 per part), then multiply: 3 parts = £15, 5 parts = £25. **Check they sum to the whole** (15 + 25 = 40 ✓). For proportion, find the value of **one** unit first.

**Worked example.** A smoothie uses bananas : strawberries in the ratio 2 : 3. To make it for a party you use 12 strawberries. How many bananas? Strawberries went ×4 (3 → 12), so bananas go ×4 too: 2 × 4 = **8 bananas**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Scaling a pancake recipe for 2 up to a class of 6.
- Mixing squash 1 : 4 with water.
- Sharing 24 sweets between two children 1 : 3.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** shares £40 as £20 each for ratio 3 : 5. **Why:** treats ratio as "halve it". **Fix:** count the *parts* (8), not the people.
- **Wrong:** adds to scale (2:3 → 2+10 : 3+10). **Why:** confuses ratio with difference. **Fix:** ratio scales by **multiplying** both sides by the same factor.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Share 30 in the ratio 2 : 3. **A:** 12 and 18.
- **Q:** Paint mixed red : white = 1 : 4. For 5 tins of red, how much white? **A:** 20 tins.
- **Q:** If 4 pens cost 80p, how much for 6? **A:** £1.20 (one pen = 20p).

### Activity / practice
numberEntry: "share-in-a-ratio" word problems with a built-in sum check; a recipe-scaling matchPairs.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Ratio and proportion** (KS2).
- **NRICH — Ratio and proportion** problem set.

## 4. Area and perimeter problem-solving (compound shapes & missing sides)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Measurement – area & perimeter

**Prerequisites:** Area and perimeter (Y4), Area, perimeter and volume (Y5).

### Key concepts
- **Perimeter** is the distance *around* (add all sides); **area** is the space *inside* (square units).
- A **compound (L-shaped)** shape can be split into rectangles; find each area and add, or subtract a "missing" rectangle.
- **Missing sides** are found because opposite sides of the whole must balance (the two heights add to the total height).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Area of a rectangle = length × width; adding and subtracting; reading lengths.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Don't memorise a special rule for L-shapes — **split** them. Draw a line to cut the L into two rectangles, find each area, and add. For a missing side, use the fact that the lengths on one side equal the lengths on the opposite side. Keep **units** consistent and write cm² for area, cm for perimeter.

**Worked example.** An L-shape: a 6 cm × 4 cm rectangle with a 2 cm × 2 cm square notch cut from a corner. Split the whole 6×4 (= 24 cm²) and subtract the 2×2 (= 4 cm²): area = **20 cm²**. Missing horizontal side = 6 − 2 = **4 cm**; missing vertical side = 4 − 2 = **2 cm**. Perimeter, going round: 6 + 4 + 4 + 2 + 2 + 2 = **20 cm**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Carpet for an L-shaped room.
- Fencing (perimeter) vs turf (area) for a garden with a patio cut out.
- Tiling a worktop around a sink cut-out.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** confuses area and perimeter. **Why:** both are "measuring the shape". **Fix:** perimeter = walk the *fence*; area = paint the *floor*.
- **Wrong:** adds only the labelled sides of an L-shape and misses the unlabelled ones. **Fix:** balance opposite sides to find the missing lengths first.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Rectangle 8 cm × 3 cm — area and perimeter. **A:** 24 cm², 22 cm.
- **Q:** An L-shape made of a 5×5 square with a 2×2 corner removed — area. **A:** 21 cm².
- **Q:** A square has perimeter 36 cm. What is its area? **A:** side 9 cm → 81 cm².

### Activity / practice
numberEntry: split-and-add area of three compound shapes; a "find the missing side" puzzle on squared paper.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Area** and **Perimeter** (KS2, compound shapes).
- **Oak National Academy — Y5/6 Area of compound shapes**.

## 5. Roman numerals to 1000 (M, D, C and year dates)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–5 (ages 8–10) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Number – numeration

**Prerequisites:** Negative numbers and Roman numerals (Y4 — basics I, V, X, L, C).

### Key concepts
- Seven letters: **I=1, V=5, X=10, L=50, C=100, D=500, M=1000.**
- Add when letters get **smaller or equal** left→right (VI = 6); **subtract** when a smaller letter sits before a larger one (IV = 4, IX = 9, XL = 40, CM = 900).
- Romans had **no zero** and no place value — that's why big numbers are long.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Recognising I, V, X and reading up to about 100 in Roman numerals.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Work left to right, biggest letters first. If a letter is smaller than the one after it, it's a *subtraction pair* (only I, X, C do this: I before V/X, X before L/C, C before D/M). Otherwise add. Clock faces, book chapters and film dates use these.

**Worked example.** Read **MCMXLIV**: M = 1000, CM = 900, XL = 40, IV = 4 → 1000 + 900 + 40 + 4 = **1944**. Going the other way, write 2026: M(1000) + M(1000) + X(10) + X(10) + V(5) + I(1) = **MMXXVI**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Reading the year on a building, statue, or end of a film (e.g. MMVIII).
- Super Bowl / monarch numbers (Henry VIII, Elizabeth II).
- Clock faces that use IIII or IV.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes IIII for 4 or VV for 10. **Why:** treats it as pure tallying. **Fix:** the subtraction rule (IV) and "never repeat V, L, D; never write a letter four times".
- **Wrong:** reads IX as 11. **Why:** adds instead of subtracting. **Fix:** smaller-before-larger = subtract.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Write 49 in Roman numerals. **A:** XLIX (40 + 9).
- **Q:** Read MCDXCII. **A:** 1492 (1000 + 400 + 90 + 2).
- **Q:** Write 2026. **A:** MMXXVI.

### Activity / practice
matchPairs: Roman ↔ Arabic for tricky values (IV, IX, XL, XC, CM); a "decode the year" fillBlank.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Roman numerals** (KS2).
- **Topmarks — Roman numerals** games.

## 6. The mean (average) and using it to solve problems

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Statistics – average

**Prerequisites:** Statistics: pie charts and the mean (Y6), column addition, division.

### Key concepts
- The **mean** = total ÷ number of values; it "shares the total out equally".
- You can work **backwards**: total = mean × number of values, so you can find a missing value.
- The mean can be a decimal that isn't one of the actual data values — that's fine.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Adding several numbers; dividing; the idea of "fair share".

### Teach it (the explanation)
To find the mean, **add everything up, then divide by how many there are**. The picture: imagine pouring all the values into one pile and sharing them out level. The reverse is powerful for problem-solving — if you know the mean and the count, multiply to recover the total, then subtract the known values to find a missing one.

**Worked example.** Scores: 7, 9, 4, 8, 7. Sum = 35; there are 5 scores; mean = 35 ÷ 5 = **7**. *Backwards:* four tests average 6 (total 24). After a fifth test the average is 7 over 5 tests (total 35). So the fifth score = 35 − 24 = **11**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Average goals per match across a season.
- Average daily temperature for a week.
- "What do I need on the last test to average 8?"

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** divides by the wrong count (forgets a value). **Why:** miscounts the data set. **Fix:** tick each value as it's added; the count = number of ticks.
- **Wrong:** thinks the mean must be one of the numbers. **Why:** confuses mean with mode. **Fix:** show a mean of 7.4 — it's the *fair-share* value, not a real score.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Mean of 3, 5, 10. **A:** 6 (18 ÷ 3).
- **Q:** Mean of 4 numbers is 10. What is their total? **A:** 40.
- **Q:** Three numbers have a mean of 5; two of them are 4 and 6. The third? **A:** 5 (total 15 − 10).

### Activity / practice
numberEntry: compute three means, then two "find the missing value" reverse-mean puzzles.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — The mean / averages** (KS2).
- **Oak National Academy — Y6 Calculate the mean**.

## 7. Algebra-readiness: function machines, missing numbers & simple formulae

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Algebra

**Prerequisites:** Algebra: formulae, expressions and equations (Y6), the four operations.

### Key concepts
- A **letter stands for an unknown number** (n, x). An expression like `2n + 3` means "double the number, then add 3".
- A **function machine** applies operations in order; running it **backwards** (inverse operations) finds the input.
- Solving an equation = "undo" the operations in **reverse order** to get the unknown alone.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Inverse operations (× undoes ÷, + undoes −), order of operations.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Think of a machine: a number goes in, operations happen, a number comes out. `IN → (×2) → (+3) → OUT`. If OUT is 11, run backwards: undo +3 (11 − 3 = 8), undo ×2 (8 ÷ 2 = 4), so IN = 4. That *is* solving `2n + 3 = 11`. Always undo in the **reverse** order, last operation first.

**Worked example.** Solve `3n − 4 = 17`. Undo −4 first: 17 + 4 = 21. Then undo ×3: 21 ÷ 3 = **7**. Check: 3 × 7 − 4 = 21 − 4 = 17 ✓.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "I'm thinking of a number…" magic tricks.
- A taxi fare formula: cost = £2 + £1 per mile → `c = 2 + m`.
- Tiles round a pond pattern: nth term.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** for `2n + 3 = 11`, divides 11 by 2 first. **Why:** ignores order, undoes the wrong operation first. **Fix:** undo the **last** operation first (the +3 before the ×2).
- **Wrong:** thinks `2n` means "twenty-something" or "2 then n". **Why:** reads it as place value. **Fix:** `2n` = 2 × n; substitute a number to show it.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** If `n = 5`, find `4n − 2`. **A:** 18.
- **Q:** Solve `n + 8 = 20`. **A:** 12.
- **Q:** Solve `5n = 35`. **A:** 7.

### Activity / practice
fillBlank: run three function machines forwards, then three backwards to find the input; substitute into a simple formula.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Function machines / Introduction to algebra** (KS2).
- **NRICH — Algebraic thinking** starters.

## 8. Mental-maths strategies (the toolbox of shortcuts)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Calculation – mental strategies

**Prerequisites:** Number bonds, times tables, place value.

### Key concepts
- Good mental maths is **choosing a strategy**, not just "counting harder": partitioning, rounding-and-adjusting, near-doubles, and using known facts.
- **Rounding and adjusting**: add/subtract a round number then correct (e.g. + 99 = + 100 − 1).
- **Derived facts**: from one fact you get many (6 × 4 = 24, so 6 × 40 = 240, 60 × 4 = 240).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Bonds to 10/20/100, tables to 12×12, partitioning into tens and ones.

### Teach it (the explanation)
For each calculation, pause and pick a tool: **Partition** (47 + 36 = 40 + 30 + 7 + 6 = 83); **round and adjust** (199 + 48 = 200 + 48 − 1 = 247); **near-double** (7 + 8 = double 7 + 1 = 15); **bridge through 10** (8 + 5 = 8 + 2 + 3 = 13); **use ÷/× links** (640 ÷ 8 = 80 because 8 × 80 = 640). The skill is matching the method to the numbers.

**Worked example.** `6 × 99` mentally: 6 × 100 = 600, then subtract 6 → **594**. And `73 − 38`: round 38 to 40 (subtract 40 → 33), then add back 2 → **35**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Working out change quickly at a shop.
- Adding scores in a game without a calculator.
- Doubling a recipe in your head.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** rounds to adjust but adjusts the wrong way (199 + 48 → 247 + 1). **Why:** loses track of the correction. **Fix:** "I added 1 too many, so take 1 back."
- **Wrong:** tries to do everything by counting in ones. **Why:** no strategy bank. **Fix:** name and practise one strategy at a time.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** `48 + 99`. **A:** 147 (48 + 100 − 1).
- **Q:** `8 × 25`. **A:** 200 (×100 ÷ 4, or 8 × 25 doubling).
- **Q:** `360 ÷ 6`. **A:** 60 (using 6 × 6 = 36).

### Activity / practice
speak: a timed "strategy out loud" round — child says which method and the answer; multipleChoice "best strategy for this sum".

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Mental maths strategies** (KS2).
- **Topmarks — Hit the Button** (rapid bonds/tables/halves).

## 9. Multi-step word problems (RUCSAC and the bar model)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Problem solving – reasoning

**Prerequisites:** All four operations; the bar model.

### Key concepts
- A multi-step problem needs the answer to one step before you can do the next — find the **hidden middle question**.
- **RUCSAC**: Read, Underline (key numbers/words), Choose operation, Solve, Answer (in words/units), Check.
- A **bar model** turns words into a picture so you can see what's known and what's missing.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Confident with +, −, ×, ÷ and the bar model representation.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Slow down at the **read** stage. Underline the numbers and the question. Draw a bar to show the parts and the whole. Decide which operation each step needs, do them in order, then write the answer **with units** and check it's sensible (estimate first). The trap in multi-step problems is stopping after step one.

**Worked example.** "A pack of 6 cakes costs £3. Mia buys 4 packs and pays with a £20 note. How much change?" Step 1: cost = 4 × £3 = £12. Step 2: change = £20 − £12 = **£8**. (The "6 cakes" is a distractor — not needed.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Planning a party budget.
- Working out a journey time with two legs.
- Comparing two shop offers to find the better deal.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** answers the first step and stops (£12). **Why:** misses that the question asked for change. **Fix:** re-read the actual question; "have I answered *that*?"
- **Wrong:** uses every number given. **Why:** assumes all data is needed. **Fix:** distractors exist — only use what the question needs.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 3 boxes hold 8 pencils each; 5 are broken. How many work? **A:** 19 (24 − 5).
- **Q:** A film is 95 min; it starts at 18:40. When does it end? **A:** 20:15.
- **Q:** Buy 3 drinks at £2 and 2 snacks at £1.50; pay £10. Change? **A:** £1 (£9 spent).

### Activity / practice
numberEntry: three two-step word problems, one containing a distractor number; child draws/describes the bar model first.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Solving problems** (KS2 reasoning).
- **NRICH — Word problems / reasoning** collection.

## 10. Reading SATs-style data (timetables, two-way tables & scaled graphs)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Statistics – interpretation

**Prerequisites:** Line graphs and timetables (Y5), bar charts (Y3).

### Key concepts
- A **timetable** is read along a row (one service) and down a column (one stop); the **gap** between two times is a duration.
- A **two-way table** crosses two categories; the right cell is found by matching a row and a column.
- On a **scaled graph**, each square may be worth more than 1 — read the axis scale before reading a value.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Telling the time (24-hour), reading axes, finding differences.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The exam skill is **careful reading**, not heavy calculation. For timetables, put a finger on the row and trace to the right time; the difference between departure and arrival is the journey time (often crossing the hour). For two-way tables, find the cell where the right row meets the right column. For scaled graphs, check "how much is one square?" first — a common slip is assuming each gridline is 1.

**Worked example.** A bus leaves at 09:50 and arrives 10:35. Journey time: 09:50 → 10:00 is 10 min, 10:00 → 10:35 is 35 min, total **45 minutes**. On a graph where each square = 5, a bar reaching the 4th line above 20 shows 20 + 4×5 = **40**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Planning to catch a real bus or train.
- Reading a sports league results table.
- Checking a chart of rainfall or temperature where the scale jumps in 5s.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** subtracts the time digits (10:35 − 09:50 = "85"). **Why:** treats time like base-10. **Fix:** count up through the hour; 60 minutes in an hour.
- **Wrong:** reads each graph square as 1. **Why:** ignores the scale. **Fix:** always read the axis labels first and find the value of one square.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Train leaves 14:25, arrives 15:10. Journey? **A:** 45 minutes.
- **Q:** On a scale of 2 per square, a bar at the 6th line reads…? **A:** 12.
- **Q:** Two-way table: 12 boys + 15 girls chose football out of 50 children — how many did not? **A:** 23.

### Activity / practice
multipleChoice + numberEntry: read three values off a scaled bar chart and compute one journey time from a mini timetable.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Timetables & interpreting graphs** (KS2).
- **Oak National Academy — Y5 Read and interpret line graphs / timetables**.

## 11. Prime numbers, factors and divisibility tests (enrichment)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Number – properties

**Prerequisites:** Multiples, factors, primes, squares and cubes (Y5).

### Key concepts
- A **prime** has exactly **two** factors (1 and itself); 1 is **not** prime (only one factor).
- **Divisibility tests** are quick checks: ÷2 (even), ÷3 (digit sum ÷3), ÷5 (ends 0/5), ÷10 (ends 0), ÷9 (digit sum ÷9).
- Factor pairs come in twos and meet at the **square root** (e.g. 36: 1×36, 2×18, 3×12, 4×9, 6×6).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Times tables, the meaning of factor and multiple.

### Teach it (the explanation)
List factor pairs systematically (1 and the number, then 2, 3, 4…) until they meet in the middle. A prime is a number whose only pair is 1 × itself. Divisibility tests save time: 372 is divisible by 3 because 3 + 7 + 2 = 12 and 12 ÷ 3 works; it's even, so also by 2; therefore by 6.

**Worked example.** Is 51 prime? Test: not even, doesn't end 0/5, digit sum 5 + 1 = 6 (÷3 works) → 51 = 3 × 17. So **51 is not prime**. The primes below 20 are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Arranging 12 chairs into equal rows (factor pairs) — but 13 chairs only makes one row (prime).
- Cicadas with prime-numbered life cycles.
- Lock codes / patterns.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** says 1 is prime, or 2 isn't (because it's even). **Why:** mis-defines "prime". **Fix:** prime = *exactly two* factors; 2 qualifies, 1 doesn't.
- **Wrong:** misses factor pairs by checking randomly. **Why:** unsystematic. **Fix:** check 1, 2, 3… in order until pairs meet.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** List all factors of 24. **A:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24.
- **Q:** Is 29 prime? **A:** Yes (no factors but 1 and 29).
- **Q:** Is 96 divisible by 3? **A:** Yes (9 + 6 = 15, ÷3 works).

### Activity / practice
categorize: sort numbers into prime / composite; numberEntry list-all-factors; a divisibility "quick test" multipleChoice.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Factors, multiples and primes** (KS2).
- **NRICH — Factors and multiples** investigations.

## 12. Equivalence: fractions, decimals and percentages of amounts

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Fractions / decimals / percentages

**Prerequisites:** Percentages (Y5), Fractions, decimals and percentages: equivalence (Y6).

### Key concepts
- ½ = 0.5 = 50%, ¼ = 0.25 = 25%, ¾ = 0.75 = 75%, ⅒ = 0.1 = 10% — the **same value, three costumes**.
- To find a **percentage of an amount**, find 10% (÷10) and 1% (÷100) as building blocks, then combine.
- "Of" means **multiply**: ½ of 30 = ½ × 30 = 15.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Dividing by 10 and 100, simple fractions of amounts.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Keep a "Rosetta stone" of the common conversions in your head. To find a percentage of a number, use **10% (divide by 10)** as the key brick: 30% = three lots of 10%; 5% = half of 10%; 1% = divide by 100. Build any percentage from 10%, 5% and 1%.

**Worked example.** Find 35% of £80. 10% of 80 = £8, so 30% = £24; 5% = £4 (half of 10%); 35% = £24 + £4 = **£28**. Check with fractions: 35% = 0.35, 0.35 × 80 = 28 ✓.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A 25% sale discount on a £40 game.
- 20% tip / VAT.
- A test scored 18/25 — what percentage?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks 0.5 means "5%" or "5 tenths is 5%". **Why:** confuses decimal and percent. **Fix:** 0.5 = 50/100 = 50%; line them up over 100.
- **Wrong:** finds 10% by dividing by 100. **Why:** mixes the two key divisions. **Fix:** 10% = ÷10, 1% = ÷100.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Write ¾ as a percentage. **A:** 75%.
- **Q:** 20% of £50. **A:** £10.
- **Q:** 15% of 60. **A:** 9 (6 + 3).

### Activity / practice
matchPairs: link ½/¼/¾/⅒/⅕ to their decimals and percentages; numberEntry "% of an amount" using the 10%/1% method.
