# Year 5 Mathematics (ages 9–10) — Upper Key Stage 2

Year 5 reaches a million, introduces primes/factors/squares/cubes, long multiplication and short division, fractions added/subtracted/multiplied, decimals to 3 places, percentages, volume, angle measurement and polygons.

---

## 1. Numbers and place value to 1,000,000

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Number – number & place value

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 place value to 10,000.

### Key concepts
- Read, write, order and compare numbers to at least 1,000,000.
- Each place is ten times the place to its right (ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten-thousands, hundred-thousands, millions).
- Round large numbers and use negative numbers in context.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Place value to 10,000; rounding; negatives.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Extend the place-value chart leftwards: ones, tens, hundreds, then **thousands** (and within thousands: thousands, ten-thousands, hundred-thousands), then **millions**. Commas group every three digits (1,000,000). **Compare** from the biggest place. **Round** big numbers using the same rule (digit to the right: 5+ up). **Negatives in context** (temperatures, money owed) extend below zero, with intervals across zero (−3 to 4 is a difference of 7).

**Worked example.** Compare 245,300 and 245,030. Equal down to thousands; then hundreds 3 vs 0 → 3 wins, so **245,300 > 245,030**. Round 245,300 to the nearest 10,000: thousands digit is 5 → round up → **250,000**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- City populations.
- Money raised in a big charity appeal.
- Distances in space (km).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** misreads 250,000 as "twenty-five thousand."
  **Why:** miscounting the place groups.
  **Fix:** group in threes from the right; read each group with its label (thousands).
- **Wrong:** rounds a big number using the wrong digit.
  **Why:** unsure which place decides.
  **Fix:** look at the digit just to the right of the rounding place.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Order: 120,400; 102,400; 120,040. **A:** 102,400; 120,040; 120,400.
- **Q:** Round 367,500 to the nearest 100,000. **A:** 400,000.
- **Q:** Difference in temperature from −4 °C to 5 °C? **A:** 9 °C.

### Activity / practice
**ordering / numberEntry:** Order six-digit numbers; round to powers of 10; "the temperature rose from −5 to 3 — by how much?"

## 2. Multiples, factors, primes, squares and cubes

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 26 · **Subject strand:** Number – multiplication & division

**Prerequisites:** Times tables to 12×12; division.

### Key concepts
- A multiple is in a number's times table; a factor divides into it exactly.
- A prime number has exactly two factors (1 and itself); a composite has more.
- A square number is n × n; a cube number is n × n × n.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- All tables; division facts; commutativity.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Factors** divide a number with no remainder — find them in pairs (12: 1&12, 2&6, 3&4). **Multiples** are the times table (multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12…). **Prime** = exactly two factors, 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13…); 1 is **not** prime (only one factor). **Square numbers** come from a number times itself: 4² = 16, written with a small 2; they make literal squares of dots. **Cube numbers**: n³ = n × n × n (2³ = 8) make cubes of blocks.

**Worked example.** Factors of 18: 1×18, 2×9, 3×6 → {1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18}. Is 18 prime? No — it has more than two factors. 5² = 5 × 5 = **25**; 3³ = 3 × 3 × 3 = **27**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Arranging objects into equal rows (factor pairs).
- Square tiles forming a square (square numbers).
- Stacking cubes into a cube (cube numbers).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** says 1 is prime.
  **Why:** thinks "only divides by 1 and itself" — but for 1 those are the same number.
  **Fix:** a prime has *exactly two different* factors; 1 has only one, so it's not prime.
- **Wrong:** calculates 5² as 5 × 2 = 10.
  **Why:** reads the index as "times 2."
  **Fix:** the small 2 means *squared* = the number times itself: 5 × 5 = 25.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** List the factors of 12. **A:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.
- **Q:** Is 7 prime? **A:** Yes (factors only 1 and 7).
- **Q:** What is 4²? And 2³? **A:** 16; 8.

### Activity / practice
**categorize / numberEntry:** Sort numbers into prime/composite; list factor pairs; compute squares and cubes; find common factors of two numbers.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Factors, multiples and primes; square and cube numbers.**
- **NRICH — primary number / factors-and-multiples investigations.**

## 3. Long multiplication and short division

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Number – multiplication & division

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 column ×/÷; tables to 12.

### Key concepts
- Multiply 3- or 4-digit numbers by a 2-digit number using long multiplication (two rows, then add).
- Divide using short division ("bus stop"), expressing remainders as needed.
- Estimate to check; keep place value with a zero placeholder.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Column multiplication ×1-digit; short division; place value.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Long multiplication** by a 2-digit number: multiply by the **ones**, then by the **tens** (writing a 0 placeholder in the ones column first because you're multiplying by tens), then **add** the two rows. **Short division:** divide left to right, carrying remainders; a final remainder can be left as "r", or taken further into decimals later. **Estimate** to size-check (24 × 16 ≈ 24 × 16 ≈ 25 × 16 = 400, actual 384).

**Worked example.** 24 × 16.
```
   2 4
 × 1 6
 -----
 1 4 4   (24 × 6)
 2 4 0   (24 × 10, note the 0 placeholder)
 -----
 3 8 4
```
So 24 × 16 = **384**. Check: ÷ — 384 ÷ 16 = 24. ✓

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Cost of many items (16 tickets at £24).
- Seats in a theatre (rows × seats).
- Sharing a large total equally.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** in long multiplication, forgets the 0 placeholder when multiplying by the tens.
  **Why:** treats the tens digit as ones.
  **Fix:** multiplying by 10s shifts the whole row one place left — write the 0 first.
- **Wrong:** in short division, drops a remainder instead of carrying it.
  **Why:** divides each digit in isolation.
  **Fix:** carry the remainder to the next digit (it joins to make a bigger number to divide).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 23 × 14 = ? **A:** 322.
- **Q:** 156 ÷ 4 = ? **A:** 39.
- **Q:** Estimate 38 × 21. **A:** About 800 (≈ 40 × 20).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Long multiplication (e.g. 36×24, 142×13) and short division (e.g. 245÷5, 738÷6 with remainder). Estimate first.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Long multiplication; short division.**
- **Oak National Academy — Year 5 written methods units.**

## 4. Add and subtract fractions

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 28 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 equivalent fractions; same-denominator add/subtract.

### Key concepts
- To add/subtract fractions, the denominators must be the same.
- Use equivalent fractions to give a common denominator, then add/subtract numerators.
- Answers can be improper fractions or mixed numbers (5/4 = 1¼).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Equivalent fractions; multiplication; same-denominator addition.

### Teach it (the explanation)
You can only add parts that are the same size. If denominators differ, **make them the same** using equivalence. **Pictorial:** show ½ and ¼ on a bar — re-cut the half into two quarters (½ = 2/4), now both are quarters: 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4. **Abstract:** find a common denominator (often the larger denominator if one is a multiple of the other, else multiply them): 1/3 + 1/4 → twelfths: 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12. Convert improper answers to mixed numbers if asked.

**Worked example.** 2/3 + 1/6. Sixths: 2/3 = 4/6. So 4/6 + 1/6 = **5/6**. And 3/4 + 1/2 = 3/4 + 2/4 = 5/4 = **1¼**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Combining parts of recipes (½ cup + ¼ cup).
- Fractions of an hour (¼ hour + ⅓ hour).
- Two people's shares of a pizza.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** ½ + ¼ = 2/6 (adds tops and bottoms).
  **Why:** adds straight across without a common denominator.
  **Fix:** re-cut to the same size: ½ = 2/4; 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4.
- **Wrong:** finds a common denominator but forgets to convert the numerators.
  **Why:** changes the bottom but not the top.
  **Fix:** whatever you multiply the denominator by, multiply the numerator by too (½ → 2/4, not 1/4).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 1/2 + 1/4 = ? **A:** 3/4.
- **Q:** 5/6 − 1/3 = ? **A:** 1/2 (5/6 − 2/6 = 3/6).
- **Q:** 3/4 + 3/4 = ? **A:** 6/4 = 1½.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Add/subtract fractions where one denominator is a multiple of the other, then with different denominators; convert improper to mixed.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Adding and subtracting fractions.**
- **Khan Academy — Adding fractions with unlike denominators.**

## 5. Multiply fractions; decimals to 3 places

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 26 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions (decimals)

**Prerequisites:** Add/subtract fractions; Year 4 decimals.

### Key concepts
- Multiply a fraction by a whole number by multiplying the numerator.
- Read, write and order decimals to three places (thousandths).
- Decimals, fractions and place value to the right of the point connect (0.125 = 125/1000 = ⅛).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Fractions of amounts; tenths/hundredths; ×.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Multiplying a fraction by a whole number:** ⅖ × 3 means three lots of ⅖ = 6/5 = 1⅕ — multiply the numerator by the whole number, keep the denominator. **Pictorial:** repeated copies of the bar. **Decimals to 3 places:** the third place is **thousandths** (0.001). Compare by lining up the decimal points and reading place by place. Convert: 0.4 = 4/10; 0.04 = 4/100; 0.004 = 4/1000. Order decimals by giving them the same number of places (0.5 = 0.500).

**Worked example.** ⅖ × 4 = (2 × 4)/5 = 8/5 = **1⅗**. Order 0.6, 0.06, 0.65: rewrite as 0.600, 0.060, 0.650 → **0.06, 0.6, 0.65**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Three portions of ⅖ of a pizza.
- Precise measurements (1.250 m, race times).
- Money to the penny (£0.075 in rates).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** ⅖ × 3 = 6/15 (multiplies both top and bottom by 3).
  **Why:** treats the whole number as a fraction 3/3.
  **Fix:** 3 lots of ⅖ adds the numerators: 2+2+2 = 6 fifths = 6/5; denominator stays 5.
- **Wrong:** thinks 0.5 < 0.45 because 45 has more digits.
  **Why:** comparing digit strings, not place value.
  **Fix:** equalise places: 0.50 vs 0.45 → 50 hundredths > 45 hundredths.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** ¾ × 2 = ? **A:** 6/4 = 1½.
- **Q:** Write 0.007 as a fraction. **A:** 7/1000.
- **Q:** Order: 0.3, 0.25, 0.305. **A:** 0.25, 0.3, 0.305.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Multiply fractions by whole numbers; order/compare decimals to 3 places; convert between decimal and fraction.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Multiplying fractions; decimals.**
- **Khan Academy — Decimals to thousandths; multiplying fractions by whole numbers.**

## 6. Percentages

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions (percentages)

**Prerequisites:** Decimals; equivalent fractions; ÷ by 10/100.

### Key concepts
- Percent means "out of 100"; 25% = 25/100.
- Percentages, fractions and decimals are three ways to write the same proportion (50% = ½ = 0.5).
- Find a percentage of an amount using 10% and 1% building blocks.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Hundredths; equivalent fractions; ÷ by 10 and 100.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Percent = per hundred.** 30% means 30 out of every 100. **Pictorial:** a 100-square shaded. **Key equivalences:** 50% = ½ = 0.5; 25% = ¼ = 0.25; 10% = 1/10 = 0.1. **Finding a percentage of an amount:** start with **10%** (÷ by 10) and **1%** (÷ by 100), then build up. 30% of 200 = three lots of 10% (20) = 60. **Convert:** % → fraction over 100 (and simplify); % → decimal (÷ 100).

**Worked example.** Find 30% of 80. 10% of 80 = 8 (80 ÷ 10). 30% = 3 × 8 = **24**. Check: 25% (¼) of 80 = 20, so 30% being a bit more (24) is sensible.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sale discounts (20% off).
- Battery/loading percentages.
- Test scores out of 100.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks 50% always means "50."
  **Why:** treats percent as a fixed number, not a proportion.
  **Fix:** 50% is half of *whatever the whole is* — 50% of 20 is 10, of 200 is 100.
- **Wrong:** 25% of 60 = 60 ÷ 25.
  **Why:** divides by the percent number.
  **Fix:** 25% = ¼, so divide by 4: 60 ÷ 4 = 15. Or use 10% + 10% + 5%.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is 10% of 50? **A:** 5.
- **Q:** Write 25% as a fraction and a decimal. **A:** ¼ and 0.25.
- **Q:** Find 20% of 150. **A:** 30.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / matchPairs:** Match %–fraction–decimal equivalents; find percentages of amounts using 10%/1% building blocks.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Percentages.**
- **Khan Academy — Intro to percents.**

## 7. Area, perimeter and volume

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 26 · **Subject strand:** Measurement

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 area & perimeter; multiplication.

### Key concepts
- Area of a rectangle = length × width; perimeter = total of the sides.
- Composite (irregular) shapes can be split into rectangles to find area.
- Volume is the space inside a solid, measured in cubic units; count or calculate cubes.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Area/perimeter of rectangles; multiplication.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Composite shapes:** split an L-shape into two rectangles, find each area, add them. **Volume:** how much space a 3-D shape fills, in **cubic units** (cm³). **Concrete:** build a cuboid from centimetre cubes and count them — a 3×2×2 cuboid is 3 layers... actually count: 3 × 2 = 6 cubes per layer, 2 layers = 12 cm³. **Abstract:** volume of a cuboid = length × width × height. Keep units straight: length (cm), area (cm²), volume (cm³).

**Worked example.** A cuboid 4 cm × 3 cm × 2 cm. Volume = 4 × 3 × 2 = **24 cm³**. An L-shape made of a 5×2 rectangle and a 2×2 square: 10 + 4 = **14 cm²**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Filling a box with sugar cubes (volume).
- Carpeting an L-shaped room (composite area).
- Water in a fish tank (capacity ≈ volume).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** for volume, multiplies only two dimensions (treats it like area).
  **Why:** forgets the third dimension (height/depth).
  **Fix:** volume needs *three* lengths multiplied (l × w × h); build it from cubes to see the layers.
- **Wrong:** finds the area of a composite shape by multiplying the longest length × tallest height (the bounding box).
  **Why:** ignores the missing corner.
  **Fix:** split into rectangles and add only the parts that exist.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Volume of a 2 cm × 2 cm × 3 cm cuboid? **A:** 12 cm³.
- **Q:** What units measure volume? **A:** Cubic units (cm³).
- **Q:** Area of an L-shape = a 4×3 plus a 2×2 — total? **A:** 16 cm².

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Volume of cuboids (count cubes and l×w×h); area of composite shapes by splitting; perimeter of compound shapes.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Area; volume.**
- **Topmarks — area and volume activities.**

## 8. Angles: measuring and drawing

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Geometry – properties of shapes

**Prerequisites:** Year 3/4 angles (right/acute/obtuse); turns.

### Key concepts
- Angles are measured in degrees (°) with a protractor; a full turn is 360°.
- Angles on a straight line add to 180°; angles around a point add to 360°.
- Acute < 90°, right = 90°, obtuse 90°–180°, reflex > 180°.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Right angles; acute/obtuse; reading scales.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A full turn is **360°**; a half turn (straight line) is **180°**; a quarter turn (right angle) is **90°**. **Measure** with a protractor: line up the centre on the vertex and 0° along one arm, read where the other arm crosses — using the scale that starts at 0 on your arm. **Facts:** angles on a straight line sum to 180° (so a missing angle = 180 − the known one); angles around a point sum to 360°. **Classify:** acute (<90), right (90), obtuse (90–180), reflex (>180).

**Worked example.** Two angles sit on a straight line; one is 130°. The other = 180 − 130 = **50°** (acute). To draw a 60° angle, mark the vertex, draw one arm, set the protractor to 60° and mark, then join.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Angles in ramps and roofs.
- Turning a steering wheel / compass bearings.
- Clock-hand angles (each hour = 30°).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** reads the protractor's *outer* scale when the arm started on the inner 0 (gets 180 − the real angle).
  **Why:** uses the wrong scale.
  **Fix:** follow the scale that reads 0 on the arm you lined up; an acute angle must read under 90.
- **Wrong:** thinks a missing angle on a straight line is found by 360 − known.
  **Why:** confuses straight line (180) with full turn (360).
  **Fix:** a straight line is a half turn = 180°; use 180 − known.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many degrees in a right angle? **A:** 90°.
- **Q:** Angles on a straight line: one is 110°, find the other. **A:** 70°.
- **Q:** Is 200° acute, obtuse or reflex? **A:** Reflex.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / multipleChoice:** Find missing angles on a line / around a point; classify angles; measure given angles to the nearest degree.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Measuring and drawing angles.**
- **Topmarks — protractor / angle estimation games.**

## 9. Polygons, reflection and translation

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Geometry – properties & position

**Prerequisites:** Shape properties; coordinates; symmetry.

### Key concepts
- Regular polygons have all sides and all angles equal; irregular ones don't.
- Reflecting a shape flips it across a mirror line; translating slides it.
- A reflected point is the same distance from the mirror line on the other side.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- 2-D shape names; coordinates; lines of symmetry.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Polygons** are closed shapes with straight sides. **Regular** = all sides equal *and* all angles equal (equilateral triangle, square, regular pentagon/hexagon). **Irregular** = sides/angles not all equal. **Reflection:** flip across a mirror line; each point's image is the same perpendicular distance on the other side, like a mirror — left/right swap. **Translation:** slide with no flip or turn. **Concrete:** fold/mirror; use a coordinate grid to reflect across an axis.

**Worked example.** Reflect the point (2, 3) across the y-axis (the vertical line x = 0). It stays at the same height (y = 3) but flips to the other side: x becomes −2 → **(−2, 3)**. A regular hexagon has 6 equal sides and 6 equal angles.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Reflections in a mirror or still water.
- Regular shapes in tiles and signs (stop sign = regular octagon).
- Repeating, sliding patterns (translation) in wallpaper.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks any shape with equal sides is "regular" (e.g. a rhombus).
  **Why:** checks sides but not angles.
  **Fix:** regular needs equal sides *and* equal angles; a rhombus has equal sides but unequal angles.
- **Wrong:** reflects a point by moving it the wrong distance from the mirror line.
  **Why:** doesn't keep equal distance either side.
  **Fix:** the image is the *same distance* from the mirror line, just the other side.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Is a square a regular polygon? **A:** Yes (equal sides and angles).
- **Q:** Reflect (3, 1) across the x-axis. **A:** (3, −1).
- **Q:** Does reflecting change a shape's size? **A:** No — only its position/orientation.

### Activity / practice
**categorize / numberEntry:** Sort polygons regular/irregular; reflect points/shapes across an axis; translate shapes by given vectors.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Polygons; reflection and translation.**
- **Topmarks — symmetry and transformation games.**

## 10. Statistics: line graphs and timetables

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Statistics

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 line graphs; 24-hour time.

### Key concepts
- Line graphs show change over time and let us read in-between (intermediate) values.
- Timetables present times in rows and columns; read across and down to plan journeys.
- We solve comparison and difference problems from data.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Reading line graphs; 24-hour time; subtraction.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Line graphs:** as well as reading plotted points, you can read **between** them (e.g. estimate the value at a time between two marks) because the data is continuous. **Timetables** (buses, trains): each column is one service, each row a stop; to find a journey time, read the departure and arrival and find the difference (mind crossing the hour: 09:50 to 10:20 is 30 minutes). **Solve problems:** "which bus gets me there before 9?", "how long is the journey?", "how much did it change between 2 and 4 pm?"

**Worked example.** A train leaves at 14:35 and arrives at 15:10. Journey = from 14:35 to 15:00 is 25 min, plus 10 min = **35 minutes**. On a temperature graph rising from 8 °C (08:00) to 20 °C (12:00), the rise is 12 °C over 4 hours.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Planning a real bus/train journey.
- Tracking temperature or a plant over weeks.
- Comparing two routes' times.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** calculates 09:50 to 10:20 as 70 minutes (20 − 50 mishandled).
  **Why:** subtracts minutes without crossing the hour properly.
  **Fix:** count up: 09:50 → 10:00 is 10 min, → 10:20 is 20 more = 30 min.
- **Wrong:** reads only exact plotted points and can't estimate between.
  **Why:** treats the graph as discrete.
  **Fix:** the joined line lets you read intermediate values — trace up from the time.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** A bus leaves 10:15, arrives 10:50 — journey time? **A:** 35 minutes.
- **Q:** On a line graph, value rose 5 → 11 — by how much? **A:** 6.
- **Q:** In a timetable, which column shows the earliest departure? **A:** The one with the smallest time.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / multipleChoice:** Read and compare values on line graphs; work out journey durations from a timetable; "which service arrives before X?"

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Line graphs; timetables.**
- **Oak National Academy — Year 5 statistics units.**
