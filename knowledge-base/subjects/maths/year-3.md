# Year 3 Mathematics (ages 7–8) — Lower Key Stage 2

Year 3 moves into the hundreds: numbers to 1000, formal column addition and subtraction, the 3, 4 and 8 times tables, tenths and equivalent fractions, perimeter, and time to the minute.

---

## 1. Numbers and place value to 1000

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Number – number & place value

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 place value to 100.

### Key concepts
- Three-digit numbers are made of hundreds, tens and ones.
- Each place is ten times the one to its right (10 ones = 1 ten; 10 tens = 1 hundred).
- Numbers to 1000 can be compared, ordered, partitioned and counted in 1s/10s/100s.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Tens and ones to 100; comparing two-digit numbers.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Concrete:** base-10 — a flat square = 100, a rod = 10, a small cube = 1. 342 = 3 hundreds, 4 tens, 2 ones. **Pictorial:** a place-value chart H | T | O. **Abstract:** 342 = 300 + 40 + 2. To compare, check **hundreds first**, then tens, then ones (a longer chain of the same rule as Year 2). Counting in 100s: 100, 200, 300… moves the hundreds digit.

**Worked example.** Compare 512 and 521. Hundreds equal (5 = 5). Tens: 1 vs 2 → 2 wins, so **521 > 512**. Partition 706: 700 + 0 + 6 (note the 0 tens).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Page numbers in a long book.
- Distances in metres on a sports day.
- Scores in a video game to 1000.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes "three hundred and five" as 3005 or 35.
  **Why:** writing parts literally / dropping the zero placeholder.
  **Fix:** build with H/T/O — 3 hundreds, 0 tens, 5 ones; the 0 holds the tens place → 305.
- **Wrong:** says 89 > 100 because "8 and 9 are big digits."
  **Why:** comparing digits not place value (more digits = bigger).
  **Fix:** 100 has a hundreds digit; 89 has none — three-digit beats two-digit.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Partition 478. **A:** 400 + 70 + 8.
- **Q:** Which is bigger, 309 or 390? **A:** 390 (tens decide; 9 > 0).
- **Q:** What is 10 more than 295? **A:** 305.

### Activity / practice
**ordering / numberEntry:** Order three-digit numbers; partition into H/T/O; "10 more / 100 more / 100 less" from a given number.

## 2. Column addition and subtraction (up to 3 digits)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 28 · **Subject strand:** Number – addition & subtraction

**Prerequisites:** Numbers and place value to 1000, Year 2 addition/subtraction within 100.

### Key concepts
- Write numbers in aligned columns (ones under ones, tens under tens) and work right to left.
- Carry a ten/hundred when a column makes 10 or more; exchange when a column is too small to subtract.
- Estimate first and check with the inverse.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Place value to 1000; regrouping within 100.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Stack the numbers with **places lined up**. **Addition:** add the ones; if 10 or more, write the ones digit and **carry** the ten to the tens column; repeat for tens, then hundreds. **Subtraction:** if the top digit is too small, **exchange** one from the next column (a ten becomes 10 ones). **Concrete first** with base-10 to see the exchange, then the written method. **Estimate** by rounding (e.g. 248 + 376 ≈ 250 + 380 = 630) so you can spot a wrong answer.

**Worked example.** 248 + 376.
```
  2 4 8
+ 3 7 6
```
Ones: 8 + 6 = 14 → write 4, carry 1. Tens: 4 + 7 + 1 = 12 → write 2, carry 1. Hundreds: 2 + 3 + 1 = 6. Answer **624**. (Estimate 630 ✓.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Adding two prices over £1.
- Combining distances run by two runners.
- Total points across two game levels.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes the full "14" in the ones column instead of carrying.
  **Why:** doesn't regroup ten ones into the tens column.
  **Fix:** with base-10, exchange 10 ones for 1 ten; only the 4 stays, the 1 ten carries.
- **Wrong:** in subtraction, does the bigger digit minus the smaller in each column (e.g. for 5−8 writes 3).
  **Why:** subtracting smaller-from-larger regardless of which is on top.
  **Fix:** exchange from the next column so the top really is bigger; show with blocks.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 356 + 287 = ? **A:** 643.
- **Q:** 503 − 178 = ? **A:** 325.
- **Q:** Estimate 412 + 295. **A:** About 700 (≈ 400 + 300).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Column sums and differences with carrying/exchanging (e.g. 467+255, 600−243). Ask for an estimate first, then the exact answer, then a check.

## 3. The 3, 4 and 8 times tables

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 26 · **Subject strand:** Number – multiplication & division

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 multiplication/division (2, 5, 10 tables).

### Key concepts
- Each new table can be built from groups, arrays, or doubling.
- Multiplication is commutative; division is the inverse.
- 8 = double 4, and 4 = double 2 — tables connect.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows 2, 5, 10 tables; understands equal groups and arrays.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Build each table from equal groups and arrays. **Doubling helps:** the 4 table is double the 2 table (2×6=12 → 4×6=24); the 8 table is double the 4 table (4×6=24 → 8×6=48). Learn facts both as multiplication and as division: from 4 × 7 = 28 you get 28 ÷ 4 = 7 and 28 ÷ 7 = 4. **Concrete/pictorial:** arrays; **abstract:** rapid recall. Practise little and often.

**Worked example.** 8 × 6: start from 4 × 6 = 24, double it → **48**. Division fact: 48 ÷ 8 = 6; 48 ÷ 6 = 8.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Legs on spiders (8), wheels on lorries, sides of dice.
- Packs of 4 (juice cartons), packs of 8.
- Sharing into equal teams.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** learns 3 × 4 but can't answer 4 × 3 or 12 ÷ 3.
  **Why:** memorising one direction without commutativity or the inverse.
  **Fix:** show the fact family from one array: 3×4, 4×3, 12÷3, 12÷4.
- **Wrong:** counts up the whole table every time to reach a fact.
  **Why:** no quick recall yet.
  **Fix:** drill key facts; use doubling shortcuts (4s from 2s, 8s from 4s).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 4 × 7 = ? **A:** 28.
- **Q:** 8 × 5 = ? **A:** 40.
- **Q:** 24 ÷ 3 = ? **A:** 8.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Rapid recall of 3, 4, 8 facts and their division partners; "write the four facts in this family from 8 and 6."

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Times tables (3, 4, 8).**
- **Topmarks — Hit the Button (3, 4, 8 tables and division).**

## 4. Multiplication and division methods (2-digit × 1-digit)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 26 · **Subject strand:** Number – multiplication & division

**Prerequisites:** The 3, 4 and 8 times tables, Place value to 1000.

### Key concepts
- Split a two-digit number into tens and ones, multiply each, then add (partitioning / distributive law).
- Division can be done by sharing or by repeated subtraction / grouping.
- Estimate to check the size of the answer.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Tables to 8; partitioning; column addition.

### Teach it (the explanation)
To multiply 23 × 4, **partition**: 23 = 20 + 3, so 23 × 4 = (20 × 4) + (3 × 4) = 80 + 12 = 92. This is the **distributive law** and the basis of the grid/column method. **Pictorial:** a grid with 20 and 3 across the top, 4 down the side, fill each cell, add. **Division:** 96 ÷ 4 — share into 4, or partition: 80 ÷ 4 = 20, 16 ÷ 4 = 4 → 24. Always estimate (23 × 4 ≈ 20 × 4 = 80, so ~90s).

**Worked example.** 36 × 5. Partition: (30 × 5) + (6 × 5) = 150 + 30 = **180**. Check by division: 180 ÷ 5 = 36. ✓

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- 24 sweets in each of 5 bags — how many?
- Cost of 4 toys at 23p each.
- Sharing 96 cards equally among 4 players.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** for 23 × 4, does 2 × 4 and 3 × 4 = 8 and 12 → "812."
  **Why:** treats the 2 as 2, not 20; doesn't keep place value.
  **Fix:** the 2 is 20, so 20 × 4 = 80; 80 + 12 = 92. Use the grid to keep places.
- **Wrong:** 96 ÷ 4 → splits as 9 ÷ 4 and 6 ÷ 4.
  **Why:** dividing digits, not values.
  **Fix:** partition into tens and ones that divide cleanly (80 and 16), divide each, add.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 24 × 3 = ? **A:** 72.
- **Q:** 84 ÷ 4 = ? **A:** 21.
- **Q:** Estimate 48 × 5. **A:** About 250 (≈ 50 × 5).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** 2-digit × 1-digit by partitioning/grid (e.g. 27×3, 45×6); division by partitioning (e.g. 72÷3, 96÷8). Estimate first.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Multiplying and dividing (grid method).**
- **Oak National Academy — Year 3 multiplication and division units.**

## 5. Fractions: tenths and equivalence

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 26 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 fractions of quantities, Division facts.

### Key concepts
- A tenth is one of ten equal parts; dividing by 10 gives tenths.
- Equivalent fractions name the same amount with different numbers (½ = 2/4 = 5/10).
- The numerator counts parts; the denominator names the size of each part.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Unit and non-unit fractions of amounts; division.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Tenths:** split a whole into 10 equal parts; each is 1/10. Split a number bar of 40 into ten parts → each part is 4, so 1/10 of 40 = 4 (i.e. 40 ÷ 10). **Equivalence:** the same point on a number line can be called different fractions. **Concrete:** a fraction wall — 1 whole, then halves, then quarters, then tenths stacked up; lay them side by side and see ½ lines up with 2/4 and 5/10. **Abstract:** multiply or divide top and bottom by the same number to find equivalents (½ = (1×2)/(2×2) = 2/4).

**Worked example.** Find 1/10 of 60: 60 ÷ 10 = **6**. Is 2/4 equivalent to ½? On the fraction wall they line up; and 2/4 = (2÷2)/(4÷2) = ½. ✓

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sharing a strip of 10 sweets — each is a tenth.
- ½ a pizza is the same as 4/8 of it.
- 1/10 of a metre = 10 cm.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** says 2/4 is more than ½ because the numbers are bigger.
  **Why:** comparing numerators/denominators directly, not the value.
  **Fix:** fraction wall — 2/4 lines up exactly with ½; same amount, different name.
- **Wrong:** finds 1/10 of 50 by subtracting 10 → 40.
  **Why:** confuses "a tenth of" with "take away ten."
  **Fix:** "tenth = share into 10 equal parts" = 50 ÷ 10 = 5.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is 1/10 of 80? **A:** 8.
- **Q:** Write a fraction equivalent to ½. **A:** e.g. 2/4, 5/10.
- **Q:** Which is bigger, 3/10 or 1/10? **A:** 3/10.

### Activity / practice
**matchPairs / numberEntry:** Match equivalent fractions (½↔5/10, ¼↔... etc.); find tenths of multiples of 10; place fractions on a 0–1 line.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Equivalent fractions & tenths.**
- **Topmarks — fraction wall / equivalence games.**

## 6. Adding and subtracting fractions (same denominator)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions

**Prerequisites:** Fractions: tenths and equivalence.

### Key concepts
- When the denominators are the same, add or subtract the numerators only.
- The denominator names the size of the parts and doesn't change.
- The parts must be the same size to combine them.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Understands numerator/denominator; fractions as equal parts.

### Teach it (the explanation)
If parts are the same size you can just count them. **Concrete:** 2 fifths of a chocolate bar + 1 fifth = 3 fifths — you added the *number of fifths*, the "fifth" stayed the same. **Pictorial:** a bar split into fifths, shade 2 then 1 more. **Abstract:** 2/5 + 1/5 = 3/5; 4/7 − 2/7 = 2/7. **Do not** add the denominators — 2/5 + 1/5 is *not* 3/10 (that would change the part size).

**Worked example.** 3/8 + 4/8. Same denominator (eighths): add numerators 3 + 4 = 7, keep eighths → **7/8**. And 7/8 − 5/8 = 2/8.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Eating slices of the same-sized pizza.
- Filling a bottle in fifths.
- Sharing parts of a chocolate bar.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** 2/5 + 1/5 = 3/10 (adds tops and bottoms).
  **Why:** treats the fraction like two separate whole numbers.
  **Fix:** the bar is still in fifths; you only added how many fifths. Denominator unchanged.
- **Wrong:** tries to add 1/2 + 1/4 directly as 2/6.
  **Why:** adds across when denominators differ.
  **Fix:** (Year 3 keeps denominators the same.) Note parts must match in size; show that halves and quarters are different sizes.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 2/6 + 3/6 = ? **A:** 5/6.
- **Q:** 5/9 − 2/9 = ? **A:** 3/9.
- **Q:** 1/4 + 2/4 + 1/4 = ? **A:** 4/4 = 1 whole.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / fillBlank:** Same-denominator addition/subtraction shown on bar models; "how many more eighths make a whole?"

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Adding and subtracting fractions (same denominator).**
- **Oak National Academy — Year 3 fractions units.**

## 7. Measurement: length, perimeter and mass/capacity

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Measurement

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 measurement, Column addition.

### Key concepts
- Length is measured in mm, cm and m; 10 mm = 1 cm, 100 cm = 1 m.
- Perimeter is the total distance around the outside of a 2-D shape — add up all the sides.
- Mass (g/kg) and capacity (ml/l) use the same place-value logic: 1000 g = 1 kg, 1000 ml = 1 l.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Reads scales; can add multi-digit numbers.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Units:** smaller units make bigger ones — 10 mm in a cm, 100 cm in a metre, 1000 g in a kilogram, 1000 ml in a litre. Convert by ×/÷ 10, 100 or 1000. **Perimeter:** measure (or read) every side and add them. **Concrete:** measure objects with a ruler; walk around a shape adding sides. **Pictorial:** a rectangle labelled 5 cm and 3 cm — perimeter = 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16 cm. **Abstract:** for a rectangle, perimeter = 2 × (length + width).

**Worked example.** A rectangle is 6 cm long and 4 cm wide. Perimeter = 6 + 4 + 6 + 4 = **20 cm** (or 2 × (6 + 4) = 20 cm).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Measuring around a picture frame to buy edging.
- How far you walk around the playground.
- Converting your height between cm and m.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** finds perimeter of a rectangle by adding only two sides (length + width).
  **Why:** forgets the opposite sides.
  **Fix:** a rectangle has *four* sides; opposite sides are equal — count all four.
- **Wrong:** says 1 m = 10 cm (or 1 cm = 10 m).
  **Why:** confuses the conversion factor / direction.
  **Fix:** lay a metre stick — 100 cm fit in a metre; 10 mm fit in a cm.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many cm in 1 metre? **A:** 100.
- **Q:** Perimeter of a square with 5 cm sides? **A:** 20 cm.
- **Q:** How many grams in 2 kg? **A:** 2000 g.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Find perimeters of rectangles and other polygons from labelled sides; convert mm↔cm, cm↔m, g↔kg, ml↔l.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Length and perimeter; mass and capacity.**
- **Topmarks — Measuring activities.**

## 8. Telling the time to the minute (12 & 24-hour)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Measurement – time

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 time to 5 minutes.

### Key concepts
- Read analogue time to the nearest minute; each small mark is one minute.
- Know seconds, minutes and hours and how they relate (60 sec = 1 min, 60 min = 1 hour).
- Tell time on 12-hour and 24-hour clocks (e.g. 3 pm = 15:00).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Reads time to 5 minutes; counts in 5s.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Between the big numbers are **small minute marks** — count in 5s to the nearest number, then add the extra minutes. **Concrete:** a clock with minute marks. **24-hour:** after 12 noon, keep counting the hours: 1 pm = 13:00, 5 pm = 17:00 (add 12 to the afternoon hour). Morning hours are written 00:00–11:59. **Units:** 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour, 24 hours in a day.

**Worked example.** Minute hand 2 marks past the 7. Count in 5s to the 7: 5,10,…,35, then +2 = **37 minutes past**. If it's the afternoon and the hour is 4, the 24-hour time is 4 + 12 = **16:37**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Reading a digital bus timetable (24-hour).
- Timing a race in minutes and seconds.
- TV schedule times (19:30 = 7:30 pm).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes 3 pm as 13:00.
  **Why:** adds 12 to the wrong reference (12-hour pm hours start at 12:00 = noon, 1 pm = 13:00).
  **Fix:** 1 pm = 13:00, so 3 pm = 15:00 (add 12 to the pm hour: 3 + 12).
- **Wrong:** reads minute hand 1 mark past 6 as "31 past."
  **Why:** counts the marks but mislabels the base.
  **Fix:** count 5s to the number (30), then add the extra minutes (1) = 31 — confirm the base is 30 at the 6.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many minutes in an hour? **A:** 60.
- **Q:** Write 5 pm in 24-hour time. **A:** 17:00.
- **Q:** Minute hand 3 marks past the 4 — how many minutes past? **A:** 23.

### Activity / practice
**multipleChoice / numberEntry:** Read analogue clocks to the minute; convert between 12- and 24-hour times; "how many minutes between 2:15 and 2:50?"

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Telling the time (to the minute / 24-hour).**
- **Topmarks — Clockworks / Telling the Time.**

## 9. Angles, lines and 2-D/3-D shape properties

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Geometry – properties of shapes

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 shape properties.

### Key concepts
- An angle measures a turn; a right angle is a quarter turn (90°).
- Angles can be compared to a right angle: acute (smaller) or obtuse (larger).
- Lines can be horizontal, vertical, parallel (never meet) or perpendicular (meet at a right angle).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Quarter/half turns; names of 2-D and 3-D shapes.

### Teach it (the explanation)
An **angle** is the amount of turn between two lines meeting at a point. A **right angle** is a square corner — a quarter turn. **Concrete:** make a right-angle checker by folding paper twice; hold it against corners. **Acute** angles are smaller than a right angle; **obtuse** are bigger (but less than a straight line). **Lines:** **horizontal** = flat like the horizon, **vertical** = straight up, **parallel** = always the same distance apart (like railway tracks), **perpendicular** = cross at a right angle. A square has 4 right angles; a rectangle too.

**Worked example.** Is the corner of a book a right angle? Hold the folded right-angle checker against it — it fits exactly → **yes, a right angle (90°)**. The hands of a clock at 1 o'clock make an angle smaller than a right angle → **acute**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Right angles in door frames, books, screens.
- Parallel lines in railway tracks, ladder rungs.
- Clock hands forming different angles through the day.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks a bigger drawing of an angle (longer lines) is a bigger angle.
  **Why:** judging angle by line length, not the amount of turn.
  **Fix:** the angle is the *opening* between the lines; lengthen the lines — the turn is unchanged.
- **Wrong:** calls any two straight lines "parallel."
  **Why:** doesn't check they stay the same distance apart.
  **Fix:** parallel lines never meet and never get closer; perpendicular ones cross at a right angle.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** A right angle is what fraction of a full turn? **A:** A quarter (90°).
- **Q:** Is an angle smaller than a right angle acute or obtuse? **A:** Acute.
- **Q:** What do we call lines that always stay the same distance apart? **A:** Parallel.

### Activity / practice
**categorize / multipleChoice:** Sort angles into "right / acute / obtuse"; identify parallel and perpendicular lines in pictures; count right angles in shapes.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Angles; lines (parallel & perpendicular).**
- **Topmarks — angle and shape games.**

## 10. Statistics: bar charts and pictograms (interpreting data)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Statistics

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 statistics, Addition/subtraction.

### Key concepts
- Bar charts use a numbered scale; read the height of each bar against it.
- Pictograms may use a key where one symbol = several items.
- We use charts to compare, total, and find differences.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Reads simple pictograms and bar charts; can add and subtract.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **bar chart** has a vertical scale (axis) marked in equal steps (e.g. 2s or 5s). To read a bar, look across from its top to the scale. **Pictograms** may have a key like "⚽ = 4." **Reading questions:** "most/least" = tallest/shortest bar; "how many altogether" = add the bars; "how many more X than Y" = subtract. **Concrete:** collect real data, then build and read the chart. Always check the **scale interval** — bars may go up in 2s or 5s, not 1s.

**Worked example.** A bar chart scale goes up in 2s. The "cats" bar reaches the line between 6 and 8 → reads 7? No — bars land on a value; if it reaches the 8 line it's 8. If the dogs bar is at 10 and cats at 6, dogs − cats = **4 more dogs**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Chart of favourite sports in the class/family.
- Goals scored by players over a season.
- Number of each animal seen on a walk.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** reads every gridline as 1 when the scale goes up in 2s.
  **Why:** ignores the scale interval.
  **Fix:** read the axis numbers first; each line is worth 2 (or 5), so count accordingly.
- **Wrong:** answers "how many more" by reading only the bigger bar.
  **Why:** forgets it's a difference (subtraction).
  **Fix:** "more than" = subtract the smaller from the larger.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** A bar reaches the "8" line — how many does it show? **A:** 8.
- **Q:** Pictogram key ⚽ = 5; a row has 3 — how many? **A:** 15.
- **Q:** Dogs = 12, cats = 9 — how many more dogs? **A:** 3.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / multipleChoice:** Read values off bar charts with 2s/5s scales; answer most/least/total/difference questions; complete a pictogram with a key.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Bar charts and pictograms.**
- **Topmarks — data handling / chart activities.**
