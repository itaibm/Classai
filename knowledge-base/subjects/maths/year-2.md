# Year 2 Mathematics (ages 6–7) — Key Stage 1

Year 2 extends to 100, introduces the 2, 5 and 10 times tables, fractions of quantities, money in pounds and pence, and the first statistics (pictograms and tally charts). Keep CPA going.

---

## 1. Numbers and place value to 100

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Number – number & place value

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 place value (tens and ones).

### Key concepts
- Any number to 100 is made of tens and ones; the digit's position gives its value.
- You can partition a number (43 = 40 + 3) and recombine it.
- Numbers can be compared and ordered using place value.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows tens and ones; can count to 100.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Concrete:** base-10 blocks — 6 ten-rods and 5 ones make 65. **Pictorial:** place-value chart with a Tens column and a Ones column. **Abstract:** 65 = 60 + 5, "6 tens and 5 ones." To compare, look at the **tens first**: 65 vs 58 — 6 tens beats 5 tens, so 65 is bigger. Only if the tens are equal do you compare the ones (47 vs 43 → compare 7 and 3).

**Worked example.** Compare 72 and 27. Tens: 7 tens vs 2 tens. 7 > 2, so **72 > 27**. (Same digits, different positions.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- House numbers along a street, ordering them.
- Pages in a book; which page comes first.
- Scores in a game to 100.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** says 19 is bigger than 21 because "9 is bigger than 1."
  **Why:** comparing the ones digit, ignoring the tens.
  **Fix:** compare tens first with rods — 2 tens beats 1 ten.
- **Wrong:** partitions 50 as "5 and 0" rather than 50 and 0.
  **Why:** reading digits, not place value.
  **Fix:** build with rods — the 5 is 5 *tens* = 50.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which is bigger, 38 or 41? **A:** 41 (4 tens beats 3 tens).
- **Q:** Partition 76. **A:** 70 + 6.
- **Q:** Put in order, smallest first: 52, 25, 50. **A:** 25, 50, 52.

### Activity / practice
**ordering / numberEntry:** Order sets of two-digit numbers; partition numbers into tens + ones; "which is greater?" comparisons.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Place value to 100.**
- **Topmarks — Place Value Basketball / Shark Numbers.**

## 2. Counting in 2s, 3s, 5s and 10s

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 18 · **Subject strand:** Number – multiplication & division

**Prerequisites:** Numbers and place value to 100.

### Key concepts
- Skip-counting means counting in equal steps.
- Counting in equal steps is the start of multiplication.
- Patterns appear: counting in 5s ends in 0 or 5; counting in 10s ends in 0; counting in 2s gives even numbers.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can count to 100 and knows tens.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Skip-counting jumps along the number line in equal-sized steps. **Concrete:** count cubes in groups of 2; count 5p coins. **Pictorial:** jumps of 2 (or 5, or 10) on a number line. **Abstract:** 2, 4, 6, 8…; 5, 10, 15, 20…; 10, 20, 30…; 3, 6, 9, 12… Notice the patterns — they help you check and predict. Counting in equal groups is exactly what "lots of" (multiplication) means.

**Worked example.** Count five 5p coins: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 → **25p**. That's "five 5s," which is 5 × 5 = 25.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Counting socks or shoes in 2s.
- Counting fingers across friends in 5s and 10s.
- 5p and 10p coins.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** counts in unequal jumps (5, 10, 14, 20).
  **Why:** loses the constant step size.
  **Fix:** physically make equal groups; say the step ("plus 5") each jump.
- **Wrong:** thinks counting in 5s can end in any digit.
  **Why:** hasn't noticed the 0/5 pattern.
  **Fix:** list 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and circle the last digits — only 0 and 5 appear.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Count in 3s to 15. **A:** 3, 6, 9, 12, 15.
- **Q:** What comes next: 10, 20, 30, ___? **A:** 40.
- **Q:** Counting in 5s, could you say 23? **A:** No — 5s end in 0 or 5.

### Activity / practice
**ordering / fillBlank:** Fill the gaps in skip-counting sequences (in 2s, 3s, 5s, 10s). Spot the odd one out that breaks the pattern.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Counting in steps.**
- **Numberblocks — "step squads" / counting-in-groups episodes.**
- **Topmarks — Hit the Button (times-table skip counting).**

## 3. Addition and subtraction within 100

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 28 · **Subject strand:** Number – addition & subtraction

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 addition/subtraction within 20, Place value to 100.

### Key concepts
- Add and subtract tens and ones separately, then recombine.
- Sometimes ten ones must be regrouped into a ten ("carrying"); sometimes a ten is broken into ten ones ("borrowing/exchanging").
- Addition and subtraction are inverses — use one to check the other.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Bonds within 20; partitioning into tens and ones.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Concrete:** base-10 blocks. To add 36 + 27: add ones (6 + 7 = 13 → that's 1 ten and 3 ones, so *exchange* ten ones for a ten), then add tens (30 + 20 + the new 10 = 60). Total 63. **Pictorial:** part the numbers into tens and ones. **Abstract:** 36 + 27 = (30+20) + (6+7) = 50 + 13 = 63. For subtraction with not enough ones (e.g. 52 − 27), *exchange* one ten for ten ones, then subtract.

**Worked example.** 45 + 38. Tens: 40 + 30 = 70. Ones: 5 + 8 = 13. Combine: 70 + 13 = **83**. Check: 83 − 38 = 45. ✓

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Adding up two prices in a shop.
- Combining two collections of cards.
- Points across two rounds of a game.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** 45 + 38 = 713 (writes 7 tens and 13 ones without regrouping).
  **Why:** doesn't exchange ten ones for a ten.
  **Fix:** with blocks, swap 10 ones for 1 ten; 70 + 13 = 83, not "713."
- **Wrong:** in 52 − 27, does 7 − 2 in the ones because "you can't do 2 − 7."
  **Why:** subtracting the smaller from the larger column-wise regardless of order.
  **Fix:** exchange a ten: 52 becomes 40 and 12; now 12 − 7 = 5, 40 − 20 = 20 → 25.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 27 + 46 = ? **A:** 73.
- **Q:** 63 − 28 = ? **A:** 35.
- **Q:** If 38 + 25 = 63, what is 63 − 25? **A:** 38.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Two-digit sums and differences, some needing regrouping (e.g. 48+37, 71−26). Ask the child to check each with the inverse.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Adding and subtracting (two-digit).**
- **Oak National Academy — KS1 addition and subtraction units.**

## 4. Multiplication and division: 2, 5 and 10 tables

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 28 · **Subject strand:** Number – multiplication & division

**Prerequisites:** Counting in 2s, 5s, 10s.

### Key concepts
- Multiplication = equal groups; "3 × 5" means 3 groups of 5.
- Multiplication can be done in either order: 3 × 5 = 5 × 3 (commutative).
- Division = sharing equally or grouping; it's the inverse of multiplication.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can skip-count in 2s, 5s and 10s.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Concrete:** make equal groups — 4 plates with 5 grapes each. **Pictorial:** an **array** of dots (rows × columns). 3 rows of 5 dots = 15 dots; turn it 90° and it's 5 rows of 3 = still 15, showing 3 × 5 = 5 × 3. **Abstract:** 3 × 5 = 15. **Division** undoes it: 15 grapes shared onto 3 plates = 5 each (15 ÷ 3 = 5); or "how many 5s in 15?" = 3 (15 ÷ 5 = 3). Use the 2, 5 and 10 tables — count in steps to find any fact.

**Worked example.** 4 × 5: four groups of 5 → 5, 10, 15, 20 = **20**. Division: 20 ÷ 5 = how many 5s in 20 → 4. And 20 ÷ 4 = 5.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Wheels on cars (4 each), pairs of socks (2 each), fingers (5/10 each).
- Sharing sweets equally among friends.
- 5p coins — value of several of them.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks 2 × 5 and 5 × 2 are different problems with different answers.
  **Why:** doesn't see multiplication as commutative.
  **Fix:** rotate the array — same dots, so same total; learn one fact, get its partner.
- **Wrong:** for "share 12 between 3," counts out 12 then guesses, ignoring "equally."
  **Why:** doesn't connect division to equal sharing.
  **Fix:** deal one-to-each like cards until gone — 4 each; that *is* 12 ÷ 3.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 3 × 5 = ? **A:** 15.
- **Q:** Share 20 equally between 5 people — how many each? **A:** 4.
- **Q:** If 2 × 10 = 20, what is 10 × 2? **A:** 20.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / matchPairs:** Times-table facts for 2, 5, 10; match each multiplication to its array; quick division facts ("how many 5s in 30?").

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Multiplication and division.**
- **Topmarks — Hit the Button (2, 5, 10 times tables & division).**
- **Numberblocks — multiplication / array episodes.**

## 5. Fractions: ⅓, ¼, ½, ¾ of shapes and quantities

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 halves and quarters, Multiplication and division basics.

### Key concepts
- The bottom number (denominator) tells how many equal parts the whole is split into; the top (numerator) tells how many you take.
- ¾ means three of four equal parts.
- Finding a fraction of a quantity is sharing: ¼ of 12 means 12 ÷ 4.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows halves and quarters; can divide small numbers.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Concrete:** fold a strip into 4 equal parts; shade 3 → ¾. Share 12 counters into 4 equal piles → each pile is ¼ (3 counters); three piles are ¾ (9). **Pictorial:** bar split into equal parts, some shaded. **Abstract:** to find ¼ of 12, divide by the denominator: 12 ÷ 4 = 3, so ¼ of 12 = 3, and ¾ of 12 = 3 × 3 = 9. For ⅓, split into 3 equal parts. Two halves, three thirds, or four quarters all rebuild the whole.

**Worked example.** ⅓ of 9. Split into 3 equal piles: 9 ÷ 3 = 3 each, so **⅓ of 9 = 3**. ¾ of 8: 8 ÷ 4 = 2, then × 3 = **6**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sharing a chocolate bar with equal chunks.
- ¾ of an hour; quarter past / quarter to (links to time).
- Splitting a packet of sweets into thirds among 3 friends.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks ⅓ is bigger than ½ because 3 > 2.
  **Why:** bigger denominator read as bigger fraction.
  **Fix:** same bar split into 2 vs 3 — thirds are smaller; more parts means smaller parts.
- **Wrong:** finds ¼ of 12 by doing 12 − 4 or 12 × 4.
  **Why:** doesn't link the denominator to "share into that many parts."
  **Fix:** physically deal 12 into 4 equal piles → 3; the denominator says how many piles.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is ¼ of 8? **A:** 2.
- **Q:** What is ¾ of 12? **A:** 9.
- **Q:** Which is bigger, ½ or ¼? **A:** ½.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / multipleChoice:** Find unit and non-unit fractions of quantities (½ of 10, ¼ of 16, ¾ of 8); pick the shape shaded ¾.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Fractions of amounts.**
- **Oak National Academy — KS1 fractions units.**

## 6. Money: pounds and pence

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Measurement – money

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 recognising coins, Addition/subtraction within 100.

### Key concepts
- £1 = 100p; amounts can be written in pounds and pence (£1.50).
- You can add amounts of money and work out change.
- Change = what's left when you subtract the cost from the money given.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Recognises coins and notes; can add/subtract within 100.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Concrete:** real/plastic coins and notes. £1 = 100p — count 100 pennies, or two 50ps, into a pound. Make amounts the most efficient way (use bigger coins first). **Change:** if a toy costs 65p and you pay £1 (100p), change = 100 − 65 = 35p. Count *up* from the price to the money paid: 65 → 70 (5p) → 100 (30p) = 35p. **Abstract:** add prices; subtract to find change.

**Worked example.** A drink is 45p; you pay 50p. Change = 50 − 45 = **5p**. Two snacks at 30p and 25p: 30 + 25 = **55p** total.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A pretend shop: price items, pay, give change.
- Pocket money — saving up for something and counting progress.
- Which coins make exactly £1?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes £1.5 to mean one pound fifty (or reads £1.05 as one pound fifty).
  **Why:** pence column needs two digits; place value of the decimal not understood.
  **Fix:** £1.50 = 1 pound and 50 pence (5 tens of pennies); £1.05 = 1 pound and 5 pence. Two digits after the dot.
- **Wrong:** finds change by subtracting the wrong way (money − money − money).
  **Why:** unclear what "change" is.
  **Fix:** "money given − cost = change"; count up from cost to money paid.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many pence in £1? **A:** 100p.
- **Q:** A pen costs 70p; you pay £1. What's your change? **A:** 30p.
- **Q:** What is 40p + 35p? **A:** 75p.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Total small shopping lists; work out change from 50p, £1, £2. "Make 80p using the fewest coins."

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Money (pounds and pence, change).**
- **Topmarks — Toy Shop Money / Change game.**

## 7. Measurement: length, mass, capacity and temperature

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Measurement

**Prerequisites:** Numbers and place value to 100.

### Key concepts
- We measure length (cm/m), mass (g/kg), capacity (ml/litres) and temperature (°C) using standard units.
- A measuring tool has a scale; read the number the marker lines up with.
- You can compare and order measurements.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can read numbers to 100 and count in steps along a scale.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Standard units let everyone agree how big something is. **Concrete:** measure a pencil with a ruler (cm), weigh fruit on scales (g/kg), pour water into a marked jug (ml). **Pictorial:** read scales — find where the marker sits, count the intervals. **Abstract:** compare and order (a 30 cm ruler is longer than a 20 cm one; 1 kg is heavier than 500 g). Always line the start of the object up with **0** on the ruler.

**Worked example.** A jug scale marks every 100 ml. The water reaches two marks above 0 → 200 ml. A pencil from 0 to the 12 cm mark is **12 cm long**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Measuring how tall the child is, in cm.
- Weighing ingredients to bake.
- Filling cups/bottles and comparing how much they hold.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** measures from the end of the ruler (or the 1) instead of 0.
  **Why:** doesn't align the object with the zero mark.
  **Fix:** always start at 0; show how starting at 1 makes the answer one short.
- **Wrong:** thinks the bigger/taller container always holds more.
  **Why:** judging capacity by height alone, not volume.
  **Fix:** pour from a tall thin glass into a short wide one — same water, different shape.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which is longer, 25 cm or 30 cm? **A:** 30 cm.
- **Q:** Which is heavier, 1 kg or 500 g? **A:** 1 kg.
- **Q:** A ruler reads from 0 to 15 — how long is the object? **A:** 15 cm.

### Activity / practice
**multipleChoice / numberEntry:** Read scales (rulers, jugs, scales); order three measurements; choose the right unit for measuring a chosen object.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Measurement (length, mass, capacity).**
- **Topmarks — Measuring (scales) activities.**

## 8. Telling the time to 5 minutes

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Measurement – time

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 o'clock and half past, Counting in 5s.

### Key concepts
- The long (minute) hand counts minutes; each number on the clock is 5 minutes apart.
- "Past" and "to": before half past we say "minutes past"; after, "minutes to" the next hour.
- Quarter past = 15 minutes past; quarter to = 15 minutes before the hour.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Reads o'clock and half past; counts in 5s.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Because there are 5 minutes between each clock number, you count the minute hand in 5s. **Concrete:** a teaching clock — move the minute hand number by number, counting 5, 10, 15… **Past/to:** from 12 round to 6 (the right side) we say "past" (e.g. minute hand on 4 = 20 past). From 6 round to 12 (the left side) we say "to" (e.g. minute hand on 8 = 20 to the next hour). **Quarter past** = minute hand on 3 (15 min). **Quarter to** = minute hand on 9 (15 min before the hour).

**Worked example.** Minute hand on 5, hour hand just past 2. Count in 5s to the 5: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 → **25 minutes past 2**. Move minute hand to the 10: 5,10,…,50 = 50 past = **10 minutes to 3**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "School/club starts at quarter past 9 — show me."
- Timing how long something takes (e.g. brushing teeth for 2 minutes).
- Bus or TV times.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** reads the minute hand on 4 as "4 minutes past."
  **Why:** reading the clock number, not minutes (each number = 5 minutes).
  **Fix:** count in 5s round to that number — 4 means 20 minutes.
- **Wrong:** keeps saying "past" after half past (e.g. "40 past" instead of "20 to").
  **Why:** hasn't switched to "to" on the second half.
  **Fix:** after the 6, count minutes *remaining* to the next hour; mark the past/to halves on the clock.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Minute hand on 3 — how many minutes past? **A:** 15 (quarter past).
- **Q:** Minute hand on 9 — say the time as "___ to." **A:** Quarter to / 15 minutes to.
- **Q:** Minute hand on 2, hour hand near 4 — what time? **A:** 10 minutes past 4.

### Activity / practice
**multipleChoice / speak:** Read clock faces to the nearest 5 minutes, including past and to; set the clock to given times like "twenty to 6."

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Telling the time (to 5 minutes).**
- **Topmarks — Telling the Time / Clockworks.**

## 9. Statistics: pictograms, tally charts and block diagrams

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Statistics

**Prerequisites:** Counting in 2s/5s/10s, Addition within 100.

### Key concepts
- Data can be collected and shown in tally charts, pictograms and block diagrams.
- Each picture/block stands for a count (sometimes one picture = more than one thing).
- We read charts to answer questions ("which had the most?", "how many altogether?").

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can count, skip-count, and add small totals.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Tally chart:** record each item with a stroke; the fifth stroke goes across the four (gate of 5) so groups are quick to count. **Pictogram:** a row of pictures, one per thing — or with a key like "🍏 = 2," count in 2s. **Block diagram / bar chart:** taller bar = more. **Concrete:** gather real data (favourite fruit in the family). **Read it:** find the tallest bar for "most," count or use the key for totals, compare two categories for "how many more?"

**Worked example.** Pictogram of pets, key 🐾 = 2. Dogs have 3 paw symbols → 3 × 2 = **6 dogs**. Cats have 2 → 4 cats. So 2 more dogs than cats.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Survey the family's favourite ice-cream flavours and chart them.
- Tally cars of each colour passing the window.
- Count and chart types of birds at a feeder.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** with a key of "= 2," counts each symbol as 1.
  **Why:** ignores the key.
  **Fix:** always read the key first; count the symbols, then multiply by what each is worth.
- **Wrong:** reads a half-symbol as a whole.
  **Why:** doesn't connect half a picture to half the value.
  **Fix:** with key = 2, half a symbol = 1; show the matching count.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** In a tally, how is 5 shown? **A:** Four strokes with a fifth across them.
- **Q:** Pictogram key 🍎 = 5; a row has 4 apples — how many? **A:** 20.
- **Q:** Which bar shows the most — read off the tallest. **A:** The tallest category (with its value).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / multipleChoice:** Read totals from a pictogram with a key; answer "most/least/how many more" from a bar chart; complete a tally for given data.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Statistics (pictograms and tally charts).**
- **Topmarks — data handling activities.**

## 10. Properties of 2-D and 3-D shapes; symmetry

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Geometry – properties of shapes

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 2-D and 3-D shapes.

### Key concepts
- 2-D shapes are described by sides and vertices (corners); 3-D shapes by faces, edges and vertices.
- A line of symmetry splits a shape into two matching halves that mirror each other.
- Shapes can be sorted by their properties.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Names common 2-D and 3-D shapes.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**2-D properties:** count **sides** (edges) and **vertices** (corners): pentagon = 5 sides, 5 vertices. **3-D properties:** **faces** (flat surfaces), **edges** (where two faces meet), **vertices** (corners): a cube has 6 faces, 12 edges, 8 vertices. **Symmetry:** fold a shape so both halves land exactly on top of each other — the fold line is a **line of symmetry**. A square has 4 lines of symmetry; a circle has many; an everyday scalene triangle may have none. **Concrete:** fold paper shapes; build solids and count faces.

**Worked example.** A cube: count flat faces (top, bottom, 4 sides) = 6 faces; count edges = 12; corners = 8. A square folded corner-to-corner and side-to-side gives matching halves → it has lines of symmetry.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Symmetry in butterflies, faces, leaves.
- Counting faces/edges of a dice and a cereal box (cuboid).
- Folding paper to make symmetrical pictures.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** counts a cube's faces as 4 (forgets top and bottom).
  **Why:** only counting the faces they can see.
  **Fix:** hold and rotate the solid, tally every face including hidden ones.
- **Wrong:** draws a "line of symmetry" that gives unequal halves.
  **Why:** thinks any dividing line is a line of symmetry.
  **Fix:** fold along the line — only a true line of symmetry makes the halves match exactly.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many faces does a cube have? **A:** 6.
- **Q:** How many sides and vertices has a pentagon? **A:** 5 sides, 5 vertices.
- **Q:** Does a square have a line of symmetry? **A:** Yes (in fact 4).

### Activity / practice
**categorize / multipleChoice:** Sort shapes by number of sides or by "has symmetry / no symmetry"; count faces/edges/vertices of given solids; draw a line of symmetry.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Properties of shapes & symmetry.**
- **Topmarks — Symmetry Sorting / shape games.**
