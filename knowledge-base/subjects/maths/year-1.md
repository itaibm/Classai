# Year 1 Mathematics (ages 5–6) — Key Stage 1

Topics in teaching order. Year 1 is hands-on: counters, fingers, cubes and real objects on nearly every step. Keep numbers small and the talk concrete.

---

## 1. Counting and numbers to 20

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Number – number & place value

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- Each number has a name, a written symbol, and a quantity that goes with it.
- Counting tells you "how many" — the last number you say is the total (the cardinal principle).
- Numbers go in a fixed order; you count one object for each number word (one-to-one).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can say some number words, roughly in order, to about ten.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Counting is matching: one number word touches one thing, in order, with none missed and none counted twice. **Concrete:** line up cubes and touch each as you say "one, two, three…". The very last word you say is *how many there are altogether* — that is the answer to "how many?" **Pictorial:** show dots or pictures and count them. **Abstract:** match the spoken word to the written numeral 1–20. Watch the teens: "thirteen" is "three-ten", i.e. ten and three more.

**Worked example.** Count the apples: 🍎🍎🍎🍎🍎🍎🍎. Touch each apple once, saying "one, two, three, four, five, six, seven." The last word is *seven*, so there are **7 apples**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Count stairs as you climb, or count toy cars / animals into a box.
- Count how many of something the child loves (football stickers, biscuits, Lego bricks).
- Count claps or jumps — the body makes the number concrete.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** counts the same object twice or skips one, gets the wrong total.
  **Why:** not matching one word to one object.
  **Fix:** move each object into a "counted" pile as it is counted, or line them up and touch each once.
- **Wrong:** counts correctly but, asked "how many?", recounts instead of saying the last number.
  **Why:** doesn't yet grasp that the last word *is* the total (cardinality).
  **Fix:** after counting, cover the objects and ask "so how many?" — emphasise the final number.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Here are 8 counters in a row. How many are there? **A:** 8 (counts each once; says the last number).
- **Q:** What number comes after 14? **A:** 15.
- **Q:** Show me 6 fingers. **A:** Holds up six fingers (e.g. 5 on one hand, 1 on the other).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / speak:** Show a picture of a group of objects (between 5 and 20). The child counts aloud and types or says how many. Repeat with different groups, including a teens number.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Counting** (counting objects and numbers to 20).
- **Numberblocks (CBeebies / BBC) — Series 1** (each episode is one small number, brilliant for cardinality).
- **Topmarks — Underwater Counting** (free online counting game to 20).

## 2. One more and one less

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 18 · **Subject strand:** Number – number & place value

**Prerequisites:** Counting and numbers to 20.

### Key concepts
- "One more" means count on by one; "one less" means count back by one.
- Numbers have neighbours: every number has one just before it and one just after it.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can count to 20 and knows the number order.

### Teach it (the explanation)
On a number track 1–20, "one more" is a single step to the right, "one less" a single step to the left. **Concrete:** put 5 cubes in a tower; add one — "one more, now 6"; take one away — "one less, now 5". **Pictorial:** point along a number line. **Abstract:** "One more than 7 is 8. One less than 7 is 6." You don't need to recount from one — just step.

**Worked example.** What is one more than 9? Find 9 on the number line, take one step right to **10**. What is one less than 9? Step left to **8**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- One more biscuit on the plate; one less when someone eats one.
- Scoring in a game: "you had 6 points, one more makes 7."
- One more / one less sticker in the album.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** for "one less than 10", says 11.
  **Why:** confuses "less" with "more," or always counts forward.
  **Fix:** physically remove a cube for "less" and add one for "more"; tie "less" to going back/down.
- **Wrong:** recounts from 1 every time instead of stepping from the number.
  **Why:** hasn't learned to start from a number (count on/back).
  **Fix:** practise "start at 7… one more… 8" without going back to 1.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** One more than 12? **A:** 13.
- **Q:** One less than 15? **A:** 14.
- **Q:** Which is bigger, one more than 8 or one less than 10? **A:** They are equal — both are 9.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Rapid-fire prompts: "one more than 6", "one less than 11", "one more than 19". Child types each answer. Mix more and less so they listen for the word.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — One more and one less.**
- **Topmarks — Helicopter Rescue / Blast Off** (number-order and more/less games).

## 3. Number bonds within 10

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Number – addition & subtraction

**Prerequisites:** Counting and numbers to 20.

### Key concepts
- A number can be split into two parts; the parts add back to the whole (part–whole).
- The pairs that make a number (its bonds) are worth knowing by heart — especially bonds to 10.
- Addition can be done in either order (3 + 2 = 2 + 3).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can count objects accurately and knows "altogether" means combine.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Take a whole, say 5, and split it. **Concrete:** 5 cubes; put some in your left hand, some in your right — 4 and 1, or 3 and 2, or 5 and 0. Every split is a *number bond* of 5. **Pictorial:** a part–whole diagram, with 5 in the whole and the two parts below. **Abstract:** 4 + 1 = 5, 3 + 2 = 5. Bonds to **10** matter most — they power later mental maths — so learn 0+10, 1+9, 2+8, 3+7, 4+6, 5+5 until they're automatic.

**Worked example.** Make 6 with two hands. Show 4 on one hand and 2 on the other: 4 + 2 = 6. Swap hands: 2 + 4 = 6 — same total. So 6 has bonds 0+6, 1+5, 2+4, 3+3.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sharing a small pile of treats into two bowls — "how could we split 7?"
- Football team: "we need 7 players; 4 are here, how many more?"
- Two pockets, ten marbles — how many ways to split them.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 are different and must be counted separately.
  **Why:** doesn't yet see addition as commutative.
  **Fix:** show the same cubes turned around — total unchanged; learn one bond, get its partner free.
- **Wrong:** to find 6 + 3, counts all from one (1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9) and loses track.
  **Why:** counting all instead of counting on from the bigger number.
  **Fix:** "put 6 in your head, count on 3: 7, 8, 9." Start from the larger number.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What two numbers make 10? Give two different pairs. **A:** e.g. 6 + 4 and 7 + 3 (any valid bond).
- **Q:** 5 + 3 = ? **A:** 8.
- **Q:** If 7 + 2 = 9, what is 2 + 7? **A:** 9 (same, order doesn't change the total).

### Activity / practice
**matchPairs:** Cards showing numbers; child matches the pairs that bond to 10 (1↔9, 2↔8, 3↔7, 4↔6, 5↔5). Then a few **numberEntry** bonds: "8 + ? = 10".

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Number bonds.**
- **Numberblocks — episodes on making and breaking numbers** (Series 2 "ten" episodes).
- **Topmarks — Hit the Button** (number bonds practice, very popular & free).

## 4. Addition and subtraction within 20

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Number – addition & subtraction

**Prerequisites:** Number bonds within 10, One more and one less.

### Key concepts
- Addition = combining or counting on; subtraction = taking away or counting back / finding the difference.
- The symbols +, −, = mean "add," "subtract," and "is the same as."
- Addition and subtraction are opposites: if 8 + 5 = 13, then 13 − 5 = 8.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows bonds within 10 and can count on/back.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Adding:** put the bigger number "in your head" and count on. **Subtracting:** count back, or take objects away and count what's left, or find the difference (how far apart). **Concrete:** cubes and a bead string for counting on/back. **Pictorial:** jumps on a number line. **Abstract:** 6 + 5 = 11; 11 − 5 = 6. The `=` sign means *the same as* — both sides balance — not "the answer comes next."

**Worked example.** 7 + 5. Hold 7 in your head. Count on 5: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12. So 7 + 5 = **12**. Check the opposite: 12 − 5 = 7. ✓

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Goals scored: "3 in the first half, 4 in the second — how many altogether?"
- Sweets eaten: "you had 15, ate 6, how many left?"
- Animals at the zoo: 9 monkeys, 4 more arrive.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** reads `4 + 3 = ?` then writes the next sum's first number, treating `=` as "and now do the next thing."
  **Why:** sees `=` as "here comes the answer" rather than "is equal to / balances."
  **Fix:** use a balance image; ask "what makes both sides the same?" Try 7 = 3 + 4 written backwards.
- **Wrong:** for 13 − 5, counts back but includes the start number, getting 9 instead of 8.
  **Why:** counts the starting number as the first jump.
  **Fix:** on the number line, the first jump *leaves* 13 and lands on 12; count the jumps, not the start.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 8 + 6 = ? **A:** 14.
- **Q:** 15 − 7 = ? **A:** 8.
- **Q:** If 9 + 4 = 13, what is 13 − 4? **A:** 9 (subtraction undoes addition).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Mixed set of within-20 sums and differences (e.g. 7+8, 16−9, 12+6, 20−4). Encourage counting on from the bigger number and counting back for subtraction.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Adding and Subtracting (within 20).**
- **Oak National Academy — KS1 Maths, addition and subtraction units.**
- **Topmarks — Hit the Button / Mental Maths Train.**

## 5. Place value: tens and ones to 100

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Number – number & place value

**Prerequisites:** Counting and numbers to 20.

### Key concepts
- A two-digit number is made of **tens** and **ones**: 34 is 3 tens and 4 ones.
- The position of a digit tells its value (the 3 in 34 means thirty, not three).
- Counting in tens (10, 20, 30…) gets you to big numbers fast.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can count to 20 reliably and recognise teen numbers.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Bundle ones into tens. **Concrete:** ten lollipop sticks make one bundle (a "ten"); base-10 rods (a rod = ten, a cube = one). 34 = three rods and four cubes. **Pictorial:** draw tens as long sticks and ones as dots. **Abstract:** 34 = 30 + 4 = "3 tens and 4 ones." The left digit counts tens, the right digit counts ones. That's why 34 and 43 are different — same digits, different positions, different value.

**Worked example.** Show 27. Take 2 ten-rods (that's 20) and 7 ones (that's 7): 20 + 7 = **27**. It has **2 tens and 7 ones**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Stickers come in packs of 10 — "3 packs and 5 loose = 35."
- 10p coins and 1p coins making an amount.
- Egg boxes hold 10 eggs: full boxes are tens, loose eggs are ones.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes "twenty-three" as 203.
  **Why:** writing each spoken part literally (20 then 3) instead of by place value.
  **Fix:** build it with ten-rods and ones; the 2 *means* two tens, so it sits in the tens column — 23.
- **Wrong:** thinks 43 is bigger digits so it must be bigger than 34… or treats 34 and 43 as the same.
  **Why:** ignoring digit position.
  **Fix:** model both with rods — 4 tens beats 3 tens; position decides value.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many tens and ones in 56? **A:** 5 tens and 6 ones.
- **Q:** What number is 4 tens and 2 ones? **A:** 42.
- **Q:** Count in tens from 0 to 50. **A:** 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / fillBlank:** "38 = ___ tens and ___ ones." Then build numbers: "Make 61 with tens and ones — how many of each?" (6 tens, 1 one).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Place value (tens and ones).**
- **Numberblocks — Series 3 "ten" and place-value episodes.**
- **Topmarks — Place Value Basketball.**

## 6. Halves and quarters

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions

**Prerequisites:** Counting and numbers to 20.

### Key concepts
- A fraction is one of **equal** parts of a whole.
- A half is one of **two** equal parts; a quarter is one of **four** equal parts.
- You can halve/quarter a shape *or* a set of objects.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can share objects and count small amounts.

### Teach it (the explanation)
"Half" only counts when the two parts are **equal**. **Concrete:** fold a paper square so both parts match exactly — each is a half. Fold again into four matching parts — each is a quarter. Share 8 sweets fairly between two — half each is 4. **Pictorial:** shade half a circle, a quarter of a square. **Abstract:** half of 8 is 4; a quarter of 8 is 2. Two halves make the whole; four quarters make the whole.

**Worked example.** Half of 6 cubes. Share into two equal piles: 3 and 3. So **half of 6 = 3**. A quarter of 8: four equal piles of 2, so a quarter of 8 = **2**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sharing a pizza or sandwich fairly between two (halves) or four (quarters).
- Cutting fruit in half; sharing a bag of grapes between friends.
- Half-time in football — half the match played.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** calls any two pieces "halves," even unequal ones.
  **Why:** "half" understood as "a piece," not "an equal piece."
  **Fix:** fold paper so the parts overlap exactly — only equal parts are halves.
- **Wrong:** thinks a quarter is bigger than a half (because 4 > 2).
  **Why:** more pieces = bigger, applying whole-number thinking.
  **Fix:** cut the same cake into 2 vs 4 — the quarter pieces are smaller; more pieces means each is smaller.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is half of 10? **A:** 5.
- **Q:** Is this shape split into halves? (show unequal split) **A:** No — the parts aren't equal.
- **Q:** What is a quarter of 12? **A:** 3.

### Activity / practice
**multipleChoice / numberEntry:** Show shapes; pick the ones split into equal halves/quarters. Then "half of 4? half of 8? quarter of 4?" Child answers.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Fractions (halves and quarters).**
- **Oak National Academy — KS1 Maths, fractions of shapes and amounts.**
- **Topmarks — fraction matching games.**

## 7. Money: recognising coins

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Measurement – money

**Prerequisites:** Counting and numbers to 20, Place value: tens and ones.

### Key concepts
- Coins have different values (1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1, £2).
- A coin's value is not about its size — a tiny 5p is worth more than a big 2p.
- You can add coins to make an amount.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can count and recognise small numbers; counts in 2s/5s/10s a little.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Each coin is worth a fixed number of pennies. **Concrete:** real or plastic coins; line them up and name each value. A 5p is worth the same as five 1p coins — swap them to prove it. **Pictorial:** pictures of coins to count up. **Abstract:** 10p + 5p + 2p = 17p. Counting mixed coins is easier if you start with the biggest and count on. Value is printed on the coin, not shown by its size.

**Worked example.** Count 10p + 5p + 1p + 1p. Start big: 10… count on 5 → 15… then 1 → 16… then 1 → **17p**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A toy shop: "this rubber costs 7p — which coins pay for it?"
- Saving coins in a money box and counting them.
- Choosing snacks within a small budget.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks the 2p is worth more than the 5p because it's bigger.
  **Why:** judging value by physical size.
  **Fix:** swap a 5p for five 1p coins, and a 2p for two — the 5p clearly buys more.
- **Wrong:** counts every coin as "1," getting 3 coins = 3p when they're 10p+5p+2p.
  **Why:** counting coins instead of their values.
  **Fix:** say each coin's value, not "one"; count on by the value (10, 15, 17).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which is worth more, a 5p or a 2p? **A:** 5p.
- **Q:** How much is 10p + 2p + 2p? **A:** 14p.
- **Q:** Show me two coins that make 7p. **A:** 5p and 2p.

### Activity / practice
**matchPairs / numberEntry:** Match coins to their values, then "make this amount": show 8p, 15p, 20p — child chooses coins. Type the total of a small set of coins.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Money (recognising coins).**
- **Topmarks — Coins / Toy Shop Money game.**

## 8. Telling the time: o'clock and half past

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Measurement – time

**Prerequisites:** Counting and numbers to 20.

### Key concepts
- A clock has two hands: the short one shows the hour, the long one shows the minutes.
- "O'clock" = the long hand points to 12; "half past" = the long hand points to 6.
- Time moves in order: the hour hand creeps from one number to the next.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows numbers 1–12 and can read them on a clock face.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Concrete:** a geared teaching clock you can turn. The **short hand** is the **hour**; the **long hand** is the **minutes**. When the long hand points straight up to **12**, it's "something **o'clock**" — read the number the short hand is on (e.g. 3 o'clock). When the long hand points straight down to **6**, half the hour has passed — "**half past**." At half past, the short hand sits *between* two numbers (half past 3 = short hand between 3 and 4).

**Worked example.** Long hand on 12, short hand on 9 → **9 o'clock**. Now move the long hand to 6: half an hour later, the short hand is between 9 and 10 → **half past 9**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "We have dinner at 6 o'clock — show me on the clock."
- TV programme starts at half past 4.
- Bedtime / wake-up times the child knows.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** reads "half past 3" as half past 4 because the short hand is near the 4.
  **Why:** reading the nearer number instead of the hour just passed.
  **Fix:** the short hand has *left* 3 but not *reached* 4 — say the number it left.
- **Wrong:** swaps the hands, reading the long hand as the hour.
  **Why:** confusing which hand is which.
  **Fix:** "short hand = short word (hour); long hand = long count (minutes)."

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Long hand on 12, short hand on 7 — what time? **A:** 7 o'clock.
- **Q:** Where does the long hand point at half past? **A:** To the 6.
- **Q:** At half past 2, between which numbers is the short hand? **A:** Between 2 and 3.

### Activity / practice
**multipleChoice / speak:** Show clock faces; child reads each (o'clock or half past). Then "set the clock to 5 o'clock / half past 8" and describe the hands.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Telling the time (o'clock and half past).**
- **Topmarks — Telling the Time (matching analogue clocks).**

## 9. 2-D and 3-D shapes

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Geometry – properties of shapes

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- 2-D shapes are flat (circle, square, triangle, rectangle); 3-D shapes are solid (cube, sphere, cone, cylinder, pyramid).
- Shapes are named by their features: number of sides and corners (2-D), or flat faces (3-D).
- The same shape stays the same shape when you turn it.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can count to about 6 (for sides/corners).

### Teach it (the explanation)
**2-D** shapes are flat like a drawing on paper. Count **sides** (straight edges) and **corners**: a triangle has 3 sides and 3 corners, a square has 4 equal sides and 4 corners. **3-D** shapes are solid things you can hold: a cube (like a dice), a sphere (a ball), a cylinder (a tin), a cone (an ice-cream cone). **Concrete:** sort real objects into flat-drawings vs solids; feel the corners and faces. A shape turned on its side is still the same shape — a triangle balanced on a point is still a triangle.

**Worked example.** Is a shape with 4 straight sides, all the same length, and 4 corners a square? Count: 4 equal sides ✓, 4 corners ✓ → yes, a **square**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Shape hunt around the house: ball = sphere, tin = cylinder, dice = cube, window = rectangle.
- Building with blocks — which solids stack, which roll?
- Road signs: triangles warn, circles command.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** says a triangle balanced on its point is "not a triangle."
  **Why:** thinks orientation changes the shape (only sees triangles "pointing up").
  **Fix:** rotate a cut-out triangle slowly — same 3 sides and corners, still a triangle.
- **Wrong:** calls a cube a square (or a sphere a circle).
  **Why:** mixing flat (2-D) names with solid (3-D) names.
  **Fix:** "flat = 2-D drawing name; solid you can hold = 3-D name." A face of the cube is a square; the cube is the solid.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many sides does a triangle have? **A:** 3.
- **Q:** Name a 3-D shape that rolls. **A:** Sphere (or cylinder/cone).
- **Q:** A flat shape with 4 equal sides — what is it? **A:** A square.

### Activity / practice
**categorize / matchPairs:** Sort pictures into 2-D vs 3-D, or match each shape to its name. Then "find something at home shaped like a cylinder."

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — 2D and 3D shapes.**
- **Topmarks — shape games (Shape Monsters / Carroll diagrams).**

## 10. Position and direction

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 16 · **Subject strand:** Geometry – position & direction

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- Position words describe where things are: on, under, next to, in front of, behind, above, below.
- Direction & turn words: left, right, forwards, backwards, a half turn, a quarter turn.
- The same object can be described from different points of view.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Understands everyday language; can follow a simple instruction.

### Teach it (the explanation)
This is mostly about precise everyday language. **Concrete:** put a teddy *on* the box, *under* the box, *next to* the box, *behind* the box — name each. For direction, the child turns their body: a **whole turn** brings you back to facing the start; a **half turn** faces the opposite way; a **quarter turn** is a corner turn (90°). Practise "turn right," "go forwards two steps." Left and right are tricky — pick a reliable cue (e.g. the hand you write with).

**Worked example.** Stand facing the window. Make a half turn — now you face the door (the opposite way). Make a quarter turn — now you face the side wall.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "Robot game": you give turn/step instructions, the child moves like a robot.
- Hide a toy and give position clues ("it's under the chair, behind the cushion").
- Following a simple treasure map: forwards, turn right, forwards.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** mixes up left and right.
  **Why:** left/right are relative and not yet automatic.
  **Fix:** use a body cue (writing hand = right) and lots of "turn to your ___" practice.
- **Wrong:** thinks "behind" depends on where *they* stand, so disagrees with another viewpoint.
  **Why:** position can be relative to the object, not the speaker.
  **Fix:** agree a reference ("behind the teddy from the teddy's point of view") and demonstrate.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** If you make a half turn, are you facing the same way or the opposite way? **A:** Opposite.
- **Q:** Put your hand *above* your head, then *below* your chin. **A:** Performs correctly.
- **Q:** A quarter turn is how much of a full turn? **A:** A quarter (one of four).

### Activity / practice
**speak / ordering:** Direction game — the tutor calls "forwards 2, turn right, forwards 1" and the child describes where they end up, or orders a set of moves to reach a target.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Maths — Position and direction.**
- **Oak National Academy — KS1 Maths, position and direction units.**
