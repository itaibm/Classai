# Year 4 Mathematics (ages 8–9) — Lower Key Stage 2

Year 4 reaches 10,000, all times tables to 12×12, rounding, negative numbers, Roman numerals, decimals (tenths and hundredths), area, coordinates and the first line graphs. This is the year multiplication-table fluency is formally checked (the national Multiplication Tables Check).

---

## 1. Numbers and place value to 10,000

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Number – number & place value

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 place value to 1000.

### Key concepts
- Four-digit numbers have thousands, hundreds, tens and ones.
- Each place is ten times the place to its right.
- Compare, order, partition and count in 1s/10s/100s/1000s.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Place value to 1000; comparing three-digit numbers.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Concrete/pictorial:** a Th | H | T | O place-value chart. 3,482 = 3 thousands, 4 hundreds, 8 tens, 2 ones = 3000 + 400 + 80 + 2. **Compare** by checking the biggest place first (thousands), then move right. Counting in 1000s changes the thousands digit (2482 → 3482). Watch zeros as placeholders (3,047 = 3 thousands, 0 hundreds, 4 tens, 7 ones).

**Worked example.** Compare 4,512 and 4,521. Thousands equal, hundreds equal (5=5), tens 1 vs 2 → 2 wins, so **4,521 > 4,512**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Distances between towns in km.
- Stadium crowd sizes.
- Big scores in a game.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes "four thousand and seven" as 4007? correct — but writes "4 thousand 7 hundred" issues, or drops zeros (407).
  **Why:** missing placeholder zeros.
  **Fix:** put each digit in its column; empty columns get a 0.
- **Wrong:** says 999 > 1000 (more 9s look bigger).
  **Why:** comparing digits not place value.
  **Fix:** 1000 has a thousands digit; 999 doesn't — four-digit beats three-digit.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Partition 6,209. **A:** 6000 + 200 + 0 + 9.
- **Q:** Which is bigger, 7,108 or 7,180? **A:** 7,180 (tens decide).
- **Q:** What is 1000 more than 4,560? **A:** 5,560.

### Activity / practice
**ordering / numberEntry:** Order four-digit numbers; partition into Th/H/T/O; "1000 more / 100 less" jumps.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Place value to 10,000.**
- **Topmarks — Place Value Charts / Shark Numbers (4-digit).**

## 2. Rounding to the nearest 10, 100 and 1000

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Number – number & place value

**Prerequisites:** Numbers and place value to 10,000.

### Key concepts
- Rounding gives a near, easier number.
- Look at the digit to the right of the place you're rounding to: 5 or more rounds up, 4 or less rounds down.
- Rounding helps estimate and check answers.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Place value to 10,000; number line ordering.

### Teach it (the explanation)
To round, decide which two "round" numbers a value sits between, then pick the nearer. **Pictorial:** a number line — is 47 nearer 40 or 50? It's past halfway (45), so 50. **The rule:** look at the digit just to the right of your rounding place. **5, 6, 7, 8, 9 → round up; 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 → round down.** Everything to the right becomes 0. Round 4,627 to the nearest 100: the tens digit is 2 (less than 5) → round down → 4,600.

**Worked example.** Round 3,486 to the nearest 1000. Between 3000 and 4000; the hundreds digit is 4 (less than 5) → round **down** to **3,000**. Round it to the nearest 100: tens digit 8 (≥5) → round **up** to **3,500**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "About how many people?" at an event.
- Estimating shopping totals.
- Rounding distances on a map.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** rounds 4 up because "round" means make bigger.
  **Why:** thinks rounding always increases.
  **Fix:** rounding goes to the *nearer* number; 4 is nearer the lower one — round down.
- **Wrong:** looks at the wrong digit (e.g. rounds to nearest 100 but checks the ones).
  **Why:** unsure which digit decides.
  **Fix:** check the digit immediately to the *right* of the place you're rounding to.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Round 68 to the nearest 10. **A:** 70.
- **Q:** Round 4,350 to the nearest 1000. **A:** 4,000.
- **Q:** Round 2,749 to the nearest 100. **A:** 2,700.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / multipleChoice:** Round given numbers to 10/100/1000; "which number rounds to 500 to the nearest 100?"

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Rounding numbers.**
- **Topmarks — Rounding games.**

## 3. Negative numbers and Roman numerals

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Number – number & place value

**Prerequisites:** Numbers and place value to 10,000.

### Key concepts
- Numbers continue below zero: −1, −2, −3 … get smaller as you go down/left.
- You can count through zero (3, 2, 1, 0, −1, −2).
- Roman numerals (I, V, X, L, C, D, M) are another way to write numbers, used on clocks and dates.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Number line ordering; place value.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Negative numbers:** extend the number line to the left of 0. Below zero, the bigger the digit after the minus, the *lower* the number (−5 is colder/lower than −2). **Concrete:** a thermometer — temperatures below freezing are negative. Count down through zero: 2, 1, 0, −1, −2. **Roman numerals:** I=1, V=5, X=10, L=50, C=100, D=500, M=1000. Repeating adds (III=3); a smaller numeral *before* a bigger one subtracts (IV=4, IX=9, XL=40). Read clocks and years (e.g. MMXXIV = 2024).

**Worked example.** Temperature falls from 3 °C by 5 degrees: 3, 2, 1, 0, −1, −2 → **−2 °C**. Write 24 in Roman numerals: XX (20) + IV (4) = **XXIV**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Winter temperatures below zero.
- Floors below ground (−1, −2) in a lift.
- Roman numerals on clock faces and film dates.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks −5 is bigger than −2 because 5 > 2.
  **Why:** applies whole-number size below zero.
  **Fix:** on the number line/thermometer, −5 is *lower* (colder) than −2, so −2 is the bigger number.
- **Wrong:** writes 4 as IIII or 9 as VIIII.
  **Why:** doesn't use the subtractive rule.
  **Fix:** a smaller numeral before a larger one subtracts: IV = 4, IX = 9.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which is colder, −3 °C or −7 °C? **A:** −7 °C.
- **Q:** What is XL? **A:** 40.
- **Q:** Count back from 2 by ones, four times. **A:** 1, 0, −1, −2.

### Activity / practice
**ordering / matchPairs:** Order a set of positive and negative numbers; match Roman numerals to values (IX↔9, XL↔40, MMXX↔2020).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Negative numbers; Roman numerals.**
- **Topmarks — Temperature / number line games.**

## 4. Times tables to 12 × 12

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 28 · **Subject strand:** Number – multiplication & division

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 tables (3, 4, 8) and 2/5/10.

### Key concepts
- All multiplication facts to 12 × 12 should be recalled quickly.
- Facts come in families with their division partners.
- Useful patterns: 9s (digits sum to 9), 11s (repeat the digit to ×9), commutativity halves the work.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Tables for 2,3,4,5,8,10; arrays; commutativity and inverse.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The goal is **rapid recall** of every fact to 12 × 12, both ways and as division. Build the missing tables (6, 7, 9, 11, 12) from what you know: 6× = double 3×; 9× = 10× minus 1× (9×7 = 70 − 7 = 63); 12× = 10× + 2×. **Patterns:** in the 9 table the tens go up and ones go down, and digits sum to 9 (18, 27, 36…). Commutativity means you really only learn about half the grid. Practise little and often — this is the year of the **Multiplication Tables Check**.

**Worked example.** 9 × 7 using the 10s trick: 10 × 7 = 70, take away one 7 → 70 − 7 = **63**. Fact family: 9×7, 7×9, 63÷9=7, 63÷7=9.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Eggs in boxes of 6 or 12.
- Scoring in games (×9, ×11 bonuses).
- Sharing into equal teams of 6 or 7.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** knows 7 × 8 but stalls on 8 × 7 or 56 ÷ 7.
  **Why:** learned one direction only.
  **Fix:** drill the whole fact family from one fact; use commutativity and the inverse.
- **Wrong:** for 6 × 7 counts up the 6 table from the start each time.
  **Why:** no instant recall; relying on skip-counting.
  **Fix:** use known facts (5×7=35, +7 = 42) and timed practice to build recall.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 7 × 8 = ? **A:** 56.
- **Q:** 9 × 6 = ? **A:** 54.
- **Q:** 72 ÷ 12 = ? **A:** 6.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Timed mixed recall across all tables to 12×12, including division facts; "fill the fact family from 6 and 9."

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Times tables.**
- **Topmarks — Hit the Button (all tables, MTC-style practice).**
- **Oak National Academy — Year 4 multiplication units.**

## 5. Column multiplication and short division

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 28 · **Subject strand:** Number – multiplication & division

**Prerequisites:** Times tables to 12 × 12, Year 3 grid method, Column addition.

### Key concepts
- Multiply a 2- or 3-digit number by a 1-digit number using the column method (with carrying).
- Short division ("bus stop") splits a number into the divisor, carrying remainders.
- Estimate to judge the answer.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- All tables to 12; partitioning; place value.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Column multiplication:** line up by place; multiply each digit by the multiplier from the right, carrying tens like in addition. **Short division (bus stop):** write the number under the "bus stop," divide each digit left to right, carrying any remainder to the next digit. **Estimate first** (243 × 3 ≈ 240 × 3 = 720). Always keep digits in their columns.

**Worked example (×).** 243 × 3.
```
  2 4 3
×     3
```
3×3 = 9; 3×4 = 12 → write 2 carry 1; 3×2 = 6 +1 = 7 → **729**.
**Worked example (÷).** 96 ÷ 4: 9 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 1 (carry the 1 → 16); 16 ÷ 4 = 4 → **24**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Total cost of several items at the same price.
- Sharing a big number of cards equally.
- Working out how many seats in rows.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** in 243 × 3 forgets to add the carried ten.
  **Why:** drops the carry between columns.
  **Fix:** note the carry above the next column and add it in; check against the estimate.
- **Wrong:** in short division, ignores the remainder instead of carrying it.
  **Why:** treats each digit independently.
  **Fix:** carry the remainder to make the next digit bigger (the 1 in 96÷4 makes 16).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** 134 × 4 = ? **A:** 536.
- **Q:** 84 ÷ 6 = ? **A:** 14.
- **Q:** Estimate 312 × 3. **A:** About 900.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Column ×1-digit (e.g. 246×3, 158×4) and short division (e.g. 96÷4, 132÷6). Estimate first, then compute.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Column multiplication; short division.**
- **Oak National Academy — Year 4 written methods units.**

## 6. Decimals: tenths and hundredths

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 26 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions (decimals)

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 tenths, Place value, Division by 10/100.

### Key concepts
- Decimals extend place value to the right of the decimal point: tenths then hundredths.
- 0.1 = 1/10, 0.01 = 1/100; ÷10 shifts digits one place right.
- Decimals and fractions can name the same number (0.5 = ½ = 5/10).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Tenths as fractions; ÷ by 10 and 100.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Past the decimal point, places keep dividing by ten: tenths, then hundredths. **Pictorial:** a 100-square — one column shaded = 0.1 (a tenth), one small square = 0.01 (a hundredth). **Dividing by 10** moves every digit one place to the right (so 4 ÷ 10 = 0.4; 36 ÷ 10 = 3.6); ÷100 moves two places. **Link to fractions:** 0.7 = 7/10; 0.25 = 25/100 = ¼. The decimal point sits *between ones and tenths* and never moves — the digits move.

**Worked example.** 1/10 of 8 as a decimal: 8 ÷ 10 = **0.8**. Write 3/100 as a decimal: hundredths → **0.03**. And 0.5 = 5/10 = **½**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Money: £0.50 is half a pound; £2.05.
- Measuring length in metres (1.25 m).
- Sports times in seconds and tenths.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks 0.45 > 0.5 because "45 > 5."
  **Why:** comparing the decimal part as a whole number, ignoring place value.
  **Fix:** line up by place: 0.5 = 0.50; 50 hundredths > 45 hundredths, so 0.5 is bigger.
- **Wrong:** 4 ÷ 10 = 0.04.
  **Why:** moves digits two places, or treats it as hundredths.
  **Fix:** ÷10 moves *one* place right: 4 → 0.4.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Write 7/10 as a decimal. **A:** 0.7.
- **Q:** What is 26 ÷ 10? **A:** 2.6.
- **Q:** Which is bigger, 0.3 or 0.25? **A:** 0.3.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / matchPairs:** Convert tenths/hundredths between fraction and decimal; ÷10 and ÷100; order a set of decimals.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Decimals (tenths and hundredths).**
- **Khan Academy — Decimals (intro, tenths & hundredths).**

## 7. Equivalent fractions and fractions of amounts

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Number – fractions

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 equivalence, Times tables, Division.

### Key concepts
- Multiply or divide numerator and denominator by the same number to make equivalent fractions.
- To find a fraction of an amount: divide by the denominator, multiply by the numerator.
- Fractions can be added/subtracted with the same denominator and counted past one whole.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Equivalent fractions; fractions of quantities; tables.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Equivalence:** ×/÷ top and bottom by the same number keeps the value (½ = 3/6 because ×3 on both). **Pictorial:** fraction wall. **Fraction of an amount:** ⅗ of 20 → divide by 5 (20 ÷ 5 = 4, that's one fifth), then × 3 = 12. **Abstract rule:** "divide by the bottom, times by the top." Adding same-denominator fractions can pass a whole: 3/4 + 2/4 = 5/4 = 1¼.

**Worked example.** ⅗ of 30: 30 ÷ 5 = 6 (one fifth), × 3 = **18**. Equivalent to ⅔? ⅔ = (2×5)/(3×5) = 10/15, etc.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- ¾ of a class, ⅗ of a team.
- Recipe scaling (⅔ of the sugar).
- Sharing a pack of sweets in fifths.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** to find ¾ of 12, divides by 3 (the numerator).
  **Why:** divides by the wrong number.
  **Fix:** divide by the *denominator* (4) to get one part (3), then × numerator (3) = 9.
- **Wrong:** makes "equivalent" fractions by adding the same number to top and bottom (½ → 3/5 by +2).
  **Why:** uses addition instead of multiplication.
  **Fix:** must *multiply* both by the same number; check on the fraction wall.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** ⅔ of 12 = ? **A:** 8.
- **Q:** Make a fraction equivalent to ¾. **A:** e.g. 6/8, 9/12.
- **Q:** 5/6 − 2/6 = ? **A:** 3/6 (= ½).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / matchPairs:** Fractions of amounts; match equivalent fractions; same-denominator add/subtract including answers over 1.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Equivalent fractions; fractions of amounts.**
- **Oak National Academy — Year 4 fractions units.**

## 8. Area and perimeter

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 24 · **Subject strand:** Measurement

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 perimeter, Multiplication.

### Key concepts
- Perimeter is the distance around a shape (add the sides); area is the space inside (count squares).
- Area is measured in square units (cm², m²).
- For a rectangle, area = length × width.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Perimeter; multiplication facts.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Perimeter** = total of all the sides (a length). **Area** = how much surface a shape covers (a region), counted in **squares**. **Concrete:** cover a rectangle with 1 cm squares and count them. **Pictorial:** a grid — a 4 by 3 rectangle has 4 columns of 3 = 12 squares. **Abstract:** area of a rectangle = length × width (4 × 3 = 12 cm²). Keep the two ideas separate: perimeter is the fence around the garden; area is the grass inside.

**Worked example.** A rectangle 5 cm by 3 cm. **Perimeter** = 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16 cm. **Area** = 5 × 3 = **15 cm²**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Fencing (perimeter) vs turf/carpet (area) for a garden or room.
- Tiles needed to cover a floor (area).
- Edging around a picture (perimeter).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** confuses area and perimeter (adds the sides when asked for area).
  **Why:** the two ideas blur together.
  **Fix:** perimeter = walk around the edge; area = fill the inside with squares. Different units (cm vs cm²).
- **Wrong:** counts only the squares along the edges for area.
  **Why:** thinks area is a boundary, not a filling.
  **Fix:** area covers the whole inside — count every square, or use length × width.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Area of a 6 cm × 4 cm rectangle? **A:** 24 cm².
- **Q:** Perimeter of the same rectangle? **A:** 20 cm.
- **Q:** What units do we use for area? **A:** Square units (e.g. cm²).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry:** Find area (by counting squares and by × ) and perimeter of rectangles; "a rectangle has area 12 cm² and one side 3 cm — what's the other side?" (4 cm).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Area and perimeter.**
- **Topmarks — area/perimeter activities.**

## 9. Coordinates and translation

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 22 · **Subject strand:** Geometry – position & direction

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 position & direction; reading numbered axes.

### Key concepts
- A point on a grid is described by coordinates (x, y): along the corridor (x), then up the stairs (y).
- The order matters: (3, 2) is different from (2, 3).
- Translating a shape slides it without turning or flipping it.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Number lines; left/right, up/down.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A coordinate grid has two axes from an origin (0,0). To plot **(x, y)** go **across first** (x), then **up** (y) — "along the corridor, up the stairs." **Concrete:** a giant floor grid; walk to a point. **Abstract:** (3, 2) means 3 right, 2 up. **Translation** moves a shape a set number of squares right/left and up/down — every point shifts the same way; size and orientation stay the same. Translating (1, 4) by "3 right, 2 up" gives (4, 6).

**Worked example.** Plot (4, 1): 4 right, 1 up. Translate it 2 left and 3 up: 4−2 = 2 across, 1+3 = 4 up → new point **(2, 4)**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Battleships / grid games.
- Map references (the cinema is at C3).
- Moving a game piece a set number of squares.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** plots (3, 2) as 3 up and 2 across (reads y first).
  **Why:** reverses the order.
  **Fix:** "along the corridor (x) before up the stairs (y)" — x always first.
- **Wrong:** thinks translation can rotate or resize the shape.
  **Why:** confuses translation with other transformations.
  **Fix:** translation only *slides*; every point moves the same distance and direction — no turning.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which comes first in (5, 2)? **A:** The 5 (across / x).
- **Q:** Plot (2, 3) — describe the move from the origin. **A:** 2 right, 3 up.
- **Q:** Translate (1, 1) by 3 right, 1 up. **A:** (4, 2).

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / multipleChoice:** Read off and plot coordinates; translate points/shapes by given amounts; "where does this point land after the slide?"

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Coordinates; translation.**
- **Topmarks — coordinate grid games.**

## 10. Statistics: line graphs and tables

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Statistics

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 bar charts, Coordinates.

### Key concepts
- Line graphs show how something changes over time (continuous data).
- Read a value by going up from the time axis to the line, then across to the scale.
- Tables organise data in rows and columns for comparison.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Reading scaled axes; coordinates; addition/subtraction.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **line graph** plots points and joins them to show a trend (e.g. temperature through the day). The bottom axis is usually **time**; the side axis is the **measured value**. **Read it like coordinates:** find the time, go up to the line, read across to the value. The line **rising** means increasing, **falling** means decreasing, **flat** means no change. **Tables** present data in a grid; read the right row and column to find a value, and compare across rows.

**Worked example.** A temperature line graph: at 9 am the line is at 12 °C, at 1 pm at 18 °C. So between 9 am and 1 pm the temperature **rose by 6 °C** (18 − 12). The steepest rise is where the line climbs most.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Temperature or rainfall through a day/week.
- A plant's height measured each week.
- Distance travelled over time on a journey.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** reads the value off the wrong axis or ignores the scale interval.
  **Why:** not tracing up-then-across, or miscounting the scale.
  **Fix:** go *up from the time, across to the value*; check what each gridline is worth.
- **Wrong:** thinks a flat line means "nothing is there" rather than "no change."
  **Why:** misreads a horizontal segment.
  **Fix:** flat = the value stayed the same over that time.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** On a line graph, what does a rising line mean? **A:** The value is increasing over time.
- **Q:** At 2 pm the line is at 15 — what's the value? **A:** 15.
- **Q:** Temperature went 10 → 16 — how much did it rise? **A:** 6.

### Activity / practice
**numberEntry / multipleChoice:** Read values and changes from a line graph; answer "when was it highest / by how much did it change"; find a value in a two-way table.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Maths — Line graphs; tables.**
- **Oak National Academy — Year 4 statistics units.**
