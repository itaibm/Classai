# French History (Histoire de France) — Topics

A **chronological** journey through the story of France, ages 5–12, in English. Each topic follows the standard Classai content contract (`_TEMPLATE.md`): four analysis fields (key concepts, prior knowledge, hooks, misconceptions) plus a teach-it explanation, ready-to-use checks, an activity, and real resources. Maps to `subjectKey: history`.

**Timeline anchors to keep on the wall** (return to these every lesson — children muddle the order):
**Prehistory** (before writing, Lascaux ~17,000 yrs ago) → **Antiquity** (Gauls; Caesar conquers Gaul **52 BC**; then Roman Gaul) → **Middle Ages** (Clovis ~500 → Charlemagne **800** → castles & cathedrals → Joan of Arc **1429**) → **Modern times** (Renaissance 1500s → Louis XIV & Versailles 1600s → **Revolution 1789** → Napoleon **1804**) → **the Republic** (tricolore, Marianne, Eiffel Tower **1889**, today).

A quick **BC/AD** reminder for the tutor: BC = before year 0, counting *down* (52 BC is older than 10 BC); AD = the years counting up to today. So 52 BC → AD 500 → AD 1429 → 1789 runs oldest to newest.

## 1. Prehistoric France — the cave painters of Lascaux

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 6–9 · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – la Préhistoire

**Prerequisites:** none (this is the start of the timeline).

### Key concepts
- **Prehistory** means *before writing* — so we know it only from things people left behind (paintings, bones, tools), not from books. This is **detective work** (archaeology).
- About **17,000 years ago**, people in what is now France painted hundreds of animals deep inside the **Lascaux** caves — among the most famous prehistoric art in the world.
- These painters were **clever and skilled**: they made paints from earth and charcoal, used animal-fat lamps for light, and drew horses, deer and bulls (*aurochs*) with real artistry.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Understands "a very long time ago," and that we can learn about the past from objects, not only books.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Long before there was a country called France — about **17,000 years ago** — people already lived on this land. There were no towns, no farms yet, no writing. So how could we possibly know anything about them? We become **detectives of the past**: we study the **things they left behind**. The most amazing clues of all are hidden deep inside caves.

In **1940**, four teenage boys exploring the woods near **Montignac** in the Dordogne (south-west France) followed their dog down a hole — and discovered a cave covered, wall to ceiling, in paintings: the **Lascaux cave**. Hundreds of animals gallop across the rock: **horses**, **deer**, huge wild bulls called **aurochs**, bison and ibex. The painters mixed colours from the **earth** (yellows, reds, browns) and **charcoal** (black), and worked by the light of little lamps that burned animal fat — deep underground in the dark.

Why did they paint? We aren't completely sure — and that's an honest, exciting part of history. Maybe to bring luck for the hunt, maybe to tell stories, maybe as something sacred. The paintings are so fragile that the real cave is now **closed** to protect them (people's breath was damaging them!), so visitors go to an exact **copy** built nearby. Lascaux reminds us that "prehistoric people" were not stupid cavemen grunting in furs — they were thoughtful, artistic humans, a lot like us.

**Worked example.** "How do we know about Lascaux's people if they left no writing?" Chain it: no writing → no books or letters → **but** they left **paintings, bones and tools** → archaeologists study these objects → from the animals painted we learn what they hunted and saw. So the *evidence* is the art itself.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Make your own "cave painting" with earthy colours (or chalk under a table, in the "dark") — why would you paint where it's so hard to reach?
- Animal lovers: count the animals (horses win!) and ask which ones still live in France today.
- The boys-and-a-dog discovery story — a real-life treasure-hunt.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Cavemen lived with dinosaurs."
  **Why:** films and toys mix them up.
  **Fix:** dinosaurs were gone about **66 million** years before these painters; they never met. Show both on a (very stretched) timeline.
- **Wrong idea:** "Prehistoric people were stupid because they had no writing."
  **Why:** "no books" is read as "not clever."
  **Fix:** they invented paints, lamps, tools and beautiful art — extremely clever with what they had.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does *prehistory* mean, and how do we learn about it? **A:** Before writing — so we use the objects people left behind (paintings, bones, tools), i.e. archaeology.
- **Q:** What is Lascaux, and what's painted there? **A:** A famous cave in France covered in prehistoric paintings of animals — horses, deer, aurochs.
- **Q:** Why can't visitors go into the real Lascaux cave now? **A:** The paintings are very fragile (visitors' breath damaged them), so people visit an exact copy instead.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize + speak.* Sort items into "prehistoric evidence" (cave painting, animal bones, flint tool) vs "things they did NOT have" (a written book, a photograph, a phone). Then a *speak* prompt: "You found a painted cave — describe one animal you see and guess why they painted it."

- **Quelle Histoire — "La Préhistoire"** (children's history, FR) — prehistory for kids.
- **BBC Bitesize / BBC Teach — "cave paintings" / prehistoric art** clips (general prehistory, kid-level).

## 2. The Gauls and Vercingetorix

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 7–10 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – l'Antiquité (les Gaulois)

**Prerequisites:** Prehistoric France (the idea of "long ago" and using sources).

### Key concepts
- The **Gauls** (*les Gaulois*) were **Celtic** peoples who lived across **Gaul** — roughly today's France — in many separate **tribes**, around 2,000+ years ago. They were skilled farmers, metalworkers and warriors.
- They were **not one united country**: dozens of tribes, often quarrelling, each with its own chief — which made them easier to conquer.
- **Vercingetorix**, a young Gaulish chief, **united many tribes** to fight the Roman army of **Julius Caesar**, but was finally defeated at **Alesia in 52 BC**. He became a French national hero — and the model for the comic-book Gaul **Asterix**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows "BC" means a very long time ago (before year 0); understands what a "tribe" and a "leader" are.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Before France was France, this land was called **Gaul**, and its people were the **Gauls** — part of the wider **Celtic** world. Picture not one kingdom but **lots of separate tribes**, living in villages, farming, raising animals, and making fine things from iron and gold. They were brave warriors with a fearsome reputation. (If you've met **Asterix and Obelix**, that little Gaulish village is a funny, cartoon version of these real people — moustaches, helmets and all!)

The Gauls' great weakness was that they were **divided**: the tribes often fought *each other*. Meanwhile, the mighty **Roman Empire** was growing, and its general **Julius Caesar** marched his disciplined army north to conquer Gaul. One young chief saw that the only hope was to **unite**. His name was **Vercingetorix**. He rallied many tribes together into a single army — the first time the Gauls truly joined forces.

For a while it worked. But Caesar trapped Vercingetorix and his army in the hilltop town of **Alesia** and built enormous walls *all around* it. In **52 BC**, starving and surrounded, Vercingetorix **surrendered** to save his people — riding out and laying down his weapons before Caesar. Gaul became Roman. Vercingetorix lost the battle, yet France remembers him as a **hero**: the man who first tried to unite the country to defend it.

**Worked example.** "Why did the Gauls lose to the Romans?" Weigh it: the Gauls were brave **but divided** into quarrelling tribes; the Romans had **one** large, trained, organised army. Even when Vercingetorix finally **united** many tribes, it was almost too late — Caesar trapped them at Alesia. So: **division vs. unity and organisation** decided it.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Asterix** — separate the funny cartoon (magic potion!) from the real, brave-but-divided Gauls.
- "United we stand": a quick game where one big team beats several small squabbling ones — *that's* why unity mattered.
- Gaulish gold and ironwork — they were superb craftspeople, not just fighters.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The Gauls were one country, like France today."
  **Why:** modern France is one nation, so the past is assumed the same.
  **Fix:** Gaul was **many separate tribes** that often fought each other — that's exactly why Rome could conquer them.
- **Wrong idea:** "Asterix is what the Gauls were really like."
  **Why:** the comic is most children's only image.
  **Fix:** Asterix is a fun cartoon (no magic potion in real life!); the real Gauls were genuine Celtic farmers, smiths and warriors.
- **Wrong idea:** "The Gauls and the Romans were the same people."
  **Why:** both are "ancient and long ago."
  **Fix:** the **Romans came from Italy** and **conquered** the Gauls; they were different peoples — until, over time, they blended (next topic).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Who were the Gauls and where did they live? **A:** Celtic tribes who lived in Gaul (roughly today's France), in many separate tribes.
- **Q:** What did Vercingetorix do, and how did it end? **A:** He united many Gaulish tribes against Julius Caesar's Romans, but was defeated and surrendered at Alesia in 52 BC.
- **Q:** Why were the Gauls easier for Rome to conquer? **A:** They were divided into many quarrelling tribes, while Rome had one big organised army.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + matchPairs.* Match: Gauls → Celtic tribes of Gaul; Vercingetorix → chief who united the tribes; Caesar → Roman general; Alesia/52 BC → the final defeat; Asterix → the cartoon Gaul. Then *multipleChoice*: "What was the Gauls' biggest weakness?" (they were divided into tribes).

- **Lumni (francetv) — les Gaulois** — short curriculum clips on Gaulish life.
- **Britannica Kids — "Gaul" / "Julius Caesar"** — kid-level reference (English).

## 3. Roman Gaul (la Gaule romaine)

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 8–11 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – l'Antiquité (la Gaule romaine)

**Prerequisites:** The Gauls and Vercingetorix (the conquest of Gaul, 52 BC).

### Key concepts
- After Caesar's conquest, Gaul became part of the **Roman Empire** for about **500 years** — this period is called **Gallo-Roman** (a blend of Gaulish and Roman ways).
- The Romans **transformed** the land: **roads**, **towns** and **cities** (Lyon — *Lugdunum* — was the capital), **aqueducts** to carry water (the **Pont du Gard**), **theatres**, **arenas** and **public baths**, plus the **Latin** language (the root of French).
- It wasn't replacement but **blending**: Gauls became Gallo-Romans, keeping some of their gods and ways while living the Roman way — the foundations of later France.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Romans (from Italy) conquered Gaul under Caesar; understands "empire" and what a city is.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Once Gaul was conquered, it became a **province** of the huge **Roman Empire** — and stayed Roman for around **500 years**. We call this the **Gallo-Roman** age, because the two ways of life **blended together**. The Gauls didn't all vanish; they slowly took on Roman customs, clothes, food and the **Latin** language — which, changed over centuries, became **French**.

The Romans were brilliant builders, and they **transformed** Gaul. They laid long, straight **roads** to move armies and goods quickly. They built proper **towns and cities** with stone buildings — **Lyon** (then called **Lugdunum**) became the **capital** of Roman Gaul. They put up grand **theatres** and **arenas** (you can still sit in the Roman arena at Nîmes today), and **public baths** where people washed and met friends. Most astonishing of all were the **aqueducts**: stone channels carrying fresh water for miles into the cities. The **Pont du Gard**, a towering three-level bridge that carried an aqueduct across a valley, still stands after **2,000 years** — built by hand, without machines.

So Roman Gaul is a story of **change you can still see**: French cities, roads, ruins and even the language all trace back to this blend. When the Roman Empire grew weak and fell apart (around the AD 400s), the roads and towns remained — ready for the next chapter.

**Worked example.** "What did the Romans leave behind in Gaul that we can still see or use?" List the lasting marks: **roads** (some under modern ones), **cities** (Lyon began as Lugdunum), **aqueducts** like the **Pont du Gard**, **arenas and theatres** (Nîmes, Orange), and the **Latin** language that became **French**. So even after Rome fell, Roman things shaped France for centuries.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- The **Pont du Gard** as an engineering puzzle — how do you carry water across a valley with no pumps? (Gravity + a tiny, perfect slope.)
- "They had heated public baths 2,000 years ago" — Roman daily life and comfort.
- Words detective: many French (and English) words come from **Latin** — show one or two simple roots.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The Romans killed all the Gauls and replaced them."
  **Why:** "conquered" sounds like "wiped out."
  **Fix:** the two peoples **blended** into Gallo-Romans; the Gauls became Roman in language and ways — they didn't disappear.
- **Wrong idea:** "Rome is in France / the Romans were French."
  **Why:** "Roman Gaul" makes Rome sound local.
  **Fix:** Rome is in **Italy**; the Romans came from abroad and ruled Gaul as part of a giant empire.
- **Wrong idea:** "The Romans built nothing that lasted."
  **Why:** it was "ages ago."
  **Fix:** roads, city foundations, arenas and the Pont du Gard survive after 2,000 years.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does *Gallo-Roman* mean? **A:** The blend of Gaulish and Roman life after Gaul became part of the Roman Empire.
- **Q:** Name two things the Romans built in Gaul that still exist. **A:** Any two of: roads, cities (Lyon/Lugdunum), aqueducts (Pont du Gard), arenas/theatres (Nîmes, Orange), baths.
- **Q:** Which language did the Romans bring, and what did it eventually become? **A:** Latin — which slowly changed into French.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize / matchPairs.* Sort "before the Romans" (Gaulish villages, tribes, no big stone cities) vs "Roman Gaul" (roads, stone cities, aqueducts, baths, Latin). Match: Lugdunum → Lyon; Pont du Gard → aqueduct; Latin → became French.

### Resources
- **Pont du Gard official site / Lumni — "la Gaule romaine"** — the aqueduct and Gallo-Roman life (FR, parent-guided).
- **Quelle Histoire — "Jules César" / Gallo-Roman** content — kid-level (FR).
- **Horrible Histories — "Rotten Romans"** (official BBC channel) — Roman life and engineering, funny and memorable (parent-previewed).

## 4. Clovis and the Franks

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 8–11 · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – le Moyen Âge (les Francs)

**Prerequisites:** Roman Gaul (and that the Roman Empire weakened and fell apart).

### Key concepts
- As the Roman Empire fell apart (AD 400s), new peoples moved into Gaul. One group, the **Franks**, gave their name to **France** (*Francia* = "land of the Franks").
- **Clovis** was the first king to **unite** the Frankish tribes into one kingdom (around AD 500) — a founding moment of France. He chose **Reims** as the place he became Christian.
- Clovis's choice to become **Christian** (baptised at Reims) tied the new kingdom to the Church and helped Franks and the local Gallo-Romans become **one people** — and started the long line of French kings.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Roman Empire ruled Gaul then weakened; understands "king" and "kingdom."

### Teach it (the explanation)
When the great Roman Empire grew weak and broke apart (around the **AD 400s**), the strong order it had kept fell away, and new peoples moved across Gaul. The most important of these were the **Franks** — and their name is the reason the country is called **France**: *Francia* simply means "land of the Franks."

At first the Franks, like the Gauls before them, were **split into groups**. Then a clever, fierce young king named **Clovis** **united** them into a single kingdom around the year **500**. This is one of the founding moments of France — for the first time, much of the old Gaul had one Frankish king.

Clovis made one more choice that mattered for a thousand years. Most Franks worshipped many gods, but the people of Gaul were largely **Christian**. Clovis decided to become a **Christian** too, and was **baptised at Reims** by a bishop. This was huge: it meant the Frankish kings and the Christian Church became partners, and the Franks and the Gallo-Romans, sharing a religion, slowly became **one people**. Because of this, Reims later became the city where French kings were crowned — a tradition reaching all the way to Joan of Arc's story, centuries later.

**Worked example.** "Why does it matter that Clovis became Christian?" Chain it: the local Gallo-Romans were Christian → Clovis (a Frank) becomes Christian too → now king and Church are partners, and Franks + Gallo-Romans share a religion → they blend into one people → the kingdom of the Franks (France) holds together. So a single choice helped *make* France.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Word detective: **France** = land of the **Franks** — the country is named after them.
- "Uniter" pattern again: like Vercingetorix tried to unite the Gauls, Clovis *succeeds* in uniting the Franks — compare them.
- Reims as a "crowning city" — set up that kings will be crowned there for centuries (link forward to Joan of Arc).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "France was always called France."
  **Why:** today's map feels timeless.
  **Fix:** the name comes from the **Franks**; before that it was **Gaul**, then **Roman Gaul** — the name changed with the people.
- **Wrong idea:** "Clovis and the Gauls were the same time."
  **Why:** everything "old" blurs together.
  **Fix:** put it in order on the timeline — **Gauls → Roman Gaul → Clovis & the Franks (~500)**. Clovis comes *much* later, after Rome fell.
- **Wrong idea:** "The Franks wiped out everyone already living there."
  **Why:** "new people moved in" sounds like replacement.
  **Fix:** Franks and Gallo-Romans **blended** into one people, especially once they shared the Christian religion.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Where does the name "France" come from? **A:** From the **Franks** — *Francia*, "land of the Franks."
- **Q:** What did Clovis do around the year 500? **A:** He united the Frankish tribes into one kingdom — a founding moment of France.
- **Q:** Why was Clovis becoming Christian (at Reims) important? **A:** It tied the kings to the Church and helped Franks and Gallo-Romans become one people.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + fillBlank.* Order the timeline so far: Lascaux → Gauls → Roman Gaul → Clovis & the Franks. Fill the blank: "The country is named after the ___." (Franks). *MatchPairs*: Clovis → first king to unite the Franks; Reims → where he was baptised (and kings were crowned).

- **Lumni (francetv) — Clovis / les Francs** — short curriculum clips.

## 5. Charlemagne, the great emperor

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 8–11 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – le Moyen Âge (Charlemagne)

**Prerequisites:** Clovis and the Franks (the Frankish kingdom).

### Key concepts
- **Charlemagne** ("Charles the Great") was a Frankish king who built a **huge empire** across western Europe and was crowned **Emperor by the Pope on Christmas Day, AD 800** in Rome.
- He cared about **learning**: he gathered scholars, encouraged **schools** and reading and copying of books — a burst of education sometimes called the "Carolingian renaissance." (French children playfully blame/thank him for school!)
- His empire didn't last as one piece — after him it was split up — but he became a legendary symbol of a united, learned Europe.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows Clovis united the Franks and founded a Christian kingdom; understands "empire" vs "kingdom."

### Teach it (the explanation)
A few hundred years after Clovis, the greatest of all the Frankish kings appeared: **Charlemagne**, which means **"Charles the Great."** He was a tall, energetic warrior-king who conquered land in every direction, until his kingdom became a vast **empire** stretching across much of western Europe — France, Germany, parts of Italy and beyond.

On **Christmas Day in the year 800**, in Rome, the **Pope crowned Charlemagne "Emperor."** This was a big deal: there hadn't been an emperor in the West since the Roman Empire fell. It said: a strong, Christian, united Europe is back.

But Charlemagne is loved for more than conquering. He believed in **learning**. At a time when very few people could read, he invited the cleverest scholars to his court, encouraged **schools** at monasteries, and had monks carefully **copy books** by hand so knowledge wasn't lost. This burst of education is one reason French schoolchildren cheekily say Charlemagne "invented school"! He even worked to make handwriting clearer and spelling more regular.

His empire was so big that, after he died, it was **divided** among his grandsons and never held together as one piece again — one part grew into France, another into Germany. But Charlemagne himself became a **legend**: the model of a great, wise, Christian emperor who valued books as well as battles.

**Worked example.** "Why is Charlemagne remembered, beyond his battles?" List it: he was **crowned Emperor (800)**, the first in the West since Rome; he built a **huge empire**; and — unusually — he championed **learning, schools and books**. So he's remembered as a *wise* ruler, not only a conqueror.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "The king who loved school" — the playful French idea that Charlemagne is to blame for homework.
- Copying books by hand: try copying a paragraph perfectly with no mistakes — why printing (much later) changed everything.
- A crown on Christmas Day in Rome — a dramatic, picture-able moment.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Charlemagne ruled France the same shape as today."
  **Why:** modern borders feel fixed.
  **Fix:** his **empire** was much bigger than France and was split up after him; France was only **one part** of it.
- **Wrong idea:** "Everyone could read back then."
  **Why:** we assume schools were normal.
  **Fix:** very **few** people could read — which is exactly why Charlemagne's push for schools and copied books mattered so much.
- **Wrong idea:** "Emperor and king mean the same thing."
  **Why:** both are "rulers."
  **Fix:** an **emperor** rules over many lands/peoples (an empire); Charlemagne was *raised* from king to **emperor** by the Pope — a step up.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does "Charlemagne" mean, and what was he crowned in the year 800? **A:** "Charles the Great"; he was crowned **Emperor** by the Pope.
- **Q:** Why is Charlemagne remembered for *learning*? **A:** He encouraged schools, gathered scholars and had books copied by hand, when few could read.
- **Q:** Did Charlemagne's empire stay together after him? **A:** No — it was divided among his heirs; one part became France, another Germany.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs + multipleChoice.* Match: Charlemagne → "Charles the Great"; year 800 → crowned Emperor; monasteries → where books were copied and schools grew. *MultipleChoice*: "What did Charlemagne become in 800?" (Emperor / a Gaul / a Roman) → Emperor.

- **Lumni (francetv) — Charlemagne** — short curriculum clips on the empire and schools.

## 6. Medieval France — castles, knights and cathedrals

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 6–11 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – le Moyen Âge (la société médiévale)

**Prerequisites:** Charlemagne (after his empire split, France entered the High Middle Ages).

### Key concepts
- In the **Middle Ages** (roughly years 1000–1400s), France was a land of **castles** (*châteaux*), **knights** (*chevaliers*), **lords**, and **peasants** who farmed the land — a layered society called the **feudal system**.
- A **castle** was both a **fortress** (for defence) and a **home** for a lord; a **knight** was a warrior on horseback who promised loyalty and followed a code of honour (chivalry). Most people, though, were **peasants** doing the hard farm work.
- It was also a great age of building soaring **cathedrals** in the new **Gothic** style — tall, with pointed arches, flying buttresses and stained-glass windows — like **Notre-Dame de Paris**, Chartres and Reims.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has the timeline so far (Clovis → Charlemagne); understands "king," "lord" and "farmer/peasant."

### Teach it (the explanation)
After Charlemagne's empire split up, France entered the heart of the **Middle Ages** — the world of **castles and knights** that fills so many stories. But let's see what it was *really* like.

Society was built in **layers**, like a pyramid — the **feudal system**. At the top, the **king**. Below him, powerful **lords** (nobles) who controlled large areas of land. Lords had **knights** (*chevaliers*) — warriors who fought on horseback in armour, swore **loyalty** to their lord, and were supposed to follow a code of honour called **chivalry** (be brave, protect the weak, keep your word). And at the bottom — by far the most people — were the **peasants**: farmers who worked the land, grew the food, and gave part of their crops to the lord in return for protection. Most children in this lesson would have been **peasant** children, not knights or princesses.

A **castle** (*château*) wasn't just for fairy tales — it was a serious **fortress**, with thick stone walls, towers and sometimes a moat, built to defend against attack. It was *also* the lord's **home**. When danger came, villagers could shelter inside.

This was also the great age of the **cathedral**. Using a brilliant new style we call **Gothic**, builders raised stone churches **higher than anything before**, with **pointed arches**, **flying buttresses** (outside supports) holding up the walls, and huge **stained-glass windows** glowing with colour. **Notre-Dame de Paris**, and the cathedrals of Chartres and Reims, took *generations* to build — some workers spent their whole lives on one cathedral they'd never see finished. They were the skyscrapers of their day.

**Worked example.** "Put medieval society in order, top to bottom." King → lords (nobles) → knights → peasants. Then notice: the **most** people were at the **bottom** (peasants doing the farming), even though stories mostly tell us about kings and knights. That's a key history idea — the famous few vs. the ordinary many.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Design your own castle: where do the walls, towers and moat go, and *why* (defence)?
- Knights and chivalry: armour, shields, jousting — *and* the "rules of honour" a knight promised.
- Cathedrals as medieval "skyscrapers": look at a Notre-Dame photo and find the pointed arches and flying buttresses.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Everyone in the Middle Ages was a king, knight or princess."
  **Why:** stories and films only show the famous few.
  **Fix:** **most** people were **peasants** — farmers doing hard daily work; knights and nobles were a small minority.
- **Wrong idea:** "Castles were just fancy palaces / homes."
  **Why:** we picture fairy-tale castles.
  **Fix:** a castle was first a **fortress for defence** (thick walls, towers, moat) — *and* a home; comfort came second to safety.
- **Wrong idea:** "Medieval people couldn't build anything impressive."
  **Why:** "old" is read as "primitive."
  **Fix:** they raised vast **Gothic cathedrals** — engineering marvels with stained glass and flying buttresses, taking generations to finish.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Order medieval society top to bottom: knights, peasants, king, lords. **A:** King → lords → knights → peasants.
- **Q:** Who were most people in the Middle Ages? **A:** **Peasants** — farmers who worked the land (not knights or royalty).
- **Q:** Name two features of a Gothic cathedral. **A:** Any two of: pointed arches, flying buttresses, tall stone walls, stained-glass windows (e.g. Notre-Dame de Paris).
- **Q:** What were the two main jobs of a castle? **A:** A fortress for **defence** and a **home** for the lord.

Then sort jobs/things into "castle" (defence walls, lord's home, moat) vs "cathedral" (stained glass, pointed arches, place to pray). *Speak* prompt: "You're a peasant child — describe one thing you do all day."

## 7. Joan of Arc (Jeanne d'Arc)

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 8–12 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – fin du Moyen Âge (Jeanne d'Arc)

**Prerequisites:** Medieval France (kings, knights, the world she lived in).

### Key concepts
- **Joan of Arc** (*Jeanne d'Arc*) was a teenage **peasant girl** who, during a long war between **France and England** (the Hundred Years' War), led French soldiers to victory at **Orléans in 1429**.
- She said **voices** of saints told her to help save France and crown the rightful king. Thanks to her, **Charles VII** was crowned king at **Reims** in 1429.
- She was captured, put on trial and **killed in 1431**, aged only about 19. Long afterwards she was cleared of wrongdoing and made a **saint** — today she is one of France's great **national heroes**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows medieval France had kings, knights and wars; understands "siege" loosely and that Reims was the crowning city (from Clovis).

### Teach it (the explanation)
For over a hundred years, France and **England** fought a long, bitter war we call the **Hundred Years' War**. By the 1420s, things looked terrible for France: the English and their allies controlled large parts of the country, and the French prince couldn't even get safely crowned as king.

Into this came one of history's most surprising heroes: a **teenage peasant girl** named **Joan**, from the village of Domrémy. Joan said she heard the **voices** of saints telling her that God wanted her to help **save France** and see the rightful prince crowned king. Astonishingly, she convinced the prince to let her ride with the army — in armour, carrying a banner — when she was only about **17**.

In **1429**, Joan helped lead the French to lift the **siege of Orléans**, a city the English had surrounded. It was a turning point: the French began to win, hope returned, and soon the prince was crowned **King Charles VII** at **Reims** — the traditional crowning city — with Joan beside him.

Then the story turns sad. Joan was **captured** by France's enemies, put on **trial**, and in **1431** she was **killed**, aged only about **19**. (Treat this gently: she was unfairly condemned by her enemies.) But that's not the end. Years later, a new trial **cleared her name**, and centuries on she was made a **saint**. Today **Jeanne d'Arc** is one of France's most beloved heroes — proof that even a young person, with courage and belief, can change history.

**Worked example.** "Why is Joan of Arc remembered as a hero?" Chain it: France was losing the war → a teenage peasant **girl** rallied the army → she helped **win at Orléans (1429)** → the king was crowned at Reims → even though she was captured and killed (1431), she had turned the war and inspired France → later cleared and made a **saint**. So: an unlikely hero whose courage changed her country.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "A teenager who led an army" — she was barely older than an older sibling; what would *you* dare to do?
- Banners and armour — she carried a banner rather than mainly fighting; a symbol of hope.
- Heroes who lose and still win: she died young, yet France remembers her above many kings.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Joan of Arc was a queen or a noble lady."
  **Why:** leaders in stories are usually royal.
  **Fix:** she was a **peasant girl** from a small village — that's what makes her so remarkable.
- **Wrong idea:** "She fought the Romans / lived in ancient times."
  **Why:** all "old history with armour" blurs together.
  **Fix:** she lived in the **Middle Ages (1400s)**, during the war between **France and England** — long after the Romans. Place 1429 on the timeline.
- **Wrong idea:** "France and England were always one friendly country."
  **Why:** modern friendship.
  **Fix:** in Joan's day they fought the long **Hundred Years' War**; she helped France resist the English.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Who was Joan of Arc, and what was unusual about her? **A:** A teenage peasant girl who led French soldiers — unusual because she was young, poor and a girl.
- **Q:** What happened at Orléans in 1429? **A:** Joan helped the French lift the English siege — a turning point in the war.
- **Q:** Which king did she help to be crowned, and where? **A:** Charles VII, crowned at Reims.
- **Q:** How is Joan of Arc remembered in France today? **A:** As a national hero and a saint (after being unfairly killed in 1431 and later cleared).

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + speak.* Order her story: hears the voices → convinces the prince → wins at Orléans (1429) → king crowned at Reims → captured → remembered as a hero/saint. *Speak* prompt: "Why do you think people still admire Joan of Arc?"

## 8. Kings and the Renaissance — François I and the Loire châteaux

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 8–12 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – les Temps modernes (la Renaissance)

**Prerequisites:** Joan of Arc (end of the Middle Ages; the war won, kings now strong).

### Key concepts
- The **Renaissance** (1500s) was a "rebirth" of art, learning and beautiful building, spreading from **Italy** into France — a bridge out of the Middle Ages into "modern times."
- King **François I** loved this new art and learning. He built dazzling **châteaux** (palaces) in the **Loire Valley** — most famously **Chambord**, with its hundreds of rooms and a clever **double-spiral staircase**.
- He invited the great Italian artist **Leonardo da Vinci** to live in France — which is why Leonardo's painting the **Mona Lisa** ended up in France (today in the **Louvre** in Paris).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows France had kings and the Middle Ages have ended; understands "palace" and "artist."

### Teach it (the explanation)
After the hard centuries of the Middle Ages, a wave of new ideas and beauty swept across Europe — the **Renaissance**, a French word meaning **"rebirth."** Starting in **Italy** in the 1400s–1500s, people fell in love again with art, science, books, and graceful buildings. It marks the start of what historians call **"modern times."**

In France, the king who threw the doors open to the Renaissance was **François I** (Francis the First). He thought a great king should be surrounded by beautiful things and clever people. So he built magnificent **châteaux** — grand palaces — especially along the **Loire Valley**. The most spectacular is **Chambord**, a fairy-tale château with **hundreds of rooms** and a famous **double-spiral staircase** where two staircases wind around each other so people going up never meet those coming down (some think the idea came from Leonardo himself).

And here's a wonderful link: François I admired the Italian genius **Leonardo da Vinci** so much that he **invited Leonardo to live in France**, near the royal châteaux, where Leonardo spent his last years. Leonardo brought a small painting with him — the **Mona Lisa** — which is why, to this day, the world's most famous painting hangs in the **Louvre** museum in **Paris** rather than in Italy.

So the Renaissance in France is a story of **kings, beauty and new ideas** — and of how France became home to some of the greatest art in the world.

**Worked example.** "Why is the Mona Lisa in France?" Chain it: François I loves Renaissance art → he invites **Leonardo da Vinci** to live in France → Leonardo brings the **Mona Lisa** with him → after he dies in France, the painting stays → today it hangs in the **Louvre** in Paris. So a king's love of art put it there.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Chambord's** double-spiral staircase — draw two spirals that never touch; a real architectural puzzle.
- The **Mona Lisa** mystery — the most famous painting in the world, and *that's why* it's in France.
- "Renaissance person": Leonardo painted *and* designed machines *and* studied the body — could you be good at lots of things?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The Renaissance was a king or a war."
  **Why:** most history words children meet are people or battles.
  **Fix:** the Renaissance is a **time of new art, learning and building** — a *change in ideas*, not a person or a fight.
- **Wrong idea:** "Leonardo da Vinci was French."
  **Why:** he died in France and the Mona Lisa is there.
  **Fix:** he was **Italian**, but **invited** to live in France by François I — that's why his work is here.
- **Wrong idea:** "Châteaux are the same as medieval castles."
  **Why:** both are called *château* / castle.
  **Fix:** Renaissance châteaux like Chambord were built for **beauty and pleasure** (palaces), not mainly for **defence** like medieval fortress-castles.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does "Renaissance" mean, and what was reborn? **A:** "Rebirth" — a rebirth of art, learning and beautiful building (from Italy into France) in the 1500s.
- **Q:** What did King François I build in the Loire Valley? **A:** Grand châteaux (palaces), most famously Chambord with its double-spiral staircase.
- **Q:** Why is the Mona Lisa in France? **A:** François I invited Leonardo da Vinci to live in France, and Leonardo brought the painting; it's now in the Louvre.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs + multipleChoice.* Match: Renaissance → "rebirth" of art and learning; François I → king who built Loire châteaux; Chambord → double-spiral staircase; Leonardo da Vinci → Italian genius invited to France; Mona Lisa → famous painting in the Louvre. *MultipleChoice*: "A Renaissance château like Chambord was built mainly for…" (beauty / defence) → beauty.

## 9. Louis XIV, the Sun King, and Versailles

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 8–12 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – l'Ancien Régime (la monarchie absolue)

**Prerequisites:** François I and the Renaissance (kings grow ever more powerful).

### Key concepts
- **Louis XIV** ruled France for an astonishing **72 years** (1643–1715) — longer than any other king of a big European country. He called himself the **"Sun King"** (*le Roi-Soleil*), with France revolving around him like planets round the sun.
- He was an **absolute monarch**: he held nearly **all** the power and answered to no one but God ("*L'État, c'est moi*" — "I am the state," a saying linked to him).
- He turned a hunting lodge into the gigantic **Palace of Versailles** — with the dazzling **Hall of Mirrors** and vast gardens — and made the nobles **live there with him**, kept busy with strict, fancy court rules so they couldn't challenge his power.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows France had increasingly powerful kings; understands "palace" and roughly what "power" means.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Some kings ruled for a few years. **Louis XIV** ruled for **72** — he became king as a small **boy** and didn't stop until he was an old man (1643–1715). He chose a brilliant nickname: the **"Sun King"** (*le Roi-Soleil*). Just as the sun sits at the centre and everything circles it, Louis wanted **all** of France to revolve around **him**.

Louis was an **absolute monarch**. "Absolute" means his power had almost **no limits** — he made the laws, led the army, and answered to no one but God. A famous saying linked to him is *"L'État, c'est moi"* — **"I am the state."** That's very different from today, where leaders are chosen and limited by laws — and remembering that difference helps explain the Revolution later.

His greatest creation was the **Palace of Versailles**. He took his father's hunting lodge near Paris and turned it into one of the **largest, grandest palaces in the world**, with hundreds of rooms, the glittering **Hall of Mirrors**, fountains and enormous formal gardens. But Versailles was also clever **politics**: Louis made the powerful **nobles come and live at court** with him. There, he kept them busy with elaborate rules and ceremonies — even *watching the king wake up* was an honour! Distracted by all this fancy etiquette and gossip, the nobles had no time or power to plot against him.

So Louis XIV is the picture of the **all-powerful king**: glorious, but it cost a fortune, and ordinary people paid heavy **taxes** for it — a problem that would grow until, two kings later, France boiled over into Revolution.

**Worked example.** "Why did Louis XIV make the nobles live at Versailles?" Reason it out: powerful nobles could **threaten** a king → so Louis brought them **close**, to court → kept them busy with **etiquette, ceremonies and pleasures** → with no time or independence to plot, they couldn't challenge him. So Versailles wasn't just a palace — it was a tool to **control** the nobles.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- The **Hall of Mirrors** — hundreds of mirrors when mirrors were rare and precious; pure showing-off.
- "I am the state" — what would it be like if **one** person made *every* rule? (Sets up the Revolution.)
- Court etiquette so strict that watching the king wake up was a privilege — weird and fascinating.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Louis XIV was an emperor / like Napoleon."
  **Why:** both seem like all-powerful French rulers.
  **Fix:** Louis XIV was a **king** (the Ancien Régime), born royal; Napoleon came **much later** and made *himself* Emperor — different times and different titles.
- **Wrong idea:** "A king back then had to obey laws and a parliament, like leaders today."
  **Why:** we judge by modern government.
  **Fix:** Louis was an **absolute** monarch — almost no limits on his power. That's a key difference that *caused* later anger.
- **Wrong idea:** "Versailles was just a beautiful palace, nothing more."
  **Why:** we see only the glamour.
  **Fix:** it was also a **political trap** to keep nobles close and powerless — and it was hugely **expensive**, paid for by taxes on ordinary people.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Why was Louis XIV called the "Sun King"? **A:** He wanted France to revolve around him as planets revolve around the sun — he was the centre of everything.
- **Q:** What does it mean that Louis XIV was an *absolute* monarch? **A:** He held almost all the power and answered to no one but God (no parliament limiting him).
- **Q:** Why did Louis make nobles live at Versailles? **A:** To keep them close and busy with court etiquette so they couldn't plot against him.
- **Q:** Name one famous part of the Palace of Versailles. **A:** The Hall of Mirrors (or the huge gardens/fountains).

### Activity / practice
*Categorize + speak.* Sort facts into "shows his power/glory" (Sun King, Hall of Mirrors, huge palace) vs "controls the nobles" (makes them live at court, strict etiquette, watching him wake). *Speak* prompt: "If one person made every single rule, what could go wrong?" (links to the Revolution).

## 10. Everyday life long ago — the three "estates"

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 8–12 · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – l'Ancien Régime (la société d'ordres)

**Prerequisites:** Louis XIV and Versailles (the world of the all-powerful king).

### Key concepts
- Under the kings (the **Ancien Régime**), French society was split into **three "estates" (groups)**: the **clergy** (Church), the **nobles**, and **everyone else** — the **Third Estate** (about 97% of people: farmers, workers, merchants).
- It was **deeply unfair**: the clergy and nobles had wealth and special privileges and paid **little or no tax**; the huge Third Estate did the work *and* paid **most of the taxes**.
- Most people were **poor peasants** who worked the land, while a tiny few lived in luxury — this unfairness is the key **cause** of the Revolution that was coming.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows France had powerful kings and nobles (Louis XIV); understands "tax" loosely (money paid to those in charge).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Before we get to the Revolution, let's see how **ordinary people** actually lived under the kings — a time called the **Ancien Régime** ("the old order"). Here's the key idea: French society was divided into **three big groups**, called the **three estates**.

- The **First Estate** was the **clergy** — the people of the Church.
- The **Second Estate** was the **nobles** — lords and ladies, the rich and powerful families.
- The **Third Estate** was **everyone else** — and that meant **almost everybody**: farmers (the most numerous), workers, shopkeepers, merchants, doctors. About **97 out of every 100 people** were in the Third Estate.

Now the part that made people angry. The first two estates — the clergy and the nobles — had the **wealth, the fine clothes, the big houses, and special privileges**, and they paid **little or no tax**. Meanwhile the enormous Third Estate did almost **all the work** *and* paid **most of the taxes**. Imagine the smallest group having the most, paying the least; the biggest group having the least, paying the most. That feels **unfair** — and it *was*.

For a poor **peasant** family, life was hard: long days farming, simple food, and if the harvest failed, real **hunger**. When the price of bread rose and people went hungry while nobles feasted, patience ran out. This unfairness is the great **cause** behind everything in the next lessons: the people of the Third Estate began to demand a **voice** and **fair** treatment.

**Worked example.** "Why was the system unfair?" Compare the groups: clergy + nobles = a **small** number, but they had **wealth and privileges** and paid **little tax**; the Third Estate = the **huge** majority, but they did the **work** and paid **most of the tax**. The smallest groups had the most and paid the least → that imbalance is the unfairness that led to revolution.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A fairness game: 3 children, but 1 gets most of the snacks and does no chores while the other 2 do everything — how does that feel?
- "A day in the life" of a peasant child vs a noble child — same age, completely different lives.
- The price of **bread** — when it rose, families starved; small things can spark big change.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Everyone in old France lived in castles and palaces."
  **Why:** history stories spotlight kings and nobles.
  **Fix:** the **vast majority** were poor **Third Estate** people — mostly peasant farmers; the rich were a tiny minority.
- **Wrong idea:** "The rich nobles paid the most tax because they had the most money."
  **Why:** it seems logical/fair.
  **Fix:** the opposite — nobles and clergy paid **little or no tax**; the poor **Third Estate** paid most. That unfairness caused fury.
- **Wrong idea:** "People were happy with the way things were."
  **Why:** "it was just how life was."
  **Fix:** many were **angry and hungry**; the unfairness built up pressure that exploded in 1789.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What were the three estates? **A:** The clergy (Church), the nobles, and the Third Estate (everyone else — most people).
- **Q:** Which estate was almost everybody in? **A:** The Third Estate (about 97% — farmers, workers, merchants).
- **Q:** Why was it unfair? **A:** The clergy and nobles had wealth and privileges and paid little tax; the huge Third Estate did the work and paid most of the taxes.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize + speak.* Sort people into the three estates (a priest, a duke, a farmer, a baker, a wealthy lady, a shoemaker). Then *speak*: "You are in the Third Estate — what would you complain about to the king?" (links straight to 1789).

## 11. The French Revolution — 1789 and the Bastille

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 9–12 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – la Révolution française

**Prerequisites:** Everyday life — the three estates (the unfairness that caused it).

### Key concepts
- In **1789**, the people of France — angry at unfairness, heavy taxes and hunger — rose up against the all-powerful king (**Louis XVI**) in the **French Revolution**.
- On **14 July 1789**, a Paris crowd stormed the **Bastille**, a royal fortress-prison that stood for the king's harsh power. Its fall became the great **symbol** of the people's power — and is now France's **national day** (le 14 juillet).
- The Revolution ended the old system where one king held all the power, and put forward a new idea: that **the people**, the citizens, should have a say. (Handle the violence sensitively: there was fear and fighting, and people including the king were later killed.)

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Ancien Régime was unfair (three estates), and that Louis-style kings held "absolute" power.

### Teach it (the explanation)
By **1789**, France was like a pot about to boil over. Remember the unfairness: a tiny group of nobles and clergy had wealth and paid little tax, while the huge **Third Estate** did the work, paid the taxes — and now, after bad harvests, couldn't even afford **bread**. The king, **Louis XVI**, still held almost **all** the power, like the Sun King before him. People had had enough. This is the **French Revolution** — when the people stood up to change who held the power.

The most famous moment came on **14 July 1789**. An angry crowd in Paris marched to the **Bastille** — a grim old royal **fortress-prison** with thick towers. To the people, the Bastille stood for the **king's harsh, unfair power**: a place where the king could lock people up. The crowd attacked, and after a tense, frightening struggle, the Bastille **fell**. (Keep this gentle: there *was* fighting and people were hurt — we don't need the gory details. The big point is what it *meant*.)

What did the fall of the Bastille **mean**? It showed, for the first time, that **ordinary people together** could stand up to the king — that power didn't belong to one royal family alone. It became the great **symbol** of the Revolution. That's why **14 July** is celebrated every year as France's **national day** (*le 14 juillet*), with parades and fireworks — a bit like the Fourth of July in the United States.

The Revolution went on for years and had hard, frightening parts — including a time when many people, even the king himself, were put to death. We can be honest that it was a **violent and scary** time, while keeping our focus on **why** it happened (people wanted fairness, food and a voice) and on the **new ideas** it gave the world (the next lesson).

**Worked example.** "Why is the storming of the Bastille so important?" Chain it: the Bastille was a **symbol** of the king's harsh power → an angry, hungry crowd captured it on **14 July 1789** → this proved **the people**, not just the king, had power → it became *the* symbol of the Revolution → today it's France's **national day**. So a single day became the emblem of huge change.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **14 July** today — fireworks and parades; "do you know *why* France celebrates this date?"
- A symbol you can picture: a prison-fortress falling = the old all-powerful king's grip breaking.
- "What would *you* protest about if something felt really unfair?" (a calm way into the causes).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The Bastille was full of prisoners who were freed."
  **Why:** "stormed a prison" suggests rescuing crowds of captives.
  **Fix:** there were only **a handful** of prisoners inside. The Bastille mattered as a **symbol** of the king's power, not for who was in it.
- **Wrong idea:** "The Revolution happened for no reason / just because people were mean."
  **Why:** the violence is remembered, the causes forgotten.
  **Fix:** it had real **causes** — deep unfairness, heavy taxes, and hunger when bread ran short. People wanted **fairness and a voice**.
- **Wrong idea:** "The king (Louis XVI) was the same Louis as Versailles' Sun King."
  **Why:** the names blur.
  **Fix:** the Sun King (Louis XIV) lived **earlier**; the king of the Revolution was **Louis XVI**, a later descendant. Place them on the timeline.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What happened on 14 July 1789? **A:** A Paris crowd stormed the Bastille — a turning point that became the symbol of the French Revolution.
- **Q:** Why did the people of France rise up? **A:** Because of deep unfairness, heavy taxes and hunger — they wanted fairness and a voice, not rule by one all-powerful king.
- **Q:** Why is 14 July special in France today? **A:** It's the national day, celebrating the storming of the Bastille (the start of the Revolution).
- **Q:** What did the Bastille stand for? **A:** The king's harsh, unfair power — that's why its fall was such a powerful symbol.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + multipleChoice.* Order: unfair three estates → hunger and high bread prices → people rise up → storming of the Bastille (14 July 1789). *MultipleChoice*: "The Bastille mattered most because it was…" (full of prisoners / a symbol of the king's power) → a symbol. *Speak*: "Explain why 14 July is celebrated in France."

## 12. Liberty, Equality, Fraternity — what the Revolution changed

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 9–12 · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – la Révolution française (les idées)

**Prerequisites:** The French Revolution — 1789 and the Bastille.

### Key concepts
- The Revolution gave France (and the world) powerful new **ideas**, summed up in three words: **Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité** — **Liberty, Equality, Fraternity** (freedom, fairness, and looking after one another like brothers and sisters).
- It declared that people are **citizens** with **rights**, not just "subjects" who must obey a king — written down in the *Declaration of the Rights of Man*.
- It **ended absolute monarchy**: France would no longer be ruled by an all-powerful king alone. These ideas still shape France today — the motto is written on town halls and on coins.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Revolution began in 1789 because the old system was unfair, and that the king held absolute power.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The Revolution wasn't only about *fighting* — its most lasting gift was a set of **ideas** that changed the world. The French summed them up in three beautiful words you can still see carved on town halls all over France today:

- **Liberté** — **Liberty**: people should be **free** (to speak, to think, to live their lives), not simply ordered about by a king.
- **Égalité** — **Equality**: people should be treated **fairly and equally** — no more nobles with special privileges paying no tax while the poor pay everything.
- **Fraternité** — **Fraternity**: people should care for **one another** like brothers and sisters, as one community.

The revolutionaries wrote a famous document, the **Declaration of the Rights of Man (and of the Citizen)**, saying that every person has **rights**. This flipped the old idea upside down. Before, you were a **subject** — someone who simply **obeyed** the king. Now you were a **citizen** — someone with **rights and a say** in your country. That change, from *subject* to *citizen*, is one of the biggest ideas in all of history.

The Revolution also **ended absolute monarchy**: never again would one all-powerful king rule France alone with no limits. The road afterwards was bumpy — there were violent, frightening years, and even Napoleon would later make himself emperor — but the **ideas** survived. Today, **Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité** is the official **motto of France**, printed on coins and stamped on public buildings. The Revolution's biggest victory was an idea: that a country belongs to its **people**.

**Worked example.** "What changed from 'subject' to 'citizen'?" Compare: a **subject** must simply **obey** the king and has no say; a **citizen** has **rights** and a **voice** in the country. The Revolution declared everyone a **citizen** with rights → so power belongs to the **people**, not only the king. That's the heart of what changed.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Spot the motto: *Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité* really is on French coins, stamps and town halls — find it in a photo.
- Three words, three ideas: act each one out — being free, sharing fairly, helping each other.
- "Subject vs citizen": would you rather simply obey, or have rights and a say? Why?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "After the Revolution everything was instantly free and fair for everyone."
  **Why:** the slogan sounds like a happy ending.
  **Fix:** the **ideas** were powerful, but real life changed **slowly** and the years right after were violent and unstable. Ideals can take a long time to fully arrive (e.g. women and many others still lacked full rights).
- **Wrong idea:** "*Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité* is just an old slogan with no meaning now."
  **Why:** it sounds like history.
  **Fix:** it's France's **official motto today**, on coins, stamps and town halls — still central to French identity.
- **Wrong idea:** "The Revolution made Napoleon king right away."
  **Why:** the order of events is fuzzy.
  **Fix:** the Revolution first ended the old monarchy and spread its ideas; **Napoleon** came **after**, and made himself **Emperor** (next lesson) — not king.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What are the three words of the French Revolution's motto, in English? **A:** Liberty, Equality, Fraternity (Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité).
- **Q:** What's the difference between a "subject" and a "citizen"? **A:** A subject just obeys the king; a citizen has rights and a say in the country.
- **Q:** Name one lasting change the Revolution made. **A:** It ended absolute monarchy / declared people are citizens with rights / gave France its motto Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs + speak.* Match each French word to its English meaning and an action: Liberté → Liberty (being free); Égalité → Equality (fair and equal); Fraternité → Fraternity (caring for each other). *Speak*: "Pick one of the three words and say why it matters to you."

## 13. Napoleon (a brief, balanced look)

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 9–12 · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – le XIXᵉ siècle (Napoléon)

**Prerequisites:** Liberty, Equality, Fraternity (the Revolution and its aftermath).

### Key concepts
- **Napoleon Bonaparte** rose to power after the Revolution and in **1804 crowned himself Emperor** of the French — **not a king** born royal, but a soldier who reached the very top.
- He was a **brilliant general** who won many famous battles, *and* a **reformer** who left lasting things in France: a clear set of laws (the **Code Napoléon**), better **schools**, and France's exam, the **baccalauréat**.
- A **balanced** view: he did great and lasting things, *but* his many **wars** caused huge loss of life across Europe, and he was finally defeated (at **Waterloo**, 1815). He was neither a simple hero nor a simple villain.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Revolution ended absolute monarchy and gave France new ideas; understands "general," "law," and "emperor vs king."

### Teach it (the explanation)
After the stormy years of the Revolution, France needed a strong leader — and one remarkable man rose all the way to the top: **Napoleon Bonaparte**. He started as an **army officer** and became such a successful **general** that, in **1804**, he did something astonishing: he **crowned himself Emperor** of the French.

Here's a point worth getting right: Napoleon was **not a king**. Kings were born royal and inherited their crown (like Louis XIV). Napoleon was an ordinary man who **earned and seized** power and made *himself* **Emperor** — a different title and a very different story. In a way, that was a Revolution-era idea too: that a person could rise by **ability**, not just by birth.

Napoleon did two kinds of things, and a fair history holds **both** in mind:

**The lasting, helpful things.** He gathered France's tangled laws into one clear set, the **Code Napoléon (Code Civil)** — many of those laws still shape France (and other countries) today. He improved **schools** and created the **baccalauréat**, the big exam French students still take. He organised banks, roads and government.

**The hard, costly things.** Napoleon was also a relentless **conqueror**. He fought many **wars** across Europe to expand his empire, and those wars cost a **huge number of lives**. In the end, the other countries united against him, and he was finally **defeated at Waterloo in 1815**, and sent away to a faraway island.

So how should we remember Napoleon? **Honestly and in balance**: a brilliant general and reformer who changed France for good in some ways — *and* a conqueror whose wars brought terrible suffering. Not a simple hero, not a simple villain — a powerful, complicated figure.

**Worked example.** "Was Napoleon a king?" No — reason it: kings were **born royal** and inherited the crown; Napoleon was an **army officer** who **rose by ability** and **crowned himself Emperor** (1804). So he's an **Emperor**, not a king — an important difference.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- The **baccalauréat** ("le bac") that French teenagers still take — Napoleon started it.
- "From soldier to emperor" — you don't have to be born royal to reach the top (a Revolution-era idea).
- Balanced thinking: practise holding *two* true things at once — great reformer **and** costly conqueror.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Napoleon was a king."
  **Why:** he ruled France grandly, so "king" feels right.
  **Fix:** he was an **Emperor** who **crowned himself** in 1804 — not born royal. Emperor ≠ king.
- **Wrong idea:** "Napoleon was simply a great hero" (or "simply a villain").
  **Why:** stories like a clear good guy or bad guy.
  **Fix:** be **balanced** — he made lasting reforms (laws, schools) **and** his wars cost huge numbers of lives. Both are true.
- **Wrong idea:** "Napoleon lived in ancient/medieval times."
  **Why:** all "history with battles" blurs.
  **Fix:** he came **after the Revolution** — Emperor in **1804**, defeated **1815**. Place him on the modern part of the timeline.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Was Napoleon a king or an emperor — and how is that different? **A:** An **Emperor** (he crowned himself in 1804); kings were born royal and inherited the throne, but Napoleon rose by ability.
- **Q:** Name one lasting thing Napoleon left France. **A:** The Code Napoléon (laws) / improved schools / the baccalauréat exam.
- **Q:** Give a *balanced* view of Napoleon. **A:** A brilliant general and reformer **and** a conqueror whose many wars cost huge numbers of lives (defeated at Waterloo, 1815).

### Activity / practice
*Categorize + speak.* Sort facts into "lasting good things" (Code of laws, schools, the baccalauréat) vs "costly things" (many wars, huge loss of life, defeat at Waterloo). *Speak*: "Was Napoleon a hero or a villain? Give one reason for *each* side." (practises balance).

- **Quelle Histoire — "Napoléon"** (children's history, FR) — his life for kids.
- **Lumni (francetv) — Napoléon Bonaparte** — short curriculum clips.

## 14. Modern France and its symbols

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 5–12 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – la République et ses symboles

**Prerequisites:** ideally the Revolution topics (many symbols come from it); but also works as a friendly **first** topic for young children.

### Key concepts
- Today France is a **Republic** (led by an elected **President**, not a king), and it has well-loved **symbols** that come mostly from the Revolution and stand for the country.
- The big ones: the **tricolore** (blue-white-red flag); **Marianne** (a woman who stands for the Republic and Liberty, with her red cap); **La Marseillaise** (the national anthem); **le 14 juillet** (the national day); and the motto **Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité**.
- The **Eiffel Tower** (built **1889** in Paris) became the most famous symbol of France around the world — and the **Louvre**, **Notre-Dame** and great food and language make French culture beloved.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- For older children: knows the Revolution gave France new ideas (Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité). For younger children: just recognises a flag and the Eiffel Tower.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Let's land in **France today**. France is no longer ruled by a king or an emperor — it is a **Republic**, led by a **President** that the people **elect** (vote for). And like every country, France has special **symbols** that say "this is us." Most of them were born in the **Revolution** — so now you'll understand *why* they exist.

- **The tricolore flag** — three stripes: **blue, white, red** (*bleu, blanc, rouge*). White was the old colour of the **king**; blue and red were the colours of **Paris**. Joining them stood for the people and the country united. You see it everywhere in France.
- **Marianne** — a figure of a **woman** who stands for the **Republic** and for **Liberty**, usually shown wearing a soft red **Phrygian cap**. Her face is on stamps and coins, and a statue or bust of her stands in every French town hall.
- **La Marseillaise** — the **national anthem**, a stirring song from the time of the Revolution, sung at events and football matches.
- **Le 14 juillet** — the **national day** (remember the Bastille!), with parades and fireworks every year.
- **Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité** — the **motto**, on town halls and coins.

And then there's the symbol the whole world knows: the **Eiffel Tower** (*la Tour Eiffel*). Built in **Paris in 1889** for a great world fair, it was meant to be **temporary** — some people thought it ugly and wanted it taken down! Instead it became the most famous landmark in France, sparkling over Paris at night. Add the **Louvre** (home of the Mona Lisa), the rebuilt **Notre-Dame** cathedral, and France's famous food and language, and you can see why French culture is loved around the world.

So the story comes full circle: from cave painters and Gauls, through kings and a Revolution, to a modern **Republic** whose flag, anthem and motto still carry the ideas people fought for — and an iron tower that says "France" to the whole planet.

**Worked example.** "Where do France's symbols come from?" Connect them back: the **tricolore**, **Marianne**, **La Marseillaise**, **14 juillet** and the **motto** all come from the **Revolution** (1789 onwards), when France became about its **people**. The **Eiffel Tower** came later (1889) as a symbol of a modern, inventive France. So the symbols tell the *story* of the country.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Recognise it: show the tricolore and the Eiffel Tower — most children already know these; now learn the story behind them.
- The Eiffel Tower was almost torn down — "would you have kept it?"
- Marianne is on coins and stamps and in every town hall — a hidden figure to spot.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "France still has a king/queen."
  **Why:** other countries do; old France had famous kings.
  **Fix:** France is a **Republic** with an **elected President** — the Revolution ended rule by kings.
- **Wrong idea:** "Marianne was a real famous queen."
  **Why:** she looks like a specific person.
  **Fix:** Marianne is a **symbol** — an imagined figure standing for the **Republic and Liberty**, not a real ruler.
- **Wrong idea:** "The Eiffel Tower has always been loved and was built to last forever."
  **Why:** it's so iconic now.
  **Fix:** it was built for the **1889** fair, meant to be **temporary**, and some people disliked it — it was *kept* because it became so famous (and useful).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What kind of country is France today, and who leads it? **A:** A Republic, led by an elected President (not a king).
- **Q:** Name three symbols of France and say what one of them is. **A:** Any three of: tricolore (flag), Marianne (figure of the Republic/Liberty), La Marseillaise (anthem), 14 juillet (national day), the motto, the Eiffel Tower — plus a correct description of one.
- **Q:** When and why was the Eiffel Tower built? **A:** In Paris in 1889 for a world fair; it was meant to be temporary but became France's most famous symbol.
- **Q:** Which colours are on the French flag, and what's it called? **A:** Blue, white and red — the *tricolore*.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs + speak.* Match each symbol to what it is: tricolore → the flag; Marianne → figure of the Republic/Liberty; La Marseillaise → the anthem; 14 juillet → national day; Eiffel Tower → famous Paris landmark (1889). *Speak*: "Which symbol of France is your favourite, and why?" Optionally connect each symbol back to the Revolution.
