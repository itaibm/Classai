# French History — Consolidated Misconceptions

The recurring wrong ideas children hold when learning the story of France, gathered in one place with **why** they happen and the **fix**. These feed `LessonAnalysis.misconceptions` and `BeatCheck.wrongAnswers`. Each topic block in `french-history.md` lists its own topic-specific misconceptions too; this file collects the *cross-cutting* ones a tutor should expect again and again.

Maps to `subjectKey: history`.

---

## A. Thinking about time & order

### 1. "The past was all kings (and battles)."
- **Why:** history stories spotlight rulers and wars, so children picture only crowns, knights and famous men.
- **Fix:** at every period, bring in **ordinary people** — a Gaulish farmer, a Gallo-Roman bath-goer, a medieval **peasant** (about 9 in 10 people!), a Paris market-woman in 1789. Ask "what did a normal *child* do all day here?" Most of France's people were never kings or knights.

### 2. Muddling Gauls / Romans / Franks (the "ancient blur").
- **Why:** several "long-ago peoples with no modern clothes" feel interchangeable.
- **Fix:** drill the order on a **physical timeline**: **Gauls** (Celtic tribes) → conquered by **Rome** (Caesar, 52 BC) → **Gallo-Romans** (a blend) → after Rome falls, the **Franks** (Clovis ~500). They came one after another; the Romans were *outsiders* who conquered the Gauls; the Franks came *later*, after Rome.

### 3. "France was always called France / always this shape."
- **Why:** today's map and name feel timeless.
- **Fix:** the land was **Gaul**, then **Roman Gaul**, and only became *Francia* — "land of the **Franks**" — after Clovis (~500). Borders and the name **changed with the people**.

### 4. "BC dates work like normal counting" (e.g. 10 BC is older than 52 BC).
- **Why:** bigger numbers usually mean later.
- **Fix:** BC years count **down** toward zero, so **52 BC is older than 10 BC**; AD years count **up**. Practise the French spine on a number line: 52 BC → AD 500 → 1429 → 1789 → 1889.

### 5. Mixing up the famous rulers (which Louis? king or emperor?).
- **Why:** "Louis," "king" and "emperor" all blur into one grand French ruler.
- **Fix:** pin three apart on the timeline: **Louis XIV** = the Sun King (Versailles, 1600s, a **king**); **Louis XVI** = the king of the **Revolution** (1789); **Napoleon** = came **after** the Revolution and made himself **Emperor** (1804) — *not* a king.

## B. Antiquity (Gauls & Roman Gaul)

### 6. "Asterix is what the Gauls were really like."
- **Why:** the comic is most children's only image of the Gauls.
- **Fix:** Asterix is a fun cartoon (there was no **magic potion**!). The real Gauls were genuine **Celtic** farmers, metalworkers and warriors — brave but **divided into many tribes**, which is why Rome could conquer them.

### 7. "The Gauls were one united country."
- **Why:** modern France is one nation.
- **Fix:** Gaul was **many separate tribes** that often fought each other. **Vercingetorix** finally united many of them against Caesar — but too late, and he lost at **Alesia (52 BC)**.

### 8. "The Romans killed all the Gauls and replaced them."
- **Why:** "conquered" sounds like "wiped out."
- **Fix:** the two peoples **blended** into **Gallo-Romans**. The Gauls took on Roman ways and the **Latin** language (which became **French**) — they didn't disappear.

## C. The Middle Ages (Franks → Joan of Arc)

### 9. "Everyone in the Middle Ages was a king, knight or princess."
- **Why:** films and fairy tales only show the famous few.
- **Fix:** the **vast majority were peasants** — farmers doing hard daily work. Knights and nobles were a small minority. (Sets up the unfairness behind the Revolution.)

### 10. "Castles and Renaissance châteaux are the same."
- **Why:** both are called *château* / "castle."
- **Fix:** a **medieval castle** was first a **fortress for defence** (thick walls, towers, moat); a **Renaissance château** like **Chambord** was built for **beauty and pleasure** (a palace). Same word, different purpose and time.

### 11. "Joan of Arc was a queen / noble lady, and lived in ancient times."
- **Why:** leaders in stories are usually royal; all "armour history" blurs.
- **Fix:** she was a **teenage peasant girl** (that's what makes her amazing), and she lived in the **Middle Ages (1400s)** during the war between **France and England** — long after the Romans.

## D. Kings & the Renaissance

### 12. "The Renaissance was a person or a war."
- **Why:** most history words children meet are people or battles.
- **Fix:** the Renaissance is a **time of new art, learning and building** (a "rebirth," spreading from **Italy**) — a change in *ideas*, not a ruler or a fight.

### 13. "Leonardo da Vinci was French."
- **Why:** he died in France and the Mona Lisa is in the Louvre.
- **Fix:** he was **Italian**, but **invited** to live in France by **François I** — which is why his **Mona Lisa** ended up in Paris.

### 14. "A king back then had to obey laws and a parliament, like leaders today."
- **Why:** we judge by modern government.
- **Fix:** **Louis XIV** was an **absolute** monarch — almost **no limits** on his power, answerable only to God ("*L'État, c'est moi*"). That very unfairness helped *cause* the Revolution.

## E. The Revolution & Napoleon (handle sensitively)

### 15. "The Revolution happened for no reason / just because people were mean."
- **Why:** the violence is remembered; the causes are forgotten.
- **Fix:** it had real **causes** — a deeply **unfair** society (the **three estates**), heavy **taxes** on the poor, and **hunger** when bread ran short. People wanted **fairness, food and a voice**. (Mention the conflict honestly, without graphic detail.)

### 16. "The Bastille was full of prisoners who were freed."
- **Why:** "stormed a prison" suggests rescuing crowds of captives.
- **Fix:** there were only **a handful** of prisoners inside on 14 July 1789. The Bastille mattered as a **symbol** of the king's harsh power — not for who was held there.

### 17. "After the Revolution, everything was instantly free and fair for everyone."
- **Why:** the motto *Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité* sounds like a happy ending.
- **Fix:** the **ideas** were powerful, but real change came **slowly**, and the years right after were unstable and frightening. Ideals can take a long time to fully arrive.

### 18. "Napoleon was a king" (or "a simple hero / a simple villain").
- **Why:** he ruled grandly (so "king" feels right); stories like a clear good/bad guy.
- **Fix:** he was an **Emperor** who **crowned himself** (1804) — not born royal. And keep it **balanced**: a brilliant general and reformer (**Code Napoléon**, schools, the **baccalauréat**) **and** a conqueror whose **wars cost huge numbers of lives** (defeated at Waterloo, 1815).

## F. Modern France & symbols

### 19. "France still has a king or queen."
- **Why:** some countries do; old France had famous kings.
- **Fix:** France is a **Republic** with an **elected President** — the Revolution ended rule by kings.

### 20. "Marianne was a real famous queen."
- **Why:** she looks like a specific person.
- **Fix:** Marianne is a **symbol** — an imagined woman standing for the **Republic and Liberty** (with her red Phrygian cap), seen in every town hall. Not a real ruler.

### 21. "The Eiffel Tower was always loved and built to last forever."
- **Why:** it's so iconic now.
- **Fix:** it was built for the **1889** world fair, meant to be **temporary**, and some people disliked it at first — it was *kept* because it became so famous.
