# French History (Histoire de France) — Subject Overview

A chronological journey through the history of France for children **ages 5–12**, written in **English**. It broadly follows the sequence French primary schools use in *histoire* (cycle 2 / cycle 3): from the painted caves of prehistory, through the Gauls, Roman Gaul, the Franks and the Middle Ages, into the kings, the Revolution, Napoleon, and the symbols of the modern Republic.

- **Maps to Classai `subjectKey`:** `history`
- **Language of instruction:** English (about France).
- **Age range:** 5–12 (lower-primary touch through upper-primary depth).
- **Curriculum reference:** the French national *programme d'histoire* for the primary cycles (*la Préhistoire, l'Antiquité — les Gaulois et la Gaule romaine, le Moyen Âge, les Temps modernes — la Renaissance et l'Ancien Régime, la Révolution française et le XIXᵉ siècle, la République et ses symboles*), retold for an English-speaking learner.

This subject sits alongside the UK-curriculum `history-geography` folder. Where that folder tells the British story (Romans *in Britain*, Anglo-Saxons, Vikings), this folder tells the **French** story — and the two rhyme: the same Roman Empire, the same medieval centuries, the same Renaissance, so a child studying both can compare "what was happening here vs there."

## How to teach French history to children

History is not "facts to memorise" — it's a set of **thinking skills** practised on a fascinating story. Across every topic, keep these five disciplines turning. (They mirror the French primary aims: *se repérer dans le temps, raisonner, s'informer, comprendre un document, pratiquer différents langages*.)

### 1. Chronology — keep a timeline on the wall
The single biggest difficulty for young children is **order**: they muddle Gauls, Romans and Franks into one fuzzy "long ago." Fix it with a **long, physical timeline** you return to every lesson, adding each new period as a card. Use clear anchors:
- **Prehistory** = *before writing* (Lascaux, ~17,000 years ago).
- **Antiquity** = Gauls, then **Roman Gaul** (Caesar conquers Gaul, 52 BC).
- **Middle Ages** = Clovis (~500) → Charlemagne (800) → castles & cathedrals → Joan of Arc (1429).
- **Modern times** = Renaissance (1500s) → Louis XIV & Versailles (1600s) → **Revolution 1789** → Napoleon (1804).
- **The Republic** = the tricolore, Marianne, the Eiffel Tower (1889), today.

Teach **BC/AD** gently (years before and after a fixed point; BC counts *down*). Centuries confuse children — "the 1700s" is friendlier than "the 18th century," so say both.

### 2. Cause & consequence — always ask "why?" and "so what?"
Don't stop at *what* happened; chain it. *Why* did the Gauls lose to Rome (one united Roman army vs. quarrelling tribes)? *So what* followed the storming of the Bastille (the end of the old all-powerful king; the start of "citizens," not "subjects")? Children remember a **chain of dominoes** far better than an isolated date.

### 3. Sources & evidence — "how do we know?"
Every topic has a "detective" angle. Prehistory has **no writing**, so we read cave paintings and bones (archaeology). The Gauls left few records, so much of what we "know" comes from their *enemy* Caesar — a reminder that sources can be **one-sided**. Versailles and the Revolution left letters, paintings and newspapers. Keep asking children *where the knowledge comes from* and whether the source might be biased.

### 4. Significance — "why do we still talk about this?"
Some things matter because they **changed everything afterwards** or because France still celebrates them. Vercingetorix and Joan of Arc became national symbols; 14 July is the national day; *Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité* is written on every town hall. Help children see the **thread from then to now**.

### 5. Link to French language & culture
This subject is a natural bridge to French. Drop in the real French words children will meet again: *château* (castle), *chevalier* (knight), *roi* (king), *liberté* (liberty), *drapeau* (flag), *quatorze juillet* (14 July). Tie people and places to things a child might already half-know — **Asterix** (the Gauls), the **Eiffel Tower**, **Notre-Dame**, **Versailles**, the **Mona Lisa** (which lives in the Louvre because of François I). Culture makes the history feel real and present.

### Tone & sensitivity
Keep it **age-appropriate and balanced**:
- Treat the **Revolution's violence** honestly but without graphic detail — name that there was conflict, fear and that people including the king were killed, while keeping the focus on *why people wanted change* (fairness, food, a voice) and the lasting ideas (liberty, equality, fraternity, citizenship).
- Keep **Napoleon balanced and brief**: brilliant general and reformer (the *Code*, schools, the *baccalauréat*), *and* a conqueror whose wars cost huge numbers of lives — not a simple hero.
- Resist "the past was all kings and battles." Always include **ordinary people** — what a Gaulish farmer, a medieval peasant, or a Paris market-woman actually did all day.

## Scope & sequence

The 14 topics in `french-history.md`, in chronological order. (Younger children, ages 5–7, can do the **bold-starred** topics as simple stories; the full set suits ages 8–12.)

| # | Topic | Rough date | Strand |
|---|-------|-----------|--------|
| 1 | ★ Prehistoric France — the cave painters of Lascaux | ~17,000 yrs ago | Prehistory |
| 2 | ★ The Gauls and Vercingetorix | ~ 800–52 BC | Antiquity |
| 3 | Roman Gaul (la Gaule romaine) | 52 BC – AD 400s | Antiquity |
| 4 | Clovis and the Franks | ~AD 500 | Early Middle Ages |
| 5 | Charlemagne, the great emperor | AD 800 | Early Middle Ages |
| 6 | ★ Medieval France — castles, knights & cathedrals | ~1000–1300 | Middle Ages |
| 7 | Joan of Arc (Jeanne d'Arc) | 1412–1431 | Late Middle Ages |
| 8 | Kings & the Renaissance — François I and the Loire châteaux | 1500s | Modern times |
| 9 | Louis XIV, the Sun King, and Versailles | 1643–1715 | Ancien Régime |
| 10 | Everyday life long ago — the three "estates" | 1600s–1700s | Ancien Régime |
| 11 | The French Revolution — 1789 and the Bastille | 1789 | Revolution |
| 12 | Liberty, Equality, Fraternity — what the Revolution changed | 1789–1799 | Revolution |
| 13 | Napoleon (a brief, balanced look) | 1799–1815 | 19th century |
| 14 | Modern France and its symbols (tricolore, Marianne, Marseillaise, 14 Juillet, Eiffel Tower) | 1789–today | The Republic |

**Sequencing notes**
- Topics 1–7 are the **chronological spine** every child needs, in order, so the timeline stays straight.
- Topics 8–10 can be taught as a cluster ("the age of kings"), with 10 (everyday life) as the human, ground-level view that sets up *why* the Revolution happened.
- Topics 11–14 form the **road to the modern Republic**: 11 (the event) → 12 (the ideas) → 13 (Napoleon) → 14 (the symbols we see today). Topic 14 is also a lovely **first** lesson for very young children — start from the flag and the Eiffel Tower they recognise, then later fill in the story behind them.
