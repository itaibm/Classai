# Ancient Israel — Heritage & History (Patriarchs to the Hasmoneans)

A roughly **chronological** journey through the ancient story of the Jewish people and the Land of Israel, for ages 5–12. It is told as **heritage** — the story a people remembers about itself — and, where we can, alongside **what archaeology and old writings have found**. Keep a long timeline on the wall and place each topic on it. Use **BCE/CE** (same as BC/AD): bigger BCE numbers are *older*.

> **Honesty rule for this whole file.** For the earliest topics (Patriarchs, Exodus) our source is mainly the **Tanakh and tradition** — beautiful, meaningful heritage narratives that scholars debate as documented history. For the later topics (kings, Temples, Maccabees) there is also **physical and written evidence**. Tell the story respectfully; when a child asks "is it *true*?", answer honestly at their level: *"This is the story our tradition tells, and here is what we've also found."*

## 1. The Land of Israel and its place in the story

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 5–8 · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Heritage – land & belonging

**Prerequisites:** none (the starting point).

### Key concepts
- The story of the Jewish people is tied to one small **land** at the meeting-point of three continents, between the **Mediterranean Sea** and the **Jordan River** — called the **Land of Israel** (*Eretz Yisrael*).
- It is a land of great variety for its size: green hills and the **Galilee** in the north, the dry **Negev** desert in the south, the **Jordan River** and the very salty **Dead Sea** (the lowest place on Earth's land).
- This land is **dear and holy to more than one people** — Jews, and also Arabs (Muslim, Christian, Druze) — an honest, important point to hold from the start.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows what a map and a country are; that different places have different land and weather.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Every people has a place it calls home in its stories. For the Jewish people, that place is the **Land of Israel** — a small strip of land beside the **Mediterranean Sea**, in the part of the world we call the Middle East. It sits where three continents almost meet, so for thousands of years travellers, traders and armies passed through it.

For such a little country, the land is full of variety. In the **north** are green hills, the **Galilee** and the freshwater **Sea of Galilee**. Down the middle runs the **Jordan River**. In the **south** stretches the **Negev**, a dry desert. And in a deep crack in the earth lies the **Dead Sea** — so salty you float without trying, and the **lowest dry land on the whole planet**.

This land is **special and beloved to more than one people**. It is the heartland of the Jewish story — but Arabs (Muslim, Christian and Druze) also live here and hold it dear, and the city of **Jerusalem** is holy to Jews, Christians and Muslims alike. Holding that in mind from the start keeps the story honest and kind.

**Worked example.** "Why did so many peoples pass through this land?" Look at a map: it sits **between the sea and the desert**, on the bridge between Africa, Asia and Europe → so traders and armies travelled through it → so many peoples have lived in and loved it over thousands of years.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- The **Dead Sea**: you float like a cork — show a photo; "why so salty?" (no river carries the water out, so it stays and dries).
- Trace the **Jordan River** on a map from the Galilee down to the Dead Sea.
- Food that grows here — olives, dates, figs, pomegranates, grapes (the "seven species").

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Israel is huge, like a big country." **Why:** it looms large in stories and news. **Fix:** it's **small** — about the size of Wales or New Jersey — but packed with hills, desert, river and sea.
- **Wrong idea:** "Only one people has ever lived there." **Why:** simplifying the story. **Fix:** many peoples have lived in the land over time; today it is home to **Jews and Arabs** together, and is dear to more than one people.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What sea is the Land of Israel beside, and what big river runs through it? **A:** The Mediterranean Sea; the Jordan River.
- **Q:** Name one thing special about the Dead Sea. **A:** It's the lowest dry land on Earth, and so salty you float in it.
- **Q:** Is the Land of Israel home to only one people? **A:** No — it is home to Jews and Arabs, and is dear and holy to more than one people.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs / labelling.* On a simple map, match labels to places: Mediterranean Sea, Jordan River, Galilee (north/green), Negev (south/desert), Dead Sea (lowest, saltiest). Then a *speak* prompt: "Describe the Land of Israel to someone who's never seen it."

## 2. Abraham and the Patriarchs (the heritage of the family)

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 5–9 · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Heritage – founding narrative

**Prerequisites:** The Land of Israel and its place in the story.

### Key concepts
- Jewish tradition begins the people's story with **Abraham** (*Avraham*), who, the Tanakh tells, left his home to journey to the Land of Israel — remembered as the first to follow **one God**.
- The **Patriarchs and Matriarchs** — Abraham & Sarah, Isaac & Rebecca, Jacob & Leah and Rachel — are the founding **family** of the Jewish people. Jacob is also called **Israel**, which gives the people and land their name.
- This is a **heritage narrative** from tradition: deeply meaningful, and the start of the people's memory of itself — told as the family's story, not as a dated, documented history.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows what a family tree is; that stories can carry a people's beliefs and memory.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Where does a people's story begin? Jewish tradition begins with one man and his family. In the Tanakh, **Abraham** lived far away, and felt called to **leave his home and travel to the Land of Israel**, trusting in **one God** at a time when most people worshipped many. For this he is remembered as the father of the Jewish people (and honoured too by Christians and Muslims).

Abraham and his wife **Sarah** had a son, **Isaac**; Isaac and **Rebecca** had **Jacob**; Jacob had a large family of sons and daughters. Jacob was given a second name — **Israel** — and *that* is where the names "the people of **Israel**" and the "**Land of Israel**" come from. Together, Abraham, Isaac and Jacob (with Sarah, Rebecca, Leah and Rachel) are the **Patriarchs and Matriarchs**: the founding family the whole people looks back to.

Is this documented, dated history? Here we are honest: this is the **story tradition tells** — a heritage narrative, very old and very precious, that scholars discuss and debate. We treat it with respect as the people's founding memory, the way every nation has stories about how it began.

**Worked example.** "Why are the Jewish people sometimes called the *children of Israel*?" Trace it: Jacob was renamed **Israel** → his children and their descendants became "the children of Israel" → that became the name of the **people** and the **land**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A **family tree**: draw Abraham → Isaac → Jacob → his children; "this is the family the whole story starts with."
- "Where does your family's story start?" — connect to the child's own grandparents and great-grandparents.
- The name game: Jacob/Israel — many people in the story have two names.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Abraham is proven by archaeology like a king on a coin." **Why:** mixing tradition with documented evidence. **Fix:** the Patriarchs come to us through **tradition/the Tanakh** as a heritage narrative; we treasure the story while being honest it isn't dated, dug-up history.
- **Wrong idea:** "Israel was always the name of the people." **Why:** the name feels timeless. **Fix:** the name comes from **Jacob**, who was also called **Israel** — *that's* the origin.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Who does Jewish tradition remember as the first father of the Jewish people? **A:** Abraham (Avraham).
- **Q:** Where do the names "people of Israel" and "Land of Israel" come from? **A:** From Jacob, who was also called Israel.
- **Q:** Is the story of Abraham documented history or heritage tradition? **A:** It is a heritage narrative from tradition/the Tanakh — precious, but not dated archaeology.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering / fillBlank.* Order the founding family: Abraham → Isaac → Jacob. Fill the blank: "Jacob was also called ___, which gives the people and land their name." (Israel).

- **BimBam — "Genesis" / Torah story animations** (Abraham and the founding family, child-friendly).
- **PJ Library — story books on Abraham and the Patriarchs** (read-aloud heritage stories).

## 3. The Exodus and Moses (the story of freedom)

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 5–9 · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Heritage – the Exodus narrative

**Prerequisites:** Abraham and the Patriarchs.

### Key concepts
- Tradition tells that Jacob's family went down to **Egypt**, grew into a people, and became **slaves** — until **Moses** (*Moshe*) led them to **freedom**: the **Exodus** (*Yetziat Mitzrayim*), "the going-out from Egypt."
- The Exodus is the great story of **freedom from slavery**, remembered every year at **Pesach (Passover)** — the most retold story in Jewish heritage.
- Tradition says that after leaving Egypt the people received the **Torah** at **Mount Sinai** (remembered at **Shavuot**) and journeyed forty years toward the Land of Israel.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows what slavery and freedom mean (gently); knows the family of Jacob/Israel from the previous topic.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The founding family grew. Tradition tells that Jacob's children went down to **Egypt** during a famine, and over generations became a whole **people** — but a new Egyptian king (**Pharaoh**) made them **slaves**, forcing them to build for him. The people suffered for a long time.

Then comes the hero of the story: **Moses** (*Moshe*). The Tanakh tells how Moses, helped by his brother Aaron and sister Miriam, stood up to Pharaoh and demanded "**Let my people go.**" After great struggles, the people finally walked out of Egypt to **freedom** — the **Exodus**, the going-out from Egypt. The most famous moment is the **crossing of the sea**, when the waters parted and the people crossed to safety.

In the wilderness, tradition says, the people came to **Mount Sinai**, where they received the **Torah** — the teaching and laws that would guide them — and then journeyed for **forty years** toward the Land of Israel. This whole story of freedom is so central that Jews retell it **every single year** at the festival of **Pesach (Passover)**, and remember the giving of the Torah at **Shavuot**.

**Worked example.** "Why do Jewish families retell the Exodus every year at Pesach?" Because it is the people's great memory of going **from slavery to freedom** → each year the family *re-lives* it at the seder so the story is never forgotten and freedom is treasured.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "**Let my people go**" — the most famous line; the courage of standing up to a powerful king.
- The Pesach **seder plate** — each food remembers part of the story (link to the festivals file).
- The crossing of the sea as the dramatic high point — picture the walls of water.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The Exodus is proven, dated history like the Romans." **Why:** confusing tradition with documented archaeology. **Fix:** it is the great **heritage narrative** of freedom from the Tanakh — central and meaningful — and scholars debate it as documented history; we tell it respectfully as the people's story.
- **Wrong idea:** "Moses was a king." **Why:** he's the leader, so "king" is assumed. **Fix:** Moses was a **leader and prophet** who led the people out of Egypt — not a king; Israel had no king until much later.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is the Exodus, and who led it? **A:** The "going-out from Egypt" — the people's escape from slavery to freedom, led by Moses.
- **Q:** Which festival retells the Exodus every year? **A:** Pesach (Passover).
- **Q:** What does tradition say the people received at Mount Sinai? **A:** The Torah (the teaching and laws), remembered at Shavuot.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering.* Put the story in order: family goes to Egypt → become slaves → Moses leads them out → cross the sea → Mount Sinai (Torah) → journey to the Land. Then a *speak* prompt: "Why is freedom worth remembering every year?"

- **BimBam — "The Exodus" / Passover story animations** (Moses, leaving Egypt, the sea).
- **PJ Library — Passover and Moses story books** (read-aloud heritage stories).

## 4. The Judges and the first Kings — Saul, David and Solomon

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 8–12 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Heritage/History – the early kingdom

**Prerequisites:** The Exodus and Moses.

### Key concepts
- After settling in the land, the people were led for a time by **Judges** (*Shoftim*) — heroes and leaders — before they asked for a **king**.
- The first three kings of Israel were **Saul** (*Shaul*), then **David** (*David*), then his son **Solomon** (*Shlomo*). David is remembered for making **Jerusalem** the capital; Solomon for building the **First Temple**.
- Here tradition and **history** begin to meet: kings, a capital and a kingdom are part of the heritage story *and* the period where archaeology and old writings start to add evidence.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Exodus story and that the people journeyed to the Land of Israel; understands "king" and "capital."

### Teach it (the explanation)
The people settled in the Land of Israel — but at first they had **no king**. Instead, in times of trouble, leaders called **Judges** (*Shoftim*) rose up to guide and rescue them. (Among them, tradition tells of the mighty **Samson** and the wise **Deborah**.) In time the people asked for a **king**, like other nations had.

The **first king** was **Saul** (*Shaul*). After him came the most famous king of all: **David** — remembered as the shepherd boy who, the story goes, defeated the giant **Goliath**, and who grew into a great king, a warrior and a writer of **psalms** (songs to God). David's biggest act for the story was capturing **Jerusalem** and making it his **capital**, the heart of the kingdom.

David's son **Solomon** (*Shlomo*) became famous for his **wisdom**, and for building the **First Temple** in Jerusalem — the great house of worship for the whole people (we meet it properly in the next topic). Under David and Solomon, tradition remembers a **golden age** when the kingdom was united and strong. From here on, the story has more and more **physical evidence** — inscriptions and ruins — alongside the tradition, so "heritage" and "documented history" travel together.

**Worked example.** "Put the first leaders in order." Trace it: **Judges** (no king yet) → King **Saul** (first king) → King **David** (made Jerusalem the capital) → King **Solomon** (built the First Temple). That's the order to remember.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **David and Goliath** — the small hero beating the giant; a story every child knows the shape of.
- David the **shepherd-poet-king** — he wrote songs (psalms) *and* led armies.
- Solomon's **wisdom** — the famous story of two mothers and one baby; "how would *you* decide?"

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Israel always had kings." **Why:** kingdoms feel like the default. **Fix:** first there were **Judges** and **no king** — kings came later, starting with **Saul**.
- **Wrong idea:** "David built the Temple." **Why:** David is the most famous king, so he gets the credit. **Fix:** David made **Jerusalem the capital**; his son **Solomon** built the **First Temple**.
- **Wrong idea:** "This is just legend, none of it is real." **Why:** over-correcting away from tradition. **Fix:** kings, Jerusalem and the kingdom are heritage *and* the period where **archaeology and old writings** add real evidence — both/and, told honestly.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Who led the people *before* they had kings? **A:** The Judges (Shoftim).
- **Q:** Name the first three kings of Israel in order. **A:** Saul, then David, then Solomon.
- **Q:** What did David do that mattered so much for the story? **A:** He made Jerusalem the capital of the kingdom.
- **Q:** What is King Solomon most famous for building? **A:** The First Temple in Jerusalem.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering / matchPairs.* Order: Judges → Saul → David → Solomon. Match king to deed: David → Jerusalem the capital / defeated Goliath; Solomon → wisdom & the First Temple; Saul → the first king.

### Resources
- **BimBam — King David / Bible story animations** (David, Goliath, the early kings).
- **BBC Bitesize — "Judaism" / Kings of Israel background** (kings and Jerusalem, for older children).

## 5. Jerusalem and the First Temple

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 8–12 · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – Jerusalem & the Temple

**Prerequisites:** The Judges and the first Kings.

### Key concepts
- **Jerusalem** (*Yerushalayim*) became the **capital** under King David and the spiritual heart of the people — a city beloved for thousands of years.
- King **Solomon** built the **First Temple** (*Beit HaMikdash*) there, around the **10th century BCE** — the central place of worship, home to the **Ark of the Covenant**.
- The First Temple stood for centuries until it was **destroyed by the Babylonians** (around **587 BCE**), and the people were taken into **exile** — a turning point in the story (and the beginning of the diaspora).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows David made Jerusalem the capital and Solomon built the Temple; can read BCE dates on a timeline.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Of all the places in the story, one city matters most: **Jerusalem**. King David made it the **capital**, and ever since it has been the heart of the Jewish people's hopes — a city sung about, prayed toward, and longed for across thousands of years. (It is also holy to Christians and Muslims, which is why it is so special and so sensitive a place.)

In Jerusalem, King **Solomon** built the **First Temple** — in Hebrew the *Beit HaMikdash*, "the Holy House" — around **3,000 years ago** (the 10th century BCE). This was *the* central place of worship for the whole people: a magnificent building that, tradition says, held the **Ark of the Covenant** (the special chest from the time of Moses). People came to the Temple for the great festivals.

The First Temple stood for **centuries** — and then, around **587 BCE**, a powerful empire, **Babylon**, conquered Jerusalem and **destroyed the Temple**, carrying many of the people away into **exile** in Babylon. This was a heartbreaking turning point. *How do we know?* Here history is on firmer ground: there are **Babylonian records and archaeology** for the conquest, even though no stone of the First Temple itself has been dug up — so we hold the tradition and the evidence together, honestly.

**Worked example.** "Why is Jerusalem so important in this story?" Chain it: David made it the **capital** → Solomon built the **Temple** there → it became the **spiritual heart** people prayed toward → so for 3,000 years Jerusalem has stood at the centre of the Jewish story (and is holy to others too).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- The **Western Wall** (Kotel) today — a surviving wall from the *later* Temple's surroundings, where people still pray; "people have prayed toward this spot for thousands of years."
- "Next year in Jerusalem" — the line Jews say at Pesach for centuries; longing for the city.
- A model or picture of the Temple — what would it have felt like to visit for a festival?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "There was only ever one Temple." **Why:** people hear "the Temple" as one thing. **Fix:** there was a **First** Temple (Solomon's, destroyed by Babylon) and later a **Second** Temple (next topics) — two, in the same holy place.
- **Wrong idea:** "Jerusalem is holy only to Jews." **Why:** focusing on one strand. **Fix:** Jerusalem is holy to **Jews, Christians and Muslims** — one reason it is so loved and so sensitive.
- **Wrong idea:** "We have the actual First Temple to look at." **Why:** assuming everything ancient survives. **Fix:** the First Temple was destroyed and **no stone of it has been found**; we know it from **tradition and the records of its destruction**.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Who built the First Temple, and in which city? **A:** King Solomon, in Jerusalem.
- **Q:** What happened to the First Temple, and who did it? **A:** It was destroyed by the Babylonians (around 587 BCE), and the people were taken into exile.
- **Q:** Is Jerusalem holy to only one people? **A:** No — it is holy to Jews, Christians and Muslims.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering / fillBlank.* Order: David makes Jerusalem the capital → Solomon builds the First Temple → Babylon destroys it → exile. Fill the blank: "The Temple in Jerusalem is called the ___ in Hebrew." (Beit HaMikdash).

### Resources
- **Israel Museum (Jerusalem) — "Model of Jerusalem in the Second Temple period"** (online images of the great model; parent-guided).
- **BBC Bitesize — "Judaism: the Temple and Jerusalem"** (the Temple's place in Jewish life).

## 6. The Prophets (voices of conscience) — a brief look

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 9–12 · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Heritage – the prophets

**Prerequisites:** Jerusalem and the First Temple.

### Key concepts
- The **Prophets** (*Nevi'im*) were people in the Tanakh who, tradition says, spoke God's message — often **reminding rulers and people to be fair, kind and just**.
- Famous prophets include **Isaiah**, **Jeremiah** and **Amos**; their words about **justice and caring for the poor** are part of Jewish heritage and have shaped ideas across the world.
- The prophets are remembered not for predicting the future like fortune-tellers, but for being the **conscience** of the people — voices for doing right.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the period of the kings and the Temple; understands "fairness" and "justice" simply.

### Teach it (the explanation)
In the time of the kings and the Temple, a special kind of person appears in the story: the **prophet** (*navi*). We often think "prophet" means someone who tells the future — but in Jewish heritage the prophets are mostly **voices of conscience**. They spoke up, even to powerful kings, to say: *be fair, look after the poor, don't cheat people, do what is right.*

Some of the most famous are **Isaiah**, **Jeremiah** and **Amos**. Their words are full of calls for **justice** and **kindness** — Isaiah's dream of a world at peace where people "**beat their swords into ploughshares**," Amos's demand that justice "**roll down like waters.**" These ideas about fairness and caring for the weak became a treasured part of Jewish heritage, and they have echoed far beyond it, into how many people everywhere think about right and wrong.

So when we meet the prophets, the question isn't "did they predict things?" but "**what did they stand for?**" — and the answer is justice, kindness and honesty.

**Worked example.** "What was a prophet's main job in the story?" Not fortune-telling → but being a **voice of conscience**: telling kings and people to be **fair, honest and kind**, especially to the poor.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "**Swords into ploughshares**" — turning weapons into farming tools; a famous image of peace (it's even on a statue at the United Nations).
- "Speaking truth to power" — a prophet daring to tell a king he's wrong; courage.
- Link to fairness the child already cares about ("that's not fair!") — the prophets felt that about the whole world.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Prophets were fortune-tellers." **Why:** the modern meaning of "predict the future." **Fix:** in this story prophets were mainly **messengers of justice** — voices urging people to do right, not crystal-ball gazers.
- **Wrong idea:** "Prophets only spoke to ordinary people." **Why:** assuming they avoided the powerful. **Fix:** they often confronted **kings** themselves, which took great courage.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What was a prophet's main role in Jewish heritage? **A:** To be a voice of conscience — calling people and rulers to be fair, honest and kind.
- **Q:** Name one famous prophet. **A:** Any of: Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos.
- **Q:** What does "swords into ploughshares" mean? **A:** Turning weapons into farming tools — a hope for peace.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs / speak.* Match prophet-ideas to meanings (justice "like waters" → fairness everywhere; "swords into ploughshares" → peace). Then a *speak* prompt: "If you were a voice for fairness today, what would you want people to do?"

- **BBC Bitesize — "Judaism: the Tanakh / Nevi'im"** (what the prophets were).
- **Sefaria (sefaria.org) — Prophets in plain English** (look up Isaiah/Amos passages; parent-guided).

## 7. The Second Temple period

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 9–12 · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – return from exile & the Second Temple

**Prerequisites:** Jerusalem and the First Temple.

### Key concepts
- After the Babylonian exile, the Persian king **Cyrus** allowed the Jews to **return** to Jerusalem and build a **Second Temple** (completed around **515 BCE**).
- The Second Temple period lasted centuries, under different empires (Persian, then Greek, then Roman), and is much **better documented** by archaeology and writings than the First.
- King **Herod** grandly rebuilt and expanded the Second Temple; it was finally **destroyed by the Romans in 70 CE** — beginning a long exile. The **Western Wall** survives from its surroundings.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the First Temple was destroyed and the people exiled to Babylon; can use a BCE/CE timeline.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The exile in Babylon did not last forever. A new empire, **Persia**, conquered Babylon, and the Persian king **Cyrus** did something remarkable: around **538 BCE** he let the exiled Jews **go home to Jerusalem** and rebuild their Temple. The **Second Temple** was finished around **515 BCE** — a return and a fresh start.

This **Second Temple period** lasted a long time — about **600 years**. During it, the land passed under different great empires: first **Persia**, then the **Greeks** (after Alexander the Great), then **Rome**. Much more about this period is **documented**: there are coins, inscriptions, buildings, and writings by historians, so historians stand on firmer ground here than for the earliest stories.

Centuries in, the Roman-appointed king **Herod the Great** rebuilt the Second Temple into something **enormous and magnificent**, with huge stone walls around it. But Roman rule grew harsh, the Jews rebelled, and in **70 CE** the Romans **destroyed the Second Temple** and much of Jerusalem. This began a very long **exile** for most of the Jewish people. One great wall of the Temple's surroundings still stands today — the **Western Wall** (Kotel), where people still come to pray.

**Worked example.** "How is the Second Temple's story different from the First's?" The First: built by Solomon, destroyed by **Babylon** (587 BCE), known mostly by **tradition**. The Second: built after the **return from exile**, rebuilt huge by **Herod**, destroyed by **Rome** (70 CE), and **much better documented** by archaeology. Same holy place, two Temples, two destructions.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- The **Western Wall** today — a real, touchable survivor of the Second Temple's surroundings.
- Cyrus letting the people go home — a rare ancient story of an empire *helping* an exiled people return.
- Herod's giant building stones — some weigh as much as several elephants; "how did they lift them?"

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The Second Temple was the same building as the First." **Why:** "the Temple" sounds like one thing. **Fix:** the First was destroyed by Babylon; the Second was a **new** building (later enlarged by Herod), destroyed by Rome in 70 CE.
- **Wrong idea:** "The Romans and Babylonians were the same destroyers." **Why:** both destroyed a Temple. **Fix:** **Babylon** destroyed the **First** Temple (587 BCE); **Rome** destroyed the **Second** (70 CE) — centuries apart.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which king let the exiled Jews return and rebuild the Temple? **A:** Cyrus of Persia.
- **Q:** Who destroyed the Second Temple, and in what year? **A:** The Romans, in 70 CE.
- **Q:** What survives today from the Second Temple's surroundings? **A:** The Western Wall (Kotel).

### Activity / practice
*Ordering.* Build the timeline: First Temple → Babylon destroys it (587 BCE) → exile → Cyrus lets them return → Second Temple (515 BCE) → Herod rebuilds it → Rome destroys it (70 CE). Then *multipleChoice*: "Which Temple did the Romans destroy?" (the Second).

- **BBC Bitesize — "Romans" + Judaism** (Roman rule context; the Temple's destruction, for older children).

## 8. The Maccabees and the Hasmoneans

**Year / Key Stage:** Ages 7–12 · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – the Maccabean revolt

**Prerequisites:** The Second Temple period.

### Key concepts
- In the **Second Temple period**, the land was ruled by **Greek (Seleucid)** kings. King **Antiochus** banned Jewish practice and put pagan worship in the Temple.
- A family led by **Mattathias** and his son **Judah the Maccabee** ("the Hammer") led a **revolt** (from **167 BCE**), won against a far bigger army, and **rededicated the Temple** — the origin of **Hanukkah**.
- The Maccabees founded the **Hasmonean** dynasty, an **independent Jewish kingdom** for about **80 years**, until the Romans took control.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Second Temple existed and the land was ruled by empires; understands "revolt" and "independence."

### Teach it (the explanation)
During the Second Temple period, the Land of Israel was ruled by **Greek** kings (the Seleucids). Most let people live as they liked — but one king, **Antiochus**, decided everyone must follow **Greek customs and gods**. He **banned** Jewish practices and put **pagan worship inside the Temple** in Jerusalem. For many Jews this was unbearable.

In a village, an old priest named **Mattathias** refused — and when he died, his son **Judah** took over. Judah was such a fierce fighter he was nicknamed the **Maccabee**, "the Hammer." With a small band against the mighty Greek army, the **Maccabees** fought a clever guerrilla war (**from 167 BCE**) — and, astonishingly, they **won**. They marched into Jerusalem, cleaned the Temple of pagan idols, and **rededicated** it to God around **164 BCE**. That rededication — *Hanukkah* means "dedication" — is exactly what the festival of **Hanukkah** celebrates every year (we meet the festival in the festivals file).

The Maccabees' family then ruled an **independent Jewish kingdom** — the **Hasmoneans** — for about **80 years**. It was a rare time of Jewish independence in the ancient world, before the **Romans** took control and, eventually, destroyed the Second Temple. This is well-documented history: we have **ancient books (the Books of the Maccabees)**, coins and writings about it.

**Worked example.** "Why do we celebrate Hanukkah?" Trace it: Greek king Antiochus **banned** Jewish practice and defiled the **Temple** → the **Maccabees revolted** and won against the odds → they **cleaned and rededicated** the Temple → that rededication is *Hanukkah*, "dedication," celebrated every year.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- The **few against the many** — a small band beating a huge army; the underdog story.
- "The Hammer" — Judah Maccabee's fierce nickname; courage and cleverness.
- Link straight to the **Hanukkah** the child may know — the menorah, the lights, the dreidel.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Hanukkah is mainly about a jar of oil." **Why:** the popular oil-miracle is the famous part. **Fix:** at its heart Hanukkah remembers the **Maccabees' victory and the rededication of the Temple** — a real history of courage and freedom (the oil story is part of the later tradition).
- **Wrong idea:** "The Maccabees fought the Romans." **Why:** Romans come up nearby in the timeline. **Fix:** the Maccabees fought the **Greeks (Seleucids)**; the **Romans** came **later** and ended Hasmonean independence.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Who did the Maccabees fight, and why? **A:** The Greek (Seleucid) king Antiochus, because he banned Jewish practice and defiled the Temple.
- **Q:** What did the Maccabees do after they won? **A:** They cleaned and rededicated the Temple — the origin of Hanukkah.
- **Q:** What was the Hasmonean kingdom? **A:** The independent Jewish kingdom the Maccabees' family ruled for about 80 years.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering / matchPairs.* Order: Antiochus bans Jewish practice → Mattathias refuses → Judah "the Maccabee" leads the revolt → victory → rededicate the Temple → Hasmonean kingdom → Romans take over. Match: Maccabee → "the Hammer"; Hanukkah → "dedication."
