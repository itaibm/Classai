# English — Year 5 (ages 9–10)

Year 5 begins Upper KS2. Grammar adds **relative clauses, modal verbs, adverbs of possibility, and brackets/dashes/commas for parenthesis**; spelling tackles **-able/-ible, -ant/-ent, silent letters and the ei/ie rule** plus the Y5/6 word list; reading develops **evidence-based inference, comparison and distinguishing fact from opinion**; writing focuses on **persuasion, cohesion and integrating dialogue**.

---

## 1. Relative clauses (who, which, that, whose, where, when)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar & punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 fronted adverbials; clauses and conjunctions.

### Key concepts
- A **relative clause** adds extra information about a noun, using a **relative pronoun**: *who, which, that, whose, where, when*.
- Use **who** for people, **which** for things, **whose** for belonging.
- A non-essential relative clause is marked off with **commas**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows nouns and that a clause has a verb.
- Can use conjunctions to join clauses.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **relative clause** is an "extra info" clause that drops in to tell you more about a noun, starting with a relative pronoun:
- **who** (people): "The boy **who won the race** was tired."
- **which** (things): "My bike, **which is bright red**, is new."
- **whose** (belonging): "The girl **whose dog ran off** was upset."
- **where/when** (place/time): "The town **where I was born**…".
If the clause is **extra** (you could remove it and the sentence still makes sense), wrap it in **commas**: "Mr Khan, **who lives next door**, waved." If it's **essential** to identify which one, you usually use *that* and no commas: "The book **that I lost** was new."

**Worked example.** Add a relative clause about "the castle".
1. Main: *The castle was huge.*
2. Add info about the castle with *which*: *The castle, **which stood on a high cliff**, was huge.*
3. It's extra info → commas around it.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **People you know:** "My friend, who plays football, …".
- **Possessions:** "The phone, which has a cracked screen, …".
- **Places:** "The park where we play …".

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** uses *which* for a person (*the man which…*).
  **Why:** doesn't match pronoun to noun.
  **Fix:** *who* for people, *which* for things.
- **Wrong:** no commas around an extra (non-essential) clause.
  **Why:** doesn't hear the "by the way" pause.
  **Fix:** test — if you can lift the clause out and it still makes sense, comma it off.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which relative pronoun is for people? **A:** *who*.
- **Q:** Add a relative clause to "The dog barked." **A:** e.g. *The dog, which was huge, barked.*
- **Q:** Do you comma off an essential clause (the one that says *which* book)? **A:** No.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`/`multipleChoice`: choose the right relative pronoun and add commas where the clause is non-essential.

## 2. Modal verbs and adverbs of possibility

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 verbs and Standard English.

### Key concepts
- **Modal verbs** (*can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would*) show **possibility, certainty, permission or obligation**.
- **Adverbs of possibility** (*possibly, probably, certainly, definitely, perhaps, maybe*) do a similar job.
- They change how **sure** the writer is.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows what a verb is.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Modal verbs** sit in front of another verb and tell us how *likely, allowed or necessary* something is:
- Possibility: *might, may, could* — "It **might** rain." (not sure)
- Certainty: *will, must* — "It **will** rain." (sure)
- Obligation: *must, should* — "You **must** wear a helmet."
- Permission: *can, may* — "You **may** leave."
**Adverbs of possibility** do the same with a `-ly` word: "It will **probably** rain", "**Perhaps** it will snow". A scale of certainty: *definitely → probably → possibly → perhaps*.

**Worked example.** Show different certainty about "we win".
1. Sure: *We **will** win.* / *We will **definitely** win.*
2. Likely: *We **should** win.* / *We will **probably** win.*
3. Unsure: *We **might** win.* / *Maybe we will win.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Weather forecasts:** "it might / will / could…".
- **Rules and safety:** "you must wear…", "you should…".
- **Predictions about a match or game.**

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** uses "**must** of" / "**should of**".
  **Why:** mishears *must've/should've* (have).
  **Fix:** it's *must **have*** / *should **have*** — never "of".
- **Wrong:** treats all modals as equally certain.
  **Why:** doesn't sense the scale.
  **Fix:** rank them: *will/must* (sure) vs *might/could* (unsure).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which is more certain: "It might snow" or "It will snow"? **A:** "It will snow".
- **Q:** Give an adverb of possibility. **A:** any of *perhaps, probably, possibly, definitely*.
- **Q:** Correct "I should of gone." **A:** *I should have gone.*

### Activity / practice
`ordering`/`multipleChoice`: rank sentences from least to most certain; choose the modal that fits the meaning.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Modal verbs* and *Adverbs of possibility***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 5 modal verbs**.

## 3. Brackets, dashes and commas for parenthesis

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 commas; relative clauses.

### Key concepts
- **Parenthesis** is extra information dropped into a sentence that could be removed.
- It is marked with a **pair** of brackets, dashes, or commas.
- The sentence must still make sense if you take the parenthesis out.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can use commas and write full sentences.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **parenthesis** is a bit of "by the way" extra information added into the middle (or end) of a sentence. You mark it off with one of three **pairs**:
- **Brackets ( )** — most formal/separate: "My dog (a noisy spaniel) barks all night."
- **Dashes – –** — more dramatic/informal: "My dog – a noisy spaniel – barks all night."
- **Commas , ,** — smoothest: "My dog, a noisy spaniel, barks all night."
The golden test: **remove the parenthesis and the sentence still works**. Always use them in **pairs** (open and close).

**Worked example.** Add a parenthesis to "The Romans built roads."
1. Extra info: they were excellent engineers.
2. Drop it in with brackets: *The Romans (who were excellent engineers) built roads.*
3. Remove it → *The Romans built roads.* ✓ still works.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Fact files:** add extra facts in brackets.
- **Adding an aside** in a story for humour (dashes).
- **Captions:** "Mount Everest (the world's tallest mountain)…".

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** opens a bracket/dash but never closes it.
  **Why:** forgets parenthesis comes in pairs.
  **Fix:** "two of them, always" — one to open, one to close.
- **Wrong:** the sentence breaks when the parenthesis is removed.
  **Why:** put essential info in parenthesis.
  **Fix:** test by deleting it — only truly *extra* info goes in parenthesis.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name the three ways to punctuate parenthesis. **A:** Brackets, dashes, commas.
- **Q:** What's the test for a parenthesis? **A:** Remove it — the sentence still makes sense.
- **Q:** Add a parenthesis to "The lion roared." **A:** e.g. *The lion (king of the jungle) roared.*

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`: insert a parenthesis (brackets, dashes or commas) into five sentences and check the sentence still works without it.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Parenthesis (brackets, dashes and commas)***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 5 parenthesis**.

## 4. Spelling: -able/-ible and -ant/-ent

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Spelling

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 suffix families.

### Key concepts
- **-able** is more common and often attaches to a whole word (*enjoy → enjoyable*); **-ible** often attaches to a non-word root (*poss → possible*).
- Related: **-ation/-ably** with *-able*; **-ibly** with *-ible*.
- **-ant/-ance** vs **-ent/-ence** follow similar root clues.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can add suffixes and knows root words.

### Teach it (the explanation)
These endings sound almost the same, so use clues:
- **-able**: usually added to a complete word, and you can often hear a related *-ation* word: *adore → adorable* (*adoration*), *enjoy → enjoyable*, *comfort → comfortable*. Adverb: *-ably* (*comfortably*).
- **-ible**: usually added to a root that is **not** a full word: *poss-ible, horr-ible, terr-ible, vis-ible*. Adverb: *-ibly* (*horribly*).
- **-ant/-ance** and **-ent/-ence** pair up the same way: *observant/observance, innocent/innocence*. Often a related word reveals the vowel (*confident → confidence*).
These have many exceptions — learn the common ones by sight too.

**Worked example.** Choose for *poss__* and *enjoy__*.
1. *poss-* is not a full word → **-ible** → *possible* (*possibly*).
2. *enjoy* is a full word, and *enjoyment* exists → **-able** → *enjoyable*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Reviews:** *enjoyable, horrible, terrible, incredible* (rate a film/game).
- **Personality words:** *confident, patient, important*.
- **Word detective:** find the related *-ation/-ation* clue word.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *enjoyible, possable*.
  **Why:** guessed without root clues.
  **Fix:** full word → *-able*; partial root → *-ible*.
- **Wrong:** *independant, confidant* (for *independent, confident*).
  **Why:** *-ant/-ent* confusion.
  **Fix:** check a related word for the vowel: *confidence → confident*.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** *-able* or *-ible*: enjoy__? **A:** *enjoyable*.
- **Q:** *-able* or *-ible*: poss__? **A:** *possible*.
- **Q:** Spell the opposite of "guilty" ending *-ent*. **A:** *innocent*.

### Activity / practice
`categorize`: sort words into *-able* / *-ible* and *-ant* / *-ent*; `fillBlank` from root words.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Words ending in -able and -ible***.
- **Spelling Frame — Year 5/6 -able/-ible, -ant/-ent**.

## 5. Silent letters, the ei/ie rule, and the Y5/6 word list

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Spelling

**Prerequisites:** Year 3/4 word list strategies.

### Key concepts
- Some words have **silent letters** you must remember (*knee, doubt, island, lamb, science*).
- "***i* before *e* except after *c***" (when the sound is `/ee/`): *believe, receive*; exceptions exist (*weird, science*).
- The Y5/6 list (~100 words) contains common-but-hard words (*accommodate, necessary, rhythm, separate, definitely*).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows spelling strategies (mnemonics, syllable-chopping).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Tricky spellings need memory tricks:
- **Silent letters** hide in plain sight: *k* in *knee/know*, *b* in *doubt/lamb/comb*, *s* in *island*, *c* in *science/scissors*, *w* in *wrist/write*, *h* in *honest/hour*. Spot and remember them.
- **i before e:** when it sounds like `/ee/`, *i* comes before *e* **except after c**: *belie**ve**, *fie**ld*** but re**ce**ive, **ce**iling. Watch exceptions: *weird, seize, science, their*.
- **Y5/6 hard words** — learn by sight + mnemonics: *necessary* ("one collar, two socks" = 1 *c*, 2 *s*); *accommodate* (room for 2 *c*s and 2 *m*s); *rhythm* ("Rhythm Helps Your Two Hips Move").

**Worked example.** Spell *receive*.
1. Sound is `/ee/`.
2. It comes **after c** → so it's *e* before *i*: *rec-**ei**-ve* → *receive*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Spelling-bee challenge** of the hardest words.
- **Mnemonic-making competition** for a tricky word.
- **Silent-letter hunt** in a book page.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *recieve, beleive*.
  **Why:** ignores the "after c" part of the rule.
  **Fix:** `/ee/` sound → i before e **except after c**.
- **Wrong:** *neccessary, accomodate, rythm*.
  **Why:** wrong number of doubled letters.
  **Fix:** use the mnemonic ("1 collar, 2 socks"; "2 c's, 2 m's").

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** *ie* or *ei*: rec__ve? **A:** *receive* (after c).
- **Q:** Find the silent letter in *doubt*. **A:** *b*.
- **Q:** Spell *necessary*. **A:** n-e-c-e-s-s-a-r-y (1 c, 2 s).

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`/`speak`: dictate Y5/6 words with silent letters and ei/ie; child writes and underlines the tricky part.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Silent letters* and *i before e***.
- **Spelling Frame — Year 5/6 statutory word list** (free).

## 6. Distinguishing fact from opinion; comparing texts

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Reading comprehension

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 inference, summarising.

### Key concepts
- A **fact** can be checked and proven true; an **opinion** is what someone thinks or feels.
- Opinion signals: *I think, best, worst, should, amazing, terrible*.
- **Comparing** texts means noticing how two texts are similar or different (viewpoint, purpose, style).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can retrieve and infer with evidence.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **fact** is true whether or not you agree — it can be checked: "The Sun is a star." An **opinion** is a personal view: "Summer is the best season." Spot opinion words: *I think, believe, best, worst, amazing, should, probably*. Texts often mix both — adverts and reviews are full of opinions dressed as facts. **Comparing** texts means asking: do they agree? Whose viewpoint is each? Same topic, different purpose? Use a sentence stem: "Both texts… but text A… whereas text B…".

**Worked example.** Sort: "Dogs have four legs." / "Dogs make the best pets."
1. "Dogs have four legs." → **fact** (checkable).
2. "Dogs make the best pets." → **opinion** (someone's view — *best* is a clue).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Adverts:** spot opinions pretending to be facts.
- **Reviews:** of a game/film — separate fact from opinion.
- **Two news reports** of the same event — compare viewpoints.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** calls a strong opinion a fact ("This is the best film *ever*" = fact).
  **Why:** confident tone feels factual.
  **Fix:** ask "can it be checked/proven?" If it's a judgement, it's opinion.
- **Wrong:** when comparing, only describes one text.
  **Why:** forgets to link them.
  **Fix:** use "Both… but… whereas…" to compare directly.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Fact or opinion: "Water boils at 100°C." **A:** Fact.
- **Q:** Fact or opinion: "Maths is boring." **A:** Opinion.
- **Q:** Give an opinion signal word. **A:** any of *think, best, should, amazing*.

### Activity / practice
`categorize`/`speak`: sort ten statements into fact/opinion; compare two short texts using "Both… but…".

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Fact and opinion* and *Comparing texts***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 5 reading comprehension**.

## 7. Writing to persuade

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition

**Prerequisites:** Fact and opinion; modal verbs; relative clauses.

### Key concepts
- **Persuasion** tries to convince the reader to agree or act.
- Tools: a clear viewpoint, **reasons + evidence**, rhetorical questions, emotive language, the "rule of three", and **modal verbs** (*must, should*).
- Structure: hook → arguments (one per paragraph) → strong conclusion.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write in paragraphs.
- Can tell fact from opinion; knows modal verbs.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Persuasive writing wants the reader to **agree or do something** — like a letter, advert, or campaign. Powerful tools (the "**AFOREST**" toolkit is handy): **A**lliteration, **F**acts, **O**pinions, **R**hetorical questions, **E**motive language, **S**tatistics, **T**riples (rule of three). Use **modal verbs** for urgency: "We **must** act now." State a clear viewpoint, give a reason **and** evidence for each, and address the reader directly ("Imagine if…"). End with a punchy call to action.

**Worked example.** Persuade people to recycle (opening + one argument).
- Hook (rhetorical question): *Did you know we throw away enough rubbish to fill a stadium every day?*
- Argument: *Recycling **must** become a habit **because** it saves precious resources, protects wildlife and reduces pollution.* (rule of three + modal + reason)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Campaigns:** save the planet, longer playtime, a school cause.
- **Adverts:** "buy this!" with rhetorical questions.
- **A persuasive letter** to a real person about a real wish.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** only states opinions with no reasons/evidence.
  **Why:** thinks persuasion = strong feelings.
  **Fix:** every point needs a *because* + evidence (a fact or example).
- **Wrong:** wishy-washy, neutral tone.
  **Why:** afraid to take a side.
  **Fix:** pick a clear viewpoint and use modal verbs/emotive language for force.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name two persuasive techniques. **A:** any two of rhetorical question, rule of three, emotive language, facts/statistics.
- **Q:** Which modal verb adds urgency? **A:** *must* (or *should*).
- **Q:** What should every persuasive point include? **A:** A reason and evidence.

### Activity / practice
Writing task: write a persuasive paragraph on a chosen cause using a rhetorical question, a modal verb, and the rule of three (rehearse the argument aloud first with `speak`).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Persuasive writing***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 5, *Writing to persuade***.

## 8. Cohesion: linking ideas across paragraphs

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition / Grammar

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 paragraphs; fronted adverbials.

### Key concepts
- **Cohesion** = the way writing "sticks together" so it flows.
- **Cohesive devices** include linking adverbials (*however, therefore, meanwhile, in addition*), pronouns (to avoid repeating nouns), and time/place references.
- Good cohesion guides the reader smoothly between and within paragraphs.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write multi-paragraph texts.
- Knows fronted adverbials.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Cohesion** is the glue between sentences and paragraphs. Three devices:
1. **Adverbials of cohesion** — link ideas: contrast (*however, on the other hand*), adding (*in addition, furthermore*), result (*therefore, as a result*), time (*meanwhile, later, eventually*). Often used as fronted adverbials to open a paragraph.
2. **Pronouns** — replace a repeated noun so you don't say "the dog" five times: "**The spaniel** barked. **It** wouldn't stop." (Make sure it's clear what *it* refers to.)
3. **Referencing back** — "this problem", "such a plan" — points back to an earlier idea.
These devices keep the reader on track and stop writing feeling like a disconnected list.

**Worked example.** Link two paragraphs about pollution.
- End of para 1: …pollution is harming our oceans.
- Open para 2 with a cohesive adverbial: ***However**, there are simple steps we can all take.* (signals a turn to solutions)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Debates/essays:** signposting "firstly… however… therefore…".
- **Recipes/instructions:** "meanwhile… next…".
- **Editing:** hunt repeated nouns and swap in pronouns.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** repeats the same noun over and over.
  **Why:** doesn't use pronouns/referencing.
  **Fix:** swap repeats for pronouns or "this/such", keeping the reference clear.
- **Wrong:** every sentence starts the same way ("Then… Then… Then…").
  **Why:** limited linking vocabulary.
  **Fix:** vary cohesive adverbials (*however, meanwhile, in addition, therefore*).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Give a cohesive adverbial that shows contrast. **A:** *however* (or *on the other hand*).
- **Q:** Why use a pronoun like *it*? **A:** To avoid repeating the noun and link sentences.
- **Q:** Which device links ideas across paragraphs? **A:** Linking/cohesive adverbials.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`: insert the best cohesive adverbial (*however, therefore, in addition, meanwhile*) between sentences; rewrite a repetitive passage using pronouns.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Cohesion and linking ideas***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 5/6 cohesive devices**.

## 9. Integrating dialogue to show character

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 direct speech; Year 4 characters.

### Key concepts
- Dialogue (speech) can **reveal character** — how someone speaks shows who they are.
- **New speaker = new line** (new paragraph).
- Use varied speech verbs and add **action beats** to show *how* it's said and move the plot.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can punctuate direct speech with inverted commas.
- Can develop a character.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Dialogue isn't just "talking heads" — it **shows character and advances the story**. Make it work harder:
- **Start a new line each time the speaker changes.**
- Vary your speech verbs (*whispered, snapped, pleaded, muttered*) instead of always "said" — but don't overdo it.
- Add **action beats** around speech to show emotion and keep the scene moving: *"I'm fine," she muttered, turning away.*
- What a character *says* and *how* they say it reveals personality — a bossy character barks orders; a shy one trails off.
Punctuate carefully: comma/end-mark inside the speech marks, capital to start the speech.

**Worked example.** Show two characters' personalities through dialogue.
> "Hurry **up**!" barked the captain, stamping his boot. (impatient)
> "I — I'm trying," stammered Sam, fumbling with the rope. (nervous)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Comics → prose:** turn a comic conversation into written dialogue.
- **Argument scenes:** show conflict between characters.
- **Favourite characters:** write how *they* would speak.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** keeps two speakers' lines in the same paragraph.
  **Why:** doesn't know the new-line rule.
  **Fix:** "new speaker, new line".
- **Wrong:** "said-bookism" overload — every line a fancy verb (*ejaculated, vociferated*).
  **Why:** over-correcting "said".
  **Fix:** mostly *said*, with occasional stronger verbs and action beats for effect.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What do you do when a new person speaks? **A:** Start a new line/paragraph.
- **Q:** What is an action beat? **A:** A small action around the speech showing how it's said.
- **Q:** Give a speech verb that shows anger. **A:** *snapped/shouted/barked*.

### Activity / practice
Writing task: write a short dialogue between two characters with contrasting personalities, using new lines, correct punctuation, and one action beat each.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Writing dialogue / using speech***.
- **Pobble 365** — image prompts featuring characters to write dialogue for.
