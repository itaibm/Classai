# English — Year 1 (ages 5–6)

Year 1 is the engine room of reading: children master **Phase 5 phonics**, blend confidently to read, segment to spell, learn the first common exception words, and write simple sentences with capital letters and full stops. Talk comes before writing in every lesson.

---

## 1. Blending sounds to read words (Phase 2/3 review)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Word reading – phonics

**Prerequisites:** none (assumes Reception Phase 2/3 sounds taught).

### Key concepts
- A **grapheme** (letter or letters) stands for a **phoneme** (a sound).
- **Blending** = saying each sound in order and pushing them together to read a word.
- You read left to right: c-a-t → "cat".

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the sounds for single letters `s a t p i n m d g o c k e u r h b f l`.
- Can say a single sound when shown its letter.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Reading a word is like being a sound detective. You don't say the *letter names* ("see, ay, tee") — you say the *sounds*: `/c/ /a/ /t/`. Then you blend: say them faster and faster until they snap together into a word — "cat!". Point under each letter as you sound it (sound-button style), then sweep your finger under the whole word as you blend.

**Worked example.** Read *pin*.
1. Point under `p`: `/p/`.
2. Point under `i`: `/i/`.
3. Point under `n`: `/n/`.
4. Blend it together: `/p/-/i/-/n/` → "pin". It's a thing that holds paper together!

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Animals:** decode *cat, dog, hen, pig, fox* and match each to its animal.
- **Snacks:** read a little shopping list — *jam, bun, ham*.
- **Toys/games:** make a word out of magnetic letters, then "press the sound buttons".

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** says letter names ("see-ay-tee") and can't make a word.
  **Why:** confusing the alphabet song (names) with phonics (sounds).
  **Fix:** model "we say the *sound* it makes" — over-articulate pure sounds (`/c/`, not "cuh-uh").
- **Wrong:** blends out of order or skips the middle sound (*ct* for *cat*).
  **Why:** not tracking left-to-right under each grapheme.
  **Fix:** use a finger/counter under each letter, one sound per touch, before blending.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Sound out and read this word: *sit*. **A:** /s/ /i/ /t/ → "sit".
- **Q:** How many sounds are in *dog*? **A:** 3 (/d/ /o/ /g/).
- **Q:** Read *flag*. **A:** /f/ /l/ /a/ /g/ → "flag".

### Activity / practice
`speak` block: show 4 CVC words (*man, top, web, run*); the child reads each aloud by sounding out then blending. Then a `matchPairs`: match the word to a picture.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Blending sounds to read words*** (phonics blending guide).
- **Alphablocks (BBC iPlayer / YouTube channel)** — animated blending of CVC words.

## 2. Segmenting to spell (sound it out to write it)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Spelling – phonics

**Prerequisites:** Blending sounds to read words.

### Key concepts
- **Segmenting** = the opposite of blending: break a spoken word into its separate sounds.
- Count the sounds, then write a grapheme for each one, in order.
- Most short words have one letter per sound.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can hear individual sounds in simple words.
- Can write the letters for taught single-letter sounds.

### Teach it (the explanation)
To spell, we go backwards from reading. Say the whole word, then chop it into sounds — like cutting a sausage. For *ship* (we'll use a digraph later) start with *map*: say "map", then break it: `/m/ /a/ /p/`. Hold up a finger for each sound. Now write one grapheme per sound: m – a – p. Read it back to check.

**Worked example.** Spell *net*.
1. Say the word: "net".
2. Segment: `/n/ /e/ /t/` — three sounds, three fingers.
3. Write a letter for each: n, e, t.
4. Read it back: "net" ✓.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Football:** spell *net, kick, goal* (start with the simple ones).
- **Pets:** spell your pet's short name by sounding it out.
- **Lunchbox:** spell *bun, jam, egg*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** misses a sound (*nt* for *net*).
  **Why:** vowels are the hardest to hear and easiest to drop.
  **Fix:** "robot voice" the word slowly, stretching the middle sound; one finger per sound.
- **Wrong:** writes letters in the wrong order (*ten* for *net*).
  **Why:** not anchoring to the first sound.
  **Fix:** always ask "what sound does it START with?" then work through in order.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Spell *pig* by sounding it out. **A:** /p/ /i/ /g/ → p-i-g.
- **Q:** How many sounds do you write for *sun*? **A:** 3.
- **Q:** Spell *frog*. **A:** /f/ /r/ /o/ /g/ → frog.

### Activity / practice
`speak`/`fillBlank`: tutor says a CVC/CCVC word aloud; child segments on fingers then types the letters. Words: *cat, hop, swim, lamp*.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Segmenting words***.
- **Phonics Play — *Picnic on Pluto / Buried Treasure*** (segmenting games, free section).

## 3. Digraphs and trigraphs (two or three letters, one sound)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Word reading – Phase 3/5

**Prerequisites:** Blending sounds to read words.

### Key concepts
- A **digraph** is two letters making one sound: `sh, ch, th, ng, ai, ee, oa`.
- A **trigraph** is three letters making one sound: `igh, ear, air`.
- Treat the whole group as ONE sound when you blend.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can blend CVC words with single letters.
- Knows that a grapheme stands for a phoneme.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Some sounds need a team of letters. In *ship*, `s` and `h` don't say `/s/ /h/` — together they say one new sound, `/sh/`. Draw a sound-button *under both letters* as one button. So *ship* is `/sh/ /i/ /p/` — three sounds, four letters. In *night*, `i-g-h` is a three-letter team saying one sound `/ie/`: `/n/ /igh/ /t/`.

**Worked example.** Read *chain*.
1. Spot the teams: `ch` and `ai`.
2. Sound: `/ch/ /ai/ /n/` — three sounds.
3. Blend → "chain".

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Weather:** *rain, snow* (well, *rain*) — find the `ai` team.
- **Animals:** *sheep, fish, shark* — hunt for `sh`/`ee`.
- **Night-time:** *light, night, moon* — the `igh` and `oo` teams.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** sounds each letter of a digraph separately (*s-h-i-p* = 4 sounds).
  **Why:** doesn't yet see letter teams as one unit.
  **Fix:** draw one sound button under the digraph; practise "spot the team first, then blend".
- **Wrong:** reads *th* as `/t/ /h/` in *that*.
  **Why:** `th` is unfamiliar as a single sound.
  **Fix:** teach `th` explicitly with a tongue-out mirror sound; collect `th` words.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many sounds in *fish*? **A:** 3 (/f/ /i/ /sh/).
- **Q:** Read *night*. **A:** /n/ /igh/ /t/ → "night".
- **Q:** Find the digraph in *boat*. **A:** `oa`.

### Activity / practice
`categorize` block: sort words by their digraph — column `sh` (*ship, shop, fish*), column `ch` (*chip, chin, rich*), column `ai` (*rain, pain, wait*).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Digraphs and trigraphs***.
- **Phonics Play — Phase 3/5 grapheme games** (free section).

## 4. Split digraphs and Phase 5 alternatives (the magic *e*)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Word reading – Phase 5

**Prerequisites:** Digraphs and trigraphs.

### Key concepts
- A **split digraph** is a vowel team split by a consonant: `a_e, i_e, o_e, u_e`.
- The silent *e* at the end changes the vowel from short to its "name": *hop → hope*, *kit → kite*.
- One sound can be spelled different ways (`ai`, `ay`, `a_e` all say `/ai/`).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can read CVC words and simple digraphs.
- Knows short vowel sounds.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Sometimes the two letters of a vowel team get *split up* by another letter. In *cake*, the `a` and the `e` are a team (`a_e`) but the `k` jumped in between them. That `e` is the "magic e" — it doesn't make its own sound; it reaches back over the consonant and tells the vowel to "say its name". So *cap* (short `/a/`) becomes *cape* (long `/ai/`). Cover the final `e` to see the short word, then add it back.

**Worked example.** Read *time*.
1. Spot `i_e` (the `i...e` team with `m` in the middle).
2. The `e` makes the `i` say its name `/ie/`.
3. Sound: `/t/ /ie/ /m/` → "time".

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Games:** *game, race, prize* — magic-e words about playing.
- **Food:** *cake, lime, cube* — sort treats.
- **Superpowers:** the silent *e* is a "superhero" that changes the word.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** pronounces the silent *e* (*cak-e*, *tim-e*).
  **Why:** assumes every letter makes a sound.
  **Fix:** teach the *e* as "lazy / silent / magic" — it works without making a sound.
- **Wrong:** reads *kite* with a short `/i/` (rhymes with *kit*).
  **Why:** ignored the split digraph signal.
  **Fix:** "spot the e on the end → make the vowel say its name". Compare pairs: *pin/pine, hop/hope*.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does the *e* do in *cake*? **A:** Makes the `a` say its name `/ai/` (it's silent itself).
- **Q:** Read *home*. **A:** /h/ /oa/ /m/ → "home".
- **Q:** Change *cub* into a long word with magic e. **A:** *cube*.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`/`multipleChoice`: show a picture of a kite — choose the spelling: *kit / kite / kight*. Then change short words to magic-e words: *can→cane, hop→hope, tub→tube*.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Split digraphs***.
- **Alphablocks — magic *e* episodes**.

## 5. Common exception (tricky) words

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Word reading / Spelling

**Prerequisites:** Blending sounds to read words.

### Key concepts
- Some words can't be fully sounded out yet — these are **tricky / common exception words**.
- You learn the **tricky part** by heart, but most of the word still follows the sounds.
- Reading them on sight makes reading fast and smooth.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can blend regular words.
- Recognises that some words "don't play fair".

### Teach it (the explanation)
Most words you can read by sounding out, but a few have a sneaky part that breaks the rules. In *said*, you'd expect `ai` to say `/ai/`, but here it says `/e/` — that's the tricky bit. We read the parts we can (`s...d`) and just *remember* the middle. The Year 1 tricky words include: *the, a, do, to, today, of, said, says, are, were, was, is, his, has, I, you, your, they, be, he, me, she, we, no, go, so, by, my, here, there, where, love, come, some, one, once, ask, friend, school, put, push, pull, full, house, our*.

**Worked example.** Read *was*.
1. Sound the parts: `w...s`.
2. The tricky bit: `a` here says `/o/`, not `/a/`.
3. So it reads "woz" = *was*. Remember the middle!

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Story openings:** "*Once* upon a time…", "*There* *was* a…" — tricky words start the best stories.
- **Speech:** "*said* the wolf", "*you* can do it".
- Make a **"tricky word wall"** the child decorates.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** tries to sound *the* as `/t/ /h/ /e/` and gets "thuh-eh".
  **Why:** applying regular blending to an irregular word.
  **Fix:** flag it as tricky; teach on sight; practise in a quick "flashcard" routine.
- **Wrong:** spells *said* as *sed* or *sayd*.
  **Why:** spelling it by sound.
  **Fix:** explicitly memorise the `ai` in *said*; "say it in a silly way" (s-AY-id) to lock spelling.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Read this word: *they*. **A:** "they" (read on sight).
- **Q:** Which part of *said* is tricky? **A:** the `ai`, which says `/e/`.
- **Q:** Spell *the*. **A:** t-h-e.

### Activity / practice
`speak`/`multipleChoice` flashcard race: flash 6 tricky words for 3 seconds each; child reads on sight. Then spell *said, was, you* from memory.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Tricky words / Common exception words***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 1 English, *Common exception words*** lessons.

## 6. Capital letters and full stops (a sentence)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** VGP – punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Segmenting to spell.

### Key concepts
- A **sentence** is a group of words that makes complete sense on its own.
- A sentence **starts with a capital letter** and **ends with a full stop**.
- *I* and people's names always get a capital letter.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write some words by segmenting.
- Can say a simple idea aloud.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A sentence is a thought, dressed up properly. It wears a **capital letter as its hat** at the start, and a **full stop as its shoe** at the end. Say it out loud first ("The dog ran."), then write it: big letter to start, a tiny dot to finish. Every sentence needs both. We also give a capital "hat" to names (*Sam, London*) and to the word *I*, wherever they appear.

**Worked example.** Turn *the cat sat on the mat* into a proper sentence.
1. Say it aloud and check it makes sense ✓.
2. Capital letter at the start: **T**he…
3. Full stop at the end: …mat**.**
4. Result: *The cat sat on the mat.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Pets:** write one sentence about a pet — "My dog is big."
- **Days out:** "We went to the park."
- **Self:** "I like football." (capital *I*!)

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** no capital, no full stop ("the dog ran").
  **Why:** punctuation feels invisible/optional.
  **Fix:** the "hat and shoe" image; proofread every sentence: "Where's its hat? Where's its shoe?".
- **Wrong:** lowercase *i* for the word *I* ("i like…").
  **Why:** doesn't know the *I* rule.
  **Fix:** explicit rule: the word *I* is *always* a capital, even in the middle.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does every sentence start with? **A:** A capital letter.
- **Q:** Fix this: *sam likes jam* . **A:** *Sam likes jam.* (capital S, full stop).
- **Q:** Which word is always a capital letter? **A:** *I*.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`: show three sentences with the capital and full stop missing; child adds them. Then write one own sentence about today.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Capital letters and full stops***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 1, *Writing a sentence***.

## 7. Joining ideas with *and*

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 15 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar

**Prerequisites:** Capital letters and full stops (a sentence).

### Key concepts
- The little word **and** joins two ideas or two things into one sentence.
- It lets you write longer, more interesting sentences.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write a simple sentence with a capital and full stop.

### Teach it (the explanation)
*And* is a **joining word** (a conjunction). Instead of two short sentences — "I like cats. I like dogs." — you can glue them: "I like cats **and** dogs." You can also join two actions: "She ran **and** jumped." Don't put a full stop before *and*; the full stop only goes at the very end.

**Worked example.** Join "I had toast." and "I had juice."
1. Take the first idea: *I had toast*.
2. Add *and*: *I had toast and*…
3. Add the second: *…juice*.
4. Result: *I had toast and juice.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Breakfast:** what did you eat? "…and…".
- **Playground:** "I played football and tig."
- **Animals:** "The lion roars and runs."

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** uses *and* over and over ("I went and I saw and I ran and…").
  **Why:** *and* feels like a magic glue for everything.
  **Fix:** "two ideas, one *and*" — then a full stop and start fresh.
- **Wrong:** puts a full stop or capital before *and* ("I like cats. And dogs.").
  **Why:** treats the joined part as a new sentence.
  **Fix:** show *and* keeps the sentence going — no full stop until the end.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Join with *and*: "The sun is hot." / "The sky is blue." **A:** *The sun is hot and the sky is blue.*
- **Q:** True or false: you put a full stop before *and*. **A:** False.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`: give pairs of short sentences; child joins each with *and* into one sentence.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Joining words (conjunctions)***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 1 conjunction *and*** resources.

## 8. Plurals with *-s* and *-es*

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 15 · **Subject strand:** Spelling / Grammar

**Prerequisites:** Segmenting to spell.

### Key concepts
- **Plural** means more than one.
- Add **-s** to most nouns (*cat → cats*).
- Add **-es** when the word ends in *s, ss, sh, ch, x, z* (*bus → buses, fox → foxes*).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows a noun is a naming word for a person, place or thing.
- Can spell simple words.

### Teach it (the explanation)
When there's more than one of something, the word changes. Usually you just add an *s*: one *dog*, two *dogs*. But some words make a hissing/buzzing sound at the end (*bus, brush, box*) and an *-s* alone would be hard to say. So we add *-es* to give an extra little sound: *bus → buses*, *fox → foxes*, *dish → dishes*.

**Worked example.** Make *box* plural.
1. Listen to the ending: *box* ends in `x`.
2. `x` is one of the special endings → add **-es**.
3. *box → boxes*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Counting toys:** one car, two *cars*; one *brush*, two *brushes*.
- **At the zoo:** *foxes, boxes, dishes*.
- **Sweets:** one *sweet*, lots of *sweets*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *boxs, foxs, bushs*.
  **Why:** applies the default *-s* rule everywhere.
  **Fix:** teach the trigger endings (s, ss, sh, ch, x, z → *-es*); say it aloud to hear the extra syllable.
- **Wrong:** uses an apostrophe for a plural (*cat's* for two cats).
  **Why:** sees apostrophes near *s* and copies the pattern.
  **Fix:** plurals NEVER need an apostrophe — just *-s/-es*.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Make *cat* plural. **A:** *cats*.
- **Q:** Make *bush* plural. **A:** *bushes* (ends in *sh* → add *-es*).
- **Q:** True or false: two dogs is spelt *dog's*. **A:** False — *dogs*, no apostrophe.

### Activity / practice
`categorize`: sort nouns into "add *-s*" (*pen, book, tree*) and "add *-es*" (*glass, witch, box*), then write the plural.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Plurals (-s and -es)***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 1, *Plural noun suffixes***.

## 9. Predicting and talking about a story

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Reading comprehension / Spoken language

**Prerequisites:** none (a read-aloud comprehension lesson).

### Key concepts
- **Predicting** = using clues so far to guess what might happen next.
- Good readers think about the story, not just say the words.
- Talking about a book helps you understand it.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can listen to a story and recall what just happened.

### Teach it (the explanation)
When you read or listen to a story, your brain is allowed to guess what's coming — that's **predicting**. You use the **clues**: what the characters are like, what's happened, the pictures. "The wolf is creeping up to the door… what do you think will happen next?" A good prediction uses a reason: "He'll blow the house down, *because* he did it to the other piggies." Predictions don't have to be right — we read on to find out.

**Worked example.** Reading *The Three Little Pigs*, we stop at the straw house.
1. Clue: the wolf is huffing and puffing.
2. Clue: it's only a straw house — weak.
3. Prediction: "I think the house will fall down, *because* straw is not strong."

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Favourite stories:** pause a known tale and predict before turning the page.
- **Films/games:** "what will the baddie do next?" — same skill.
- **Cliffhangers:** predict the end of a comic strip.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** guesses something random with no link to the story (e.g. "a spaceship comes").
  **Why:** doesn't yet use textual clues.
  **Fix:** "use a clue from the story" — model "I think… *because* the book told me…".
- **Wrong:** thinks a prediction is "wrong" and gives up.
  **Why:** treats reading like a test.
  **Fix:** praise reasoned guesses; "great readers change their mind as they read on".

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a prediction? **A:** A good guess about what will happen next, using clues.
- **Q:** Give a reason word we use in predictions. **A:** *because*.
- **Q:** [After a story stop] What might happen next and why? **A:** any reasoned answer using a clue.

### Activity / practice
`speak` block: tutor reads to a stopping point and asks "What happens next, and *why* do you think that?" Child answers in a full sentence using *because*.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Reading: making predictions***.
- **CBeebies Bedtime Stories** — read-aloud stories to pause and predict.

## 10. Writing a short story (oral first)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition

**Prerequisites:** Capital letters and full stops, Joining ideas with *and*.

### Key concepts
- **Say it before you write it** — rehearse the sentence aloud first.
- A short story has a **beginning, middle and end** in order.
- Re-read your writing to check it makes sense.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write simple sentences with capitals and full stops.
- Can sequence two or three events.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Writers think and talk before they write. First, **say your sentence out loud** (this is "talk for writing") — if you can say it, you can write it. Tell your story in order: **first** something starts, **then** something happens, **at the end** it finishes. Write one sentence at a time, capital to start, full stop to end. When you've finished, **read it back** to check nothing is missing.

**Worked example.** Plan and write a tiny story about a lost dog.
1. Beginning (say it): "A little dog got lost." → write it.
2. Middle: "He looked and looked for his home." → write it.
3. End: "A girl helped him get back." → write it.
4. Read back: does it make sense, in order? ✓

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Pets/animals:** a lost or adventurous animal.
- **Superheroes:** a hero who saves the day in three steps.
- **Real day:** retell something that really happened today as a mini-story.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes one long run-on with no full stops.
  **Why:** transcription overload — too much at once.
  **Fix:** "one idea, one sentence" — say it, write it, full stop, next.
- **Wrong:** events out of order (end before middle).
  **Why:** hasn't planned the sequence.
  **Fix:** plan with "first / then / at the end" fingers before writing.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What should you do *before* you write a sentence? **A:** Say it out loud.
- **Q:** What three parts does a story have? **A:** Beginning, middle and end.
- **Q:** Why do we read our writing back? **A:** To check it makes sense.

### Activity / practice
`ordering` then `speak`: order three story sentences (beginning/middle/end), then the child says and writes their own three-sentence story about a chosen character.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *How to write a story***.
- **Pobble 365** — daily story-starter pictures for a talk-and-write prompt.
