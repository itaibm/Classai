# English — Year 6 (ages 10–11)

Year 6 is the summit of KS2. Grammar adds **semicolons, colons and dashes between clauses, the subjunctive form, the passive voice, hyphens, bullet points and the full range of cohesive devices**; spelling completes the **Y5/6 word list**; reading demands **evidence-based analysis, summarising across a whole text and evaluating an author's choices**; writing is **planned for audience and purpose, précised, and proof-read** to a high standard.

---

## 1. Semicolons, colons and dashes between clauses

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 parenthesis; clauses; co-ordination.

### Key concepts
- A **semicolon (;)** joins two closely-related **complete sentences** (independent clauses) without a conjunction.
- A **colon (:)** introduces something — a list, an explanation, or an example.
- A **dash (–)** can do a similar job to a colon or to mark a sudden break.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows what a main clause (a complete sentence) is.
- Can use commas, brackets and dashes for parenthesis.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Three "upgrade" marks for joining ideas:
- **Semicolon ;** — links **two full sentences** that are closely related, where a full stop would feel too final and *and* too weak: *"It was raining; we stayed inside."* Test: there must be a complete sentence on **both** sides.
- **Colon :** — **introduces** what follows: a list (*"You need three things: a pen, paper and a ruler."*), an explanation (*"He had one wish: to fly."*), or an example. The part **before** the colon is a complete sentence.
- **Dash –** — a more informal way to introduce or to make a dramatic pause: *"There was only one option left – run."*

**Worked example.** Join "The test was hard." and "Everyone passed."
1. They're closely related, both complete sentences.
2. Use a semicolon: *The test was hard; everyone passed.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Lists with a lead-in:** "We packed everything: …".
- **Dramatic reveals** in a story (dash).
- **Linking cause/contrast** ideas tightly (semicolon).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** uses a semicolon where there isn't a full sentence on both sides (*"I like apples; and oranges."*).
  **Why:** treats it like a comma.
  **Fix:** test both halves — each must stand alone as a sentence; no conjunction needed.
- **Wrong:** colon with an incomplete clause before it (*"Three things: …"* after *"You need:"* fine, but *"My favourites are: red"* — the *are* makes it clumsy).
  **Why:** colon used after an incomplete lead-in.
  **Fix:** make the part **before** the colon a complete statement.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What must be true on both sides of a semicolon? **A:** A complete sentence (independent clause).
- **Q:** Which mark introduces a list? **A:** A colon.
- **Q:** Join with a semicolon: "It was late." / "We went home." **A:** *It was late; we went home.*

### Activity / practice
`multipleChoice`/`fillBlank`: choose semicolon, colon or dash for ten sentences and justify each choice.

## 2. The passive voice

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 verbs; subject and object of a sentence.

### Key concepts
- In the **active voice**, the subject does the action (*The dog chased the cat*).
- In the **passive voice**, the action is done *to* the subject (*The cat was chased by the dog*).
- The passive is useful when the *doer* is unknown, unimportant, or deliberately hidden.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can identify the subject and verb of a sentence.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Most sentences are **active**: the subject does the verb — *"The chef cooked the meal."* In the **passive**, you flip it so the thing *receiving* the action goes first, and you use a form of *be* + past participle: *"The meal was cooked (by the chef)."* The doer (the chef) becomes optional and can vanish: *"The meal was cooked."*
Why use it?
- When you **don't know** who did it: *"The window was broken."*
- When the **doer doesn't matter**: *"The bridge was built in 1890."*
- In **formal/scientific** writing: *"The mixture was heated."*
- To **hide** the culprit: *"Mistakes were made."*

**Worked example.** Make active "Someone stole the jewels." passive.
1. Move the object (*the jewels*) to the front.
2. Use *was* + past participle (*stolen*): *The jewels were stolen.*
3. (The doer "someone" can be dropped — that's the point.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Mystery/whodunnit:** the passive hides the culprit.
- **Science reports:** "the liquid was poured…".
- **News headlines:** "A man has been arrested."

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks the passive is "wrong" or always worse.
  **Why:** told to "avoid the passive".
  **Fix:** it's a *choice* — great for hiding/de-emphasising the doer; just don't overuse it.
- **Wrong:** can't form it (leaves out *be* or uses wrong participle).
  **Why:** unfamiliar verb form.
  **Fix:** formula = subject + *was/were* + past participle (+ *by …*).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Is "The ball was kicked by Sam" active or passive? **A:** Passive.
- **Q:** Make "The cat ate the fish" passive. **A:** *The fish was eaten (by the cat).*
- **Q:** Why use the passive in "The window was broken"? **A:** The doer is unknown/unimportant.

### Activity / practice
`multipleChoice`/`fillBlank`: convert active sentences to passive and back; identify which version hides the doer.

### Resources

## 3. The subjunctive and formal vs informal language

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar / vocabulary

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 Standard English; modal verbs.

### Key concepts
- The **subjunctive** is a formal verb form for wishes, suggestions and hypotheticals: *"If I **were** you…", "I suggest he **be** told."*
- Writing has **registers**: formal (reports, letters of complaint) vs informal (texts, chats).
- Vocabulary and structures should match the audience and purpose.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows Standard English and verb forms.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The **subjunctive** is a special, formal verb form for things that are **imagined, wished, demanded or suggested** rather than real:
- "If I **were** rich, I'd…" (not "was" — it's hypothetical).
- "The Queen demands that the gate **be** opened." (not "is")
- "I suggest he **take** the earlier train." (not "takes")
It sounds formal and is mainly used in formal writing. More broadly, writers choose a **register** to fit the reader: formal (no contractions, Standard English, careful vocabulary, maybe passive/subjunctive) for official writing; informal (contractions, chatty, slang) for friends. The skill is **matching language to audience and purpose** — a complaint letter and a text to a mate read very differently.

**Worked example.** Make formal: "Hey, can you sort out my broken bike? Cheers."
- Formal version: *I am writing to request that my bicycle **be** repaired as soon as possible. I would be grateful for your prompt attention.* (subjunctive *be*, no contractions, formal vocabulary)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Letters of complaint** vs texts to friends.
- **Posh invitations:** "We request that you **be** present."
- **Wishes/daydreams:** "If I **were** a millionaire…".

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** "If I **was** you…" in formal writing.
  **Why:** uses everyday speech form.
  **Fix:** subjunctive uses *were* for hypotheticals — "If I **were** you".
- **Wrong:** mixes registers (slang in a formal letter, or stiff language in a friendly note).
  **Why:** doesn't consider audience.
  **Fix:** "who's reading this?" — match formality to the reader.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Subjunctive: "If I ___ you" (was/were)? **A:** *were*.
- **Q:** Is a text to a friend formal or informal? **A:** Informal.
- **Q:** Make "I want you to be quiet" formal/subjunctive. **A:** *I request that you be quiet.*

### Activity / practice
`multipleChoice`/`fillBlank`: choose the subjunctive form; rewrite an informal message in formal register.

## 4. Hyphens, bullet points and the colon for lists

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** VGP – punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Semicolons, colons and dashes.

### Key concepts
- A **hyphen (-)** joins words to avoid ambiguity (*man-eating shark* vs *man eating shark*) and in compound adjectives (*a well-known author*).
- A **colon** introduces a list; **bullet points** present items in a list clearly.
- Bullet points should be **consistent** in style.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can use colons and dashes.

### Teach it (the explanation)
- A **hyphen** is a short joining line *inside* a word group. It removes confusion: *"a man-eating shark"* (the shark eats men) vs *"a man eating shark"* (a man is eating shark). Use it in **compound adjectives** before a noun: *a well-known singer, a five-year-old child, a bright-blue coat*.
- A **colon** introduces a **list** after a complete sentence: *"Pack the following: a torch, a map and water."*
- **Bullet points** lay a list out clearly. Keep them **consistent** — all start the same way (all phrases, or all full sentences), often introduced by a colon.

**Worked example.** Punctuate this list with a colon and bullets.
> To make a den you will need:
> - some blankets
> - two chairs
> - a few pegs
(Lead-in is a complete sentence, colon introduces, bullets are consistent phrases.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Instructions / kit lists** with bullets.
- **Funny ambiguity:** "man-eating chicken" vs "man eating chicken".
- **Ages/measurements:** *a ten-year-old, a two-metre fence*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** confuses hyphen (-) with dash (–).
  **Why:** they look similar.
  **Fix:** hyphen joins words tightly (no spaces: *well-known*); dash separates with spaces and adds an aside.
- **Wrong:** inconsistent bullets (some phrases, some full sentences, random capitals).
  **Why:** no style rule applied.
  **Fix:** pick one style and keep all bullets the same.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Add a hyphen: a well known actor. **A:** *a well-known actor*.
- **Q:** What mark introduces a bulleted list? **A:** A colon.
- **Q:** Why hyphenate "man-eating"? **A:** To show *man* is what's being eaten / avoid confusion.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`: add hyphens to compound adjectives; rewrite a list with a colon and consistent bullet points.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Hyphens* and *Bullet points / colons in lists***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 6 hyphens and punctuation**.

## 5. The Y5/6 statutory word list and advanced spelling

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Spelling

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 silent letters and ei/ie.

### Key concepts
- Master the hardest common words on the Y5/6 list (*accommodate, conscience, embarrass, necessary, rhythm, definitely, separate, occur(red)*).
- Use **morphology** (root + prefix + suffix) and **mnemonics** to lock them in.
- The **-cious/-tious** and **-cial/-tial** endings follow root clues.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows spelling strategies and the Y3/4 list.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The Year 5/6 list is the "boss level" of primary spelling. Strategies:
- **Mnemonics** for doubled/tricky letters: *embarrass* ("really red cheeks" = 2 r's, 2 s's); *necessary* (1 c, 2 s); *because* (Big Elephants Can Always Understand Small Elephants).
- **Morphology:** break by root + affix — *interrupt* = *inter* (between) + *rupt* (break); *occur → occurred* (double the *r* before *-ed*).
- **-cious/-tious** (sound `/shus/`): often *-cious* if the root has *-ce* (*grace → gracious*), *-tious* if root has *-tion* (*ambition → ambitious*).
- **-cial/-tial:** usually *-cial* after a vowel (*official, special*), *-tial* after a consonant (*partial, essential*).

**Worked example.** Spell *occurred*.
1. Root: *occur* (2 c's, 1 r).
2. Add *-ed*: short vowel + single final consonant → **double the r** → *occurred*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Spelling-bee** of the trickiest words.
- **Etymology hunt:** find Latin/Greek roots (*rupt, struct, port*).
- **Mnemonic invention** challenge.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *embarass, neccessary, ocurred*.
  **Why:** wrong number of doubled letters.
  **Fix:** use the mnemonic; for *occurred*, double the *r* before *-ed*.
- **Wrong:** *delicous, ambicious* (wrong /shus/ spelling).
  **Why:** guessed without root clue.
  **Fix:** *-ce* root → *-cious* (*grace→gracious*); *-tion* root → *-tious* (*ambition→ambitious*).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Spell *embarrass*. **A:** e-m-b-a-r-r-a-s-s (2 r, 2 s).
- **Q:** *-cious* or *-tious*: from *ambition*? **A:** *ambitious*.
- **Q:** Add *-ed* to *occur*. **A:** *occurred*.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`/`speak`: dictate Y5/6 list words and `/shus/`, `/shal/` words; child writes, underlines the tricky part, and gives a mnemonic.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Tricky spellings (-cious/-tious, -cial/-tial)***.
- **Spelling Frame — Year 5/6 statutory word list** (free word lists & tests).

## 6. Summarising and analysing a whole text

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Reading comprehension

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 summarising, fact/opinion, comparing texts.

### Key concepts
- **Summarise** the main ideas drawn from **more than one paragraph** across a text.
- **Analyse** how the author's **word choices and structure** create meaning and effect.
- Support every point with **evidence** and explain the **impact** on the reader (PEE: Point–Evidence–Explain).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can infer with evidence, summarise paragraphs, tell fact from opinion.

### Teach it (the explanation)
At Year 6 you read like a critic. Two skills:
1. **Whole-text summary:** pull the main ideas from across the text — not paragraph by paragraph, but the overall argument or arc — and express it briefly.
2. **Analysis of author's craft:** explain *how* the writer achieves an effect. Use **PEE**: make a **Point**, give the **Evidence** (a quote), then **Explain** the effect on the reader. e.g. *Point:* the writer builds tension; *Evidence:* "the silence pressed against her ears"; *Explain:* the metaphor makes the silence feel heavy and threatening, so the reader feels the character's fear.

**Worked example.** Analyse word choice in "The waves *clawed* at the shore."
- **Point:** The writer makes the sea seem violent.
- **Evidence:** the verb "clawed".
- **Explain:** "clawed" suggests sharp claws and aggression, so the sea feels like a dangerous creature attacking the land.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Book club:** justify your view of a character with quotes.
- **Lyrics/poems:** analyse a powerful line.
- **Two articles** on one event — summarise and compare the bias.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** quotes evidence but doesn't explain the effect.
  **Why:** stops at finding the quote.
  **Fix:** finish the PEE — "this makes the reader feel/think…".
- **Wrong:** summary retells one part, not the whole text.
  **Why:** grabs a single section.
  **Fix:** "what is the *whole* text about overall?" — draw ideas from across it.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What do the letters PEE stand for? **A:** Point, Evidence, Explain.
- **Q:** [waves example] What effect does "clawed" create? **A:** Makes the sea seem violent/like an attacking creature.
- **Q:** Should a whole-text summary cover one paragraph or the main ideas across the text? **A:** The main ideas across the whole text.

### Activity / practice
`speak`/`fillBlank`: write a PEE paragraph analysing one word/phrase from a passage, then a one-sentence whole-text summary.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Analysing language* and *Summarising***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 6 reading comprehension**.

## 7. Précis: shrinking a longer passage

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 summarising; cohesion.

### Key concepts
- To **précis** is to rewrite a longer passage **much shorter**, keeping the meaning, in your own words.
- Cut repetition, examples and minor detail; keep the essential ideas.
- The result must still read as a fluent, cohesive piece.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can summarise and identify main ideas.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **précis** (pronounced "pray-see") is a tightly compressed version of a passage — say, three paragraphs shrunk into two or three sentences — that keeps the key meaning but in **your own words**. Steps:
1. Read the whole passage and find the **main ideas**.
2. Cross out repetition, examples, and side-details.
3. Rewrite the essentials in your own words, as fluent prose (not bullet points).
4. Check it still makes sense and flows — use cohesive devices to link the kept ideas.
Précising is brilliant for note-making, revision summaries, and tightening your own writing.

**Worked example.** Précis this:
- Original (40 words): *"The Romans were skilled builders. They built long, straight roads across their empire so their armies could march quickly. Many of these roads were so well made that parts of them still survive today, almost two thousand years later."*
- Précis (≈14 words): *"The Romans built durable straight roads for fast army travel; some still survive today."*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Revision notes:** shrink a textbook page.
- **Blurbs:** turn a long plot into a back-cover summary.
- **Tweet it:** say the gist in one short message.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** copies phrases word-for-word.
  **Why:** thinks précis = shortening by deleting.
  **Fix:** put it in your *own* words while keeping the meaning.
- **Wrong:** drops an essential idea while keeping a minor detail.
  **Why:** can't rank importance.
  **Fix:** ask "would the meaning change if I cut this?" Keep only ideas that pass.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a précis? **A:** A much shorter version that keeps the meaning, in your own words.
- **Q:** What should you cut first? **A:** Repetition, examples and minor details.
- **Q:** Should a précis use your own words? **A:** Yes.

### Activity / practice
Writing task: précis a given 50–60 word passage into ~15 words in your own words, keeping it fluent.

## 8. Planning, editing and proof-reading for audience and purpose

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition

**Prerequisites:** all earlier writing topics; cohesion; punctuation.

### Key concepts
- Strong writing is **planned** for a specific **audience and purpose**, drawing on models read.
- The **writing process** is plan → draft → **revise** (improve ideas/words) → **edit/proof-read** (fix spelling, punctuation, grammar) → publish.
- **Proof-reading** catches errors a writer often misses — read slowly, aloud, one thing at a time.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write multi-paragraph texts in a range of genres.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Excellent writers don't just write once. They:
1. **Plan** for audience & purpose: "Who reads this? What do I want it to do?" Choose the right genre, register and structure.
2. **Draft** to get ideas down (don't stop for spelling yet).
3. **Revise** — the big improvements: stronger vocabulary, varied sentences, better cohesion, "show don't tell", cut waffle.
4. **Proof-read/edit** — the polish: spelling, punctuation (capitals, full stops, apostrophes, commas), tense agreement, homophones (*their/there*).
**Proof-reading tip:** read it **aloud, slowly** — your ear catches missing words and run-ons. Check **one thing at a time** (e.g. one pass just for full stops/capitals, one for apostrophes).

**Worked example.** Proof-read this sentence: *"me and my freind was so happy we new wed won"*.
1. *me and my friend* → *My friend and I* (Standard English, *friend* spelling).
2. *was* → *were* (agreement).
3. *new* → *knew*; *wed* → *we'd* (homophone/contraction).
4. Capital + full stop.
→ *My friend and I were so happy; we knew we'd won.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Real audiences:** a letter to the council, a story for a younger sibling, a review for a website.
- **Be the editor:** proof-read a deliberately error-filled paragraph.
- **Publishing:** make a "best copy" of a piece to share.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** treats first draft as final.
  **Why:** doesn't value revising/editing.
  **Fix:** "great writing is re-writing" — always revise then proof-read.
- **Wrong:** writes the same for every reader.
  **Why:** ignores audience/purpose.
  **Fix:** decide audience and purpose *first*, and match register, vocabulary and structure.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name the two things you decide before writing. **A:** Audience and purpose.
- **Q:** What's the difference between revising and proof-reading? **A:** Revising improves ideas/words; proof-reading fixes spelling/punctuation/grammar.
- **Q:** Give one good proof-reading strategy. **A:** Read aloud slowly / check one thing at a time.

### Activity / practice
Editing task: proof-read a short error-filled paragraph (spelling, apostrophes, homophones, tense, capitals), then plan a piece by stating its audience, purpose and structure.
