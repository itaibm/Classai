# English — Year 2 (ages 6–7)

Year 2 secures phonics as a route to fluent reading (**Phase 6**), adds spelling rules for suffixes and homophones, introduces the apostrophe, the parts of speech (noun/verb/adjective/adverb), the four sentence types, and writing for different purposes. By the end, children read most words automatically and write joined-up sentences with growing accuracy.

---

## 1. Reading longer words and building fluency (Phase 6)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Word reading – phonics

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 Phase 5 phonics, blending and split digraphs.

### Key concepts
- Break long words into **syllables** (beats) to read them.
- Read more and more words **on sight** (automatically), which makes reading smooth.
- Use phonics as the trusty backup for any new word.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can blend digraphs and split digraphs.
- Reads CVC and simple CCVCC words confidently.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Big words look scary until you **chop them into beats** (syllables). Clap the word: *rab-bit* (2 claps), *fan-tas-tic* (3 claps). Read one beat at a time, then push them together. As you read more, lots of words become **sight words** — your eyes just *know* them, so you don't sound them out every time. That's fluency: reading smoothly, with expression, at a comfy pace.

**Worked example.** Read *sunset*.
1. Clap the syllables: *sun – set* (2 beats).
2. Read each beat: /s/ /u/ /n/ → "sun"; /s/ /e/ /t/ → "set".
3. Push together → "sunset".

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Dinosaurs:** *T-rex, ste-go-sau-rus* — chop the long names.
- **Football clubs / place names:** clap the syllables in team names.
- **Favourite book:** find and "beat-read" the longest word on a page.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** tries to read a long word in one go and gives up.
  **Why:** working memory overload.
  **Fix:** "clap it into beats first", read beat by beat.
- **Wrong:** reads word-by-word, robotic, no expression.
  **Why:** still decoding everything.
  **Fix:** re-read the sentence a second time for fluency; model expressive reading.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many syllables in *elephant*? **A:** 3 (*el-e-phant*).
- **Q:** Read *playground*. **A:** *play-ground* → "playground".
- **Q:** What does it mean to read a word "on sight"? **A:** Read it instantly without sounding out.

### Activity / practice
`ordering`/`speak`: clap-count syllables of 6 words and sort into 1/2/3-syllable columns, then read each aloud.

## 2. Adding suffixes: the spelling rules (-ing, -ed, -er, -est)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Spelling

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 plurals and segmenting.

### Key concepts
- A **suffix** is an ending added to a root word (*jump → jumping*).
- **Drop the *e***: words ending in *e* drop it before *-ing/-ed/-er/-est* (*hope → hoping*).
- **Double the consonant**: short-vowel CVC words double the last letter (*run → running*).
- ***y → i***: change *y* to *i* before most suffixes (*happy → happier*).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows short and long vowel sounds.
- Can add a simple *-s/-es*.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Adding an ending isn't always "just stick it on". Three rules sort out the tricky ones:
1. **Drop the e** — if the word ends in a silent *e*, drop it: *bake → baking*, *nice → nicer*.
2. **Double up** — a short word with one vowel and one consonant doubles the last consonant to protect the short sound: *hop → hopping* (not *hoping*!), *big → bigger*.
3. **y to i** — change a final *y* to *i* before the ending: *happy → happiest*, *cry → cried* (but keep *y* before *-ing*: *crying*).

**Worked example.** Add *-ing* to *run* and *-ed* to *smile*.
1. *run*: short vowel, one consonant → **double** → *running*.
2. *smile*: ends in *e* → **drop the e** → *smiled*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Sport:** *running, jumping, swimming, hopping* (lots of doublers).
- **Feelings:** *happier, sadder, funnier*.
- **Past adventures:** *we baked, we raced, we tried*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *runing, hoping* (for *running, hopping*).
  **Why:** didn't double the consonant, so the vowel reads long.
  **Fix:** test: short vowel + one final consonant → double it; compare *hoping*/*hopping*.
- **Wrong:** *bakeing, niceer*.
  **Why:** kept the silent *e*.
  **Fix:** "drop the e before a vowel suffix".
- **Wrong:** *happyer, cryed*.
  **Why:** kept the *y*.
  **Fix:** change *y → i* before *-er/-est/-ed* (but not before *-ing*).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Add *-ing* to *hop*. **A:** *hopping* (double the *p*).
- **Q:** Add *-ed* to *hope*. **A:** *hoped* (drop the *e*).
- **Q:** Add *-est* to *happy*. **A:** *happiest* (*y → i*).

### Activity / practice
`categorize`: sort root words by rule needed (drop-e / double / y→i / just add), then write each with *-ing* or *-ed*.

- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Adding -ing, -ed, -er and -est***.

## 3. Suffixes that make new words (-ful, -less, -ness, -ly, -ment)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Spelling / Vocabulary

**Prerequisites:** Adding suffixes: the spelling rules.

### Key concepts
- Suffixes can change what a word *means* and its job: *-ful* (full of), *-less* (without), *-ness* (a state), *-ly* (in this way), *-ment* (the result).
- For most of these, you just add the suffix to the whole root (*care → careful, careless*).
- Change *y → i* first if the word ends in *y* (*happy → happiness*).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows what a root word is.
- Can spell common root words.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Suffixes are word-builders. Take *help*: add *-ful* and it means "full of help" → *helpful*; add *-less* and it means "without help" → *helpless*. *-ness* turns a describing word into a "thing" word: *kind → kindness*. *-ly* tells *how*: *quick → quickly*. *-ment* names a result: *enjoy → enjoyment*. For most, just add it on. If the root ends in *y*, swap to *i* first: *happy → happiness, happily*.

**Worked example.** Build words from *hope*.
1. *hope* + *-ful* = *hopeful* (note: keep the *e* here — *-ful* starts with a consonant).
2. *hope* + *-less* = *hopeless*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Superheroes:** *powerful, fearless*.
- **Feelings:** *sadness, kindness, happily*.
- **Describing a place:** *colourful, useless, peaceful*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *-full* with two *l*s (*carefull*).
  **Why:** the word *full* has two *l*s, so it's copied.
  **Fix:** as a suffix it loses one *l*: *-ful*.
- **Wrong:** *happyness, happyly*.
  **Why:** didn't change *y → i*.
  **Fix:** root ending in *y* → change to *i* before the suffix.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Add *-ful* to *care*. **A:** *careful* (one *l*).
- **Q:** Make a "thing" word from *kind*. **A:** *kindness*.
- **Q:** Make *happy* into a "how" word. **A:** *happily*.

### Activity / practice
`matchPairs`/`fillBlank`: match roots to suffixes to make real words; then complete sentences ("She smiled ____" → *happily*).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Suffixes -ful, -less, -ly, -ment, -ness***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 2 spelling suffixes**.

## 4. Apostrophes: contractions and possession

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 capital letters and full stops.

### Key concepts
- An apostrophe in a **contraction** shows where letters are missing (*do not → don't*).
- An apostrophe for **possession** shows something belongs to someone (*the dog's bone*).
- An apostrophe is **NOT** used to make a plural.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write simple sentences.
- Knows what a noun is.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The apostrophe (') has two jobs.
**Job 1 — squashing words (contraction):** when we push two words together, some letters drop out, and the apostrophe stands in their place like a little gravestone: *do not → don't*, *I am → I'm*, *it is → it's*, *can not → can't*.
**Job 2 — belonging (possession):** add *'s* to show something belongs to someone: *the cat's tail* = the tail of the cat; *Sam's bag* = the bag belonging to Sam.
Important: apostrophes do **not** make plurals. Two cats = *cats*, never *cat's*.

**Worked example.** Contract *she will* and show possession for *Tom + book*.
1. *she will*: drop the *wi*, apostrophe in its place → *she'll*.
2. Tom's book: add *'s* → *Tom's book*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Speech:** "*I'm* tired", "*don't* go!" — how people really talk.
- **Belongings:** label classroom items — *Mia's pencil, the teacher's desk*.
- **Pets:** *the dog's lead, the rabbit's hutch*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** apostrophe in plurals ("apple's 50p", "two car's").
  **Why:** sees apostrophes near *s* and over-applies (the "greengrocer's apostrophe").
  **Fix:** plurals never take an apostrophe — only contraction or possession do.
- **Wrong:** confuses *its* / *it's*.
  **Why:** assumes possessive always has an apostrophe.
  **Fix:** *it's* = "it is"; *its* (no apostrophe) = belonging to it. Test by saying "it is".

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Contract *cannot*. **A:** *can't*.
- **Q:** Show the bag belongs to Sam. **A:** *Sam's bag*.
- **Q:** True or false: two dogs is written *dog's*. **A:** False — *dogs*.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`: rewrite sentences using contractions; add possessive *'s* to phrases ("the toy of the boy" → *the boy's toy*).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Apostrophes***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 2 apostrophes (contraction & possession)**.

## 5. Nouns, adjectives, verbs and expanded noun phrases

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 sentence basics.

### Key concepts
- **Noun** = a naming word (person, place, thing): *dog, school, ball*.
- **Adjective** = a describing word: *fluffy, huge, red*.
- **Verb** = a doing/being word: *run, is, jump*.
- An **expanded noun phrase** adds adjectives to a noun: *the fluffy, white dog*.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write a simple sentence.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Words have jobs. A **noun** names things (you can usually put *the* in front: *the cat*). An **adjective** describes a noun (*the GRUMPY cat*). A **verb** is the action or the being word (*the cat SLEEPS*). When you add describing words in front of a noun, you make an **expanded noun phrase** — it paints a clearer picture: not just *a dog*, but *a small, noisy dog*. Use a comma between two adjectives.

**Worked example.** Build up the noun *castle*.
1. Noun: *castle*.
2. Add an adjective: *a dark castle*.
3. Expand with two adjectives: *a dark, spooky castle*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Monsters:** describe a made-up monster with three adjectives.
- **Food:** *a gooey, chocolate cake*.
- **Sport:** verbs in a match commentary — *runs, kicks, scores*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** calls every word a "noun" or can't tell adjective from verb.
  **Why:** word-class labels are abstract.
  **Fix:** quick tests — noun: "can I put *the* before it?"; verb: "can I *do* it / put *I am ___ing*?"; adjective: "does it describe a noun?".
- **Wrong:** strings adjectives with no comma (*a big red fast car*).
  **Why:** doesn't know to separate them.
  **Fix:** comma between adjectives in a list (*a big, red, fast car*).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which word is the verb: *The happy dog ran*? **A:** *ran*.
- **Q:** Add two adjectives to *forest*. **A:** e.g. *a dark, silent forest*.
- **Q:** Is *quickly* a noun, verb or adjective? **A:** None — it's an adverb (describes the verb).

### Activity / practice
`categorize`: sort a word list into nouns / adjectives / verbs. Then `fillBlank`: expand three nouns into noun phrases.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Nouns, adjectives and verbs*** and ***Expanded noun phrases***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 2 word classes**.

## 6. The four sentence types (statement, question, command, exclamation)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar & punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 capital letters and full stops.

### Key concepts
- **Statement** — tells you something; ends in a full stop. *The sky is blue.*
- **Question** — asks something; ends in a question mark. *Is the sky blue?*
- **Command** — tells you to do something; often starts with a "bossy verb". *Shut the door.*
- **Exclamation** — shows strong feeling; starts with *What* or *How* and ends in *!* . *What a mess!*

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write a sentence with a capital and full stop.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Sentences come in four flavours, each with its own ending mark.
- A **statement** just tells: *Dogs bark.* → full stop.
- A **question** asks and needs a **question mark**: *Why do dogs bark?* → ?
- A **command** orders you about with a bossy verb at the front: *Sit down. Tidy up.*
- An **exclamation** bursts with feeling and (properly) starts with *What…* or *How…*: *What a big dog! How fast it runs!* → !

**Worked example.** Sort: "Stop!" / "Where are you?" / "I am tired."
1. *Stop!* → command (bossy verb, strong).
2. *Where are you?* → question (asks, ?).
3. *I am tired.* → statement (tells, full stop).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **A recipe / instructions:** all commands ("Mix the flour. Add the eggs.").
- **An interview:** all questions.
- **A surprise party:** exclamations ("What a surprise!").

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** ends a question with a full stop ("Where is it.").
  **Why:** forgets the special mark.
  **Fix:** "an asking sentence needs a ? — read it aloud, does your voice go up?".
- **Wrong:** calls anything with *!* an "exclamation sentence".
  **Why:** confuses an exclamation mark with an exclamation *sentence*.
  **Fix:** a true exclamation sentence starts with *What* or *How*; a command can also use *!*.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What mark ends a question? **A:** A question mark (?).
- **Q:** What type is "Close the window."? **A:** A command.
- **Q:** Turn "The cake is big." into an exclamation. **A:** *What a big cake!*

### Activity / practice
`categorize`/`multipleChoice`: sort eight sentences into the four types and add the correct end mark.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Statements, questions, commands and exclamations***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 2, *Sentence types***.

## 7. Joining sentences: subordination with *because, when, if, that*

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 joining with *and*; Nouns, verbs and adjectives.

### Key concepts
- **Co-ordination** (*and, but, or*) joins two equal ideas.
- **Subordination** (*when, if, that, because*) adds an idea that depends on the main one.
- *because* explains a reason and makes writing more interesting.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write a sentence and join with *and*.

### Teach it (the explanation)
You can grow a sentence in two ways. **Co-ordinating** words (*and, but, or*) glue two ideas that could each stand alone: "I like cats **but** I don't like spiders." **Subordinating** words (*because, when, if, that*) add a part that leans on the main idea: "I wore a coat **because** it was cold." The *because* part doesn't make sense alone — it explains the main part. Try these openers: *because, when, if, that*.

**Worked example.** Add a *because* part to "I was happy."
1. Main idea: *I was happy*.
2. Add a reason with *because*: *I was happy **because** it was my birthday.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Excuses/reasons:** "I was late **because**…".
- **Rules:** "**If** it rains, we stay in."
- **Feelings:** "I felt scared **when** the lights went out."

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** uses *because* but gives no real reason ("I was happy because happy").
  **Why:** treats it as just another joining word.
  **Fix:** *because* must answer "why?" — the part after it is the reason.
- **Wrong:** can't tell *but/or* from *because/when*.
  **Why:** all feel like "joining words".
  **Fix:** co-ordinators join equals; subordinators (*because/when/if*) add a dependent reason/time/condition.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Finish: "We ran inside because…". **A:** any reason, e.g. *…it started to rain.*
- **Q:** Which word shows a reason: *and, but, because*? **A:** *because*.
- **Q:** Join with *but*: "I like tea." / "I don't like coffee." **A:** *I like tea but I don't like coffee.*

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`: complete sentences with the best conjunction (*and / but / because / when / if*).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Conjunctions (co-ordination and subordination)***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 2 subordinating conjunctions**.

## 8. Homophones: *there / their / they're* and friends

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Spelling / Vocabulary

**Prerequisites:** Apostrophes: contractions and possession.

### Key concepts
- **Homophones** sound the same but are spelled differently and mean different things.
- You choose the spelling by the **meaning**: *there* (a place), *their* (belonging), *they're* (they are).
- Other pairs: *here/hear, to/two/too, see/sea, be/bee*.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can read these words.
- Understands apostrophe contractions (*they're = they are*).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Some words are sound-alikes (homophones) but have different jobs:
- ***there*** = a place or "there is" → "Put it over **there**." (it has the word *here* hiding in it — both about place).
- ***their*** = belongs to them → "**their** dog" (it has *heir*, someone who *owns* — about owning).
- ***they're*** = a squashed *they are* → "**They're** happy." (apostrophe = missing *a*).
Pick by meaning. Tip: if you can say "they are", use *they're*.

**Worked example.** Choose for: "___ going to ___ house over ___."
1. "they are going" → **they're**.
2. "the house belonging to them" → **their**.
3. "over [that place]" → **there**.
→ "**They're** going to **their** house over **there**."

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Texting:** these are the words people muddle most online — be the expert!
- **Directions:** "It's over there."
- **Family:** "their car, their dog".

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** uses *there* for all three.
  **Why:** they sound identical.
  **Fix:** test by meaning: place→*there*; belonging→*their*; "they are"→*they're*.
- **Wrong:** *to/too/two* mixed up.
  **Why:** all sound like "too".
  **Fix:** *two* = number; *too* = also/very (extra *o* for "extra"); *to* = the rest.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which means "belonging to them"? **A:** *their*.
- **Q:** Fill in: "___ my best friends." (they are) **A:** *They're*.
- **Q:** Choose: "I want to come ___ ." (also) **A:** *too*.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`/`multipleChoice`: choose the right homophone in 6 sentences (mix of there/their/they're and to/too/two).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Homophones (there, their, they're)***.
- **Grammarsaurus — homophones pack**.

## 9. Making inferences from a text

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Reading comprehension

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 predicting and talking about a story.

### Key concepts
- **Retrieval** = finding an answer the text says directly.
- **Inference** = working out something the text *doesn't* say, using clues + what you know.
- Always back up an inference with evidence: "I know this *because* the text says…".

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can read or listen to a short passage and recall events.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Some answers are easy to find — the text says them straight out. That's **retrieval** ("What colour was the coat?" → it says "red"). But great readers can also **read between the lines** — that's **inference**. The text might say "Tom's hands were shaking and he couldn't speak." It never says "Tom was scared" — but you *work it out* from the clues. Inference = clue in the text + what you already know.

**Worked example.** Passage: *"Mia opened the fridge, sighed, and shut it again. Her tummy rumbled."*
- Retrieval: "What did Mia open?" → the fridge (it says so).
- Inference: "How does Mia feel?" → hungry — because her tummy rumbled and there was nothing she wanted (clues + what we know about rumbling tummies).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Detectives:** inference is being a detective with clues.
- **Films:** "how do we know the character is sad?" — face, music.
- **Real life:** "Why is the floor wet?" — infer from clues.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** answers an inference question with "it doesn't say".
  **Why:** thinks every answer must be written down.
  **Fix:** "use the clues to work it out — what do the clues tell you?".
- **Wrong:** guesses with no evidence.
  **Why:** confuses inference with random imagination.
  **Fix:** require "because the text says…"; point to the clue word.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is inference? **A:** Working out something the text doesn't say, using clues.
- **Q:** [Tom passage] How do we know Tom is scared? **A:** His hands shake and he can't speak.
- **Q:** Is "What colour was the coat?" retrieval or inference? **A:** Retrieval.

### Activity / practice
`speak`/`multipleChoice`: read a 3-sentence passage; answer one retrieval and one inference question, justifying with a clue.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *Reading: finding clues / inference***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 2 reading comprehension**.

## 10. Writing instructions (a how-to)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition

**Prerequisites:** The four sentence types (commands); Adding suffixes.

### Key concepts
- Instructions tell someone **how to do** something, **in order**.
- They use **commands** (bossy verbs) and **time/order words** (*first, next, then, finally*).
- They often have a title, a "you will need" list, and numbered steps.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows what a command is.
- Can write sequenced sentences.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Instructions help someone do a job step by step. They start each step with a **bossy verb** (a command): *Cut, Mix, Stick, Pour*. They use **order words** so you don't get muddled: **First**… **Next**… **Then**… **Finally**…. Good instructions also tell you **what you need** before you start. Keep each step short and clear — the reader must be able to follow it without you there.

**Worked example.** Instructions: "How to make a jam sandwich."
- You will need: bread, butter, jam, a knife.
1. **First**, take two slices of bread.
2. **Next**, spread butter on one slice.
3. **Then**, spread jam on top.
4. **Finally**, put the slices together and eat!

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Cooking:** a real recipe the child likes.
- **Games:** "how to play tig / your favourite game".
- **Crafts:** "how to make a paper plane".

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes a story ("I made a sandwich and it was nice…") instead of instructions.
  **Why:** mixes recount with instruction.
  **Fix:** instructions tell *the reader* what to do — start each step with a bossy verb, not "I".
- **Wrong:** steps out of order or missing.
  **Why:** didn't plan the sequence.
  **Fix:** plan steps with *first/next/then/finally*; test by following them.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What kind of sentence starts each instruction step? **A:** A command (bossy verb).
- **Q:** Give two order words. **A:** any two of *first, next, then, finally*.
- **Q:** What should you list before the steps? **A:** What you will need.

### Activity / practice
`ordering` then writing: put four jumbled steps in order, then write your own set of instructions for a simple task using order words and commands.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — *How to write instructions***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 2, *Writing instructions***.
