## 1. Writing a balanced argument (discussion text)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Writing – composition (discussion genre)

**Prerequisites:** Writing to persuade (Y5), Cohesion: linking ideas across paragraphs (Y5).

### Key concepts
- A **balanced argument (discussion)** presents *both* sides fairly, then reaches a reasoned conclusion — unlike persuasion, which pushes one side.
- Structure: introduction (the question) → arguments **for** → arguments **against** → conclusion (your reasoned view).
- **Cohesion** between viewpoints uses connectives: *on the one hand… on the other hand…, however, in contrast, despite this*.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Paragraphing; forming an opinion; persuasive devices.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A discussion text is a fair courtroom, not a sales pitch. State the issue as a question, give the strongest points **for** (each in its own paragraph with a reason and example), then the strongest **against**, then a conclusion that weighs them and states *your* view with a "because". Keep the register fairly formal and the tone even-handed until the conclusion.

**Worked example.** Title: *Should children have homework?* For: "Firstly, homework reinforces what is learnt in class, because practice strengthens memory." Against: "However, some argue it reduces free time for play, which also supports learning." Conclusion: "On balance, a small amount of homework is valuable, because it builds habits without overwhelming children."

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A class debate topic the child cares about (screen time, school uniform, zoo animals).
- Two characters in a story who disagree.
- A "letters to the editor" page with opposing views.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes a persuasive piece for one side only. **Why:** confuses discussion with persuasion. **Fix:** insist on a paragraph for each side *before* the conclusion.
- **Wrong:** the conclusion just repeats the title. **Why:** avoids committing. **Fix:** model "On balance… because…" to weigh and decide.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How is a discussion text different from a persuasive one? **A:** It gives both sides fairly before concluding; persuasion argues one side.
- **Q:** Give a connective that introduces the opposing view. **A:** *However / on the other hand / in contrast.*
- **Q:** What two things should a conclusion do? **A:** Weigh both sides and state a reasoned opinion.

### Activity / practice
ordering: arrange jumbled paragraphs of a discussion text into for / against / conclusion; then fillBlank the linking connectives.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Discussion / balanced argument texts** (KS2).
- **Oak National Academy — Y6 Balanced argument writing**.

## 2. Editing and proofreading craft (revise vs edit)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Writing – editing & transcription

**Prerequisites:** Planning, editing and proof-reading for audience and purpose (Y6 — this deepens the *how*).

### Key concepts
- **Revising** improves *content* (word choice, sentence variety, clarity); **proofreading** fixes *errors* (spelling, punctuation, grammar). They are separate passes.
- Read **aloud** to catch missing words and clumsy sentences your eye skips.
- Use a fixed **checklist** (capitals, full stops, spelling of tricky words, sense) so nothing is missed.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Sentence punctuation; common spelling rules; the writing process.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Don't try to fix everything at once. First **revise**: is it clear, varied, vivid? Up-level weak words (*nice → delightful*), vary sentence openers, cut repetition. Then **proofread** as a separate pass, hunting only for errors — read aloud and point at each word. A symbol code (^ insert, // new paragraph, sp spelling) makes editing visible.

**Worked example.** Draft: "the dog was nice and it run fast and it was a good dog." Revise → combine and up-level: "The energetic spaniel sprinted across the field." Proofread the original's errors: missing capital *T*, *run → ran* (tense), repetition of "dog".

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "Be an editor" — fix a deliberately error-filled paragraph.
- Polishing the child's own story before "publishing" it.
- Spotting typos in a real menu or sign.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks editing = only fixing spelling. **Why:** conflates the two passes. **Fix:** name them — revise (improve) then proofread (correct).
- **Wrong:** reads silently and misses dropped words. **Why:** the brain auto-fills. **Fix:** read aloud, finger on each word.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What's the difference between revising and proofreading? **A:** Revising improves content/style; proofreading corrects errors.
- **Q:** Up-level "the food was nice." **A:** e.g. "The meal was delicious."
- **Q:** Fix: "we was late and we run home." **A:** "We were late and we ran home."

### Activity / practice
fillBlank / categorize: mark up an error-filled paragraph — sort changes into "revise" vs "proofread"; then correct.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Editing and proofreading** (KS2 writing).
- **Oak National Academy — Edit and improve writing** units.

## 3. Poetry forms (haiku, cinquain, kennings and free verse)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Writing – poetry

**Prerequisites:** How writers create effect — word choice and figurative language (Y4).

### Key concepts
- A **poetic form** has rules about lines, syllables or structure: **haiku** (3 lines, 5–7–5 syllables), **cinquain**, **kenning** (compound noun riddles like *book-worm*), **free verse** (no fixed pattern).
- Poems compress meaning — every word earns its place; sound (rhythm, rhyme, alliteration) matters.
- **Imagery** (similes, metaphors, the senses) makes a picture in the reader's mind.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Counting syllables; similes and metaphors; line breaks.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Forms are scaffolds, not cages. A **haiku** captures a single moment in 5–7–5 syllables — clap the syllables out. A **kenning** describes a thing without naming it using hyphenated compound words (a cat = *mouse-catcher, lap-warmer*). **Free verse** drops the rules but keeps deliberate line breaks and strong imagery. Read the form aloud to feel its shape.

**Worked example.** Haiku (count the claps): "Snow falls on the hill (5) / soft white blanket on the world (7) / footprints disappear (5)." A kenning poem for *dog*: "Tail-wagger, / ball-chaser, / night-guarder, / face-licker."

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Write a haiku about the weather right now.
- Kennings about the child's pet or favourite animal.
- Free-verse poem about a strong feeling (excitement before a match).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks all poems must rhyme. **Why:** common assumption. **Fix:** show haiku and free verse — rhythm and imagery matter more than rhyme.
- **Wrong:** miscounts syllables (counts letters or words). **Why:** confuses units. **Fix:** clap or tap the *beats* in each word ("blan-ket" = 2).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many syllables in each line of a haiku? **A:** 5, 7, 5.
- **Q:** Write a kenning for "the sun". **A:** e.g. *sky-lamp, day-bringer, warmth-giver.*
- **Q:** Does free verse have to rhyme? **A:** No.

### Activity / practice
speak / fillBlank: clap syllables and complete a 5–7–5 haiku; build a 4-line kenning poem for a chosen animal.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Poetry: haiku, kennings, free verse** (KS2).
- **The Poetry Society — Young Poets** resources.

## 4. Reading inference: the deep dive (reading between the lines)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Reading – comprehension (inference)

**Prerequisites:** Inference and retrieval with evidence (Y3), Making inferences from a text (Y2).

### Key concepts
- **Inference** = working out what the text *implies* but doesn't state, using **clues in the text + what you already know**.
- Good inference is **evidence-based**: you can point to the words that prove it ("It says… which tells me…").
- Different layers: inferring feelings, motives, what's about to happen, and what a character is *like*.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Retrieving stated facts; basic inference; some background/world knowledge.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Inference is detective work. The author leaves clues; you combine them with your own knowledge to deduce the hidden meaning. Use the formula **"It says ___, so I think ___ because ___."** "She slammed the door and refused her tea" → the *clue* is the slamming and refusing; *I know* people do that when upset → so she's angry, even though the word "angry" never appears.

**Worked example.** Text: "Tom's hands shook as he opened the envelope. He read it twice, then a slow smile spread across his face." Infer: he was **nervous** (shaking hands), then **happy/relieved** (the smile) — likely good news. Evidence: "hands shook" and "slow smile spread".

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "Whodunnit" clue-hunting in a short mystery.
- Reading a character's body language in a comic strip (no speech).
- Working out a friend's mood from how they act, not what they say.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** gives an answer the text doesn't support ("he was at the beach"). **Why:** guesses without evidence. **Fix:** demand the proving words — "show me where."
- **Wrong:** only retrieves stated facts, won't infer. **Why:** thinks the answer must be written. **Fix:** model "the text doesn't say it, but the clues tell us…".

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** "Her stomach rumbled as she stared at the empty fridge." How does she feel? **A:** Hungry — evidence: rumbling stomach, empty fridge.
- **Q:** What three things make a strong inference answer? **A:** A point, evidence from the text, and an explanation ("because").
- **Q:** Why isn't "I just think so" a good answer? **A:** It has no evidence from the text.

### Activity / practice
multipleChoice + speak: read short extracts, choose the best inference, then justify with "It says… so… because…".

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Inference and deduction** (KS2 reading).
- **Oak National Academy — Reading comprehension: inference** units.

## 5. Dictionary and thesaurus skills

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–5 (ages 7–10) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Vocabulary – reference skills

**Prerequisites:** Alphabetical order; the Year 3/4 word list & tricky spellings.

### Key concepts
- A **dictionary** gives meaning, spelling, word class and pronunciation; a **thesaurus** gives **synonyms** (and antonyms) to vary and sharpen writing.
- Look words up using **alphabetical order to the 2nd, 3rd, 4th letter**, and use **guide words** at the top of the page.
- Choosing a synonym means checking it fits the **meaning and tone** — not all synonyms are interchangeable.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The alphabet; first-letter alphabetical ordering; reading short definitions.

### Teach it (the explanation)
To find a word fast, order by the first letter, then the second, then the third (*chair* before *cheese* because *a* < *e*). Guide words show the first and last word on each page — if your word falls between them alphabetically, it's on that page. A thesaurus helps "up-level" vocabulary, but you must check the synonym actually fits: *cold* and *frosty* aren't the same in "a cold reply".

**Worked example.** Order alphabetically: *bread, brain, brick, bring* → *brain, bread, brick, bring* (compare the 3rd letter: a, e, i, i; then 4th letter for brick vs bring: c before n). Thesaurus: a better word for "said" in an argument → *snapped, retorted, argued* (not *whispered*).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Race to find a word in the dictionary (guide-word game).
- "Word of the day" — find its meaning and a synonym.
- Up-level overused words (*good, said, nice, big*) in the child's own writing.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** orders by first letter only and gives up. **Why:** doesn't compare further letters. **Fix:** "same first letter? look at the next one."
- **Wrong:** swaps in any synonym regardless of sense. **Why:** treats synonyms as identical. **Fix:** read the sentence back — does the new word still fit?

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which comes first: *plant* or *plane*? **A:** *plane* (4th letter: e before t).
- **Q:** What do guide words tell you? **A:** The first and last word on that page.
- **Q:** Give a stronger word for "happy". **A:** e.g. *delighted, thrilled, joyful.*

### Activity / practice
ordering: alphabetise five similar words; matchPairs synonyms; "find it on the page" guide-word task.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Using a dictionary and thesaurus** (KS2).
- **Oak National Academy — Dictionary skills** units.

## 6. Formal vs informal register (and Standard English)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** VGP – register & vocabulary

**Prerequisites:** The subjunctive and formal vs informal language (Y6 — this widens with vocabulary & contractions), Standard English and verb tenses (Y4).

### Key concepts
- **Register** is how formal your language is, matched to **audience and purpose** (a text to a friend vs a letter to the head teacher).
- Informal features: contractions (*don't*), slang, exclamation marks, chatty tone. Formal features: no contractions, precise vocabulary, the **passive** and sometimes the **subjunctive**, complete sentences.
- **Standard English** (agreed correct forms — *we were*, not *we was*) is expected in formal writing.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Contractions; the passive voice; audience and purpose.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Ask first: **who is this for and why?** That sets the register. A formal letter avoids contractions and slang, uses sophisticated vocabulary (*request* not *want*, *however* not *but anyway*), and may use the passive ("It is requested that…") or subjunctive ("If I were…"). Informal writing is relaxed and personal. Both are correct *in the right place* — the skill is matching register to situation.

**Worked example.** Informal (to a friend): "Hey! Wanna come to mine Saturday? It'll be class!" Same message, formal (to a teacher): "I would like to invite you to an event at my home on Saturday. I hope you will be able to attend." Note: no contractions, precise vocabulary, full sentences.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Rewrite a text message as a formal email.
- A character who speaks differently to friends vs to the king.
- Spotting register in real letters, adverts and texts.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** uses contractions/slang in a formal letter. **Why:** writes how they speak. **Fix:** a "formal toolkit" checklist — expand contractions, swap slang for precise words.
- **Wrong:** thinks formal = always "better". **Why:** over-applies. **Fix:** register must *match audience*; informal is right for a friend.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Make formal: "I wanna ask if you can come." **A:** "I would like to ask whether you are able to attend."
- **Q:** Name two features of informal writing. **A:** Contractions and slang/chatty tone.
- **Q:** Standard English form of "we was happy"? **A:** "We were happy."

### Activity / practice
categorize: sort phrases into formal / informal; fillBlank: rewrite an informal note as a formal letter.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Formal and informal language / Standard English** (KS2).
- **Oak National Academy — Y6 Formal and informal writing**.

## 7. Paragraphing and cohesion (organising and linking ideas)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Writing – structure & cohesion

**Prerequisites:** Organising writing into paragraphs (Y3), Cohesion: linking ideas across paragraphs (Y5).

### Key concepts
- A **paragraph** is a group of sentences about **one idea**; start a new paragraph when the **topic, time, place or speaker** changes (TiPToP).
- **Cohesion** is the glue: **topic sentences**, **connectives/adverbials** (*meanwhile, in addition, as a result*), and **pronouns** that refer back without repeating.
- Across a whole text, paragraphs should **flow in a logical order** and link to each other.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Writing in sentences; fronted adverbials; basic paragraphs.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Each paragraph opens with a **topic sentence** that says what it's about, then develops it. Start a new paragraph on **TiPToP** — a change of **Time, Place, Topic or Person** (new speaker). Link paragraphs with cohesive devices: time adverbials (*later, soon after*), logical connectives (*therefore, however, in addition*), and pronouns that point back ("The dragon roared. **It** had not eaten for days."). This stops writing feeling like a disconnected list.

**Worked example.** Two ideas, two paragraphs, linked: "First, the team trained hard every morning. **As a result**, by the final they were fitter than ever. **However**, the opposition had a secret weapon…" — the connectives *as a result* and *however* glue the ideas and signal the relationship.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sorting a jumbled news report back into sensible paragraphs.
- Improving a "wall of text" with no paragraph breaks.
- Spotting paragraph breaks in a favourite book.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** one giant paragraph / random breaks. **Why:** no rule for breaking. **Fix:** teach TiPToP — break on a change of time, place, topic or person.
- **Wrong:** repeats the noun every sentence ("Sam… Sam… Sam"). **Why:** no cohesion. **Fix:** use pronouns and connectives to link back.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** When do you start a new paragraph? **A:** On a change of Time, Place, Topic or Person (TiPToP).
- **Q:** What is a topic sentence? **A:** The opening sentence that tells you what the paragraph is about.
- **Q:** Give a connective that shows a result. **A:** *As a result / therefore / so / consequently.*

### Activity / practice
ordering: reassemble jumbled paragraphs into logical order; fillBlank the linking connectives between them.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Paragraphs and cohesion** (KS2 writing).
- **Oak National Academy — Organising paragraphs / cohesion** units.

## 8. Apostrophes mastery: the its / it's trap and tricky possessives

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** VGP – punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Apostrophes: contractions and possession (Y2), The possessive apostrophe with plural nouns (Y4).

### Key concepts
- Two apostrophe jobs: **contraction** (a letter is missing: *do not → don't*) and **possession** (belonging: *the dog's bone*).
- The big trap: **it's = it is/it has**; **its = belonging to it** (no apostrophe — like *his/hers*).
- Plurals don't take apostrophes (*apples*, not *apple's*) — the "greengrocer's apostrophe" error.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Singular vs plural; basic contractions and possession.

### Teach it (the explanation)
For contractions, the apostrophe marks **missing letters** — *can't* = *cannot*. For possession, add **'s** to a singular owner (*Sam's book*), and just **'** after a plural that already ends in s (*the girls' team*). The famous trap: test **it's** by expanding it to **"it is"** — if that makes sense, keep the apostrophe; if it means *belonging to it*, use **its** (no apostrophe). Never use an apostrophe just to make a plural.

**Worked example.** "The dog wagged **its** tail because **it's** happy." Test: "it is happy" ✓ (so *it's*), but "belonging to it tail" → *its* (no apostrophe). Plural: "I bought three **apples**" — *not* "apple's".

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Spot the wrong apostrophe on real shop signs ("Carrot's 50p").
- A "contraction detective" — find the missing letters.
- Editing the child's own writing for stray apostrophes.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes "the cat licked it's paw". **Why:** assumes possession always needs an apostrophe. **Fix:** the "it is" test — it fails here, so use *its*.
- **Wrong:** "banana's for sale". **Why:** thinks a plural needs an apostrophe. **Fix:** plurals just add s, no apostrophe.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** *It's / Its* raining and the cat lost *it's / its* toy. **A:** It's (it is) raining; its (belonging) toy.
- **Q:** Add the apostrophe: "the boys coats" (more than one boy). **A:** the boys' coats.
- **Q:** Is "I like apple's" correct? **A:** No — *apples* (plural, no apostrophe).

### Activity / practice
categorize: sort sentences into "needs apostrophe" / "no apostrophe"; fillBlank the *its/it's* trap.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Apostrophes (contraction, possession, its/it's)** (KS2).
- **Oak National Academy — Apostrophes** units.

## 9. Figurative language and imagery (similes, metaphors, personification)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Writing & reading – language for effect

**Prerequisites:** How writers create effect — word choice and figurative language (Y4 — this widens the toolkit and analysis).

### Key concepts
- **Simile**: compares using *like* or *as* (*as quiet as a mouse*). **Metaphor**: says one thing *is* another (*the classroom was a zoo*). **Personification**: gives human qualities to non-human things (*the wind whispered*).
- These create **imagery** — pictures and feelings in the reader's mind.
- In reading, you can **analyse** why a writer chose an image and the **effect** it has.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Adjectives and verbs; basic similes; reading for meaning.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Figurative language paints pictures words alone can't. A **simile** uses *like/as*; a **metaphor** is bolder — it claims a thing *is* something else; **personification** lets objects act like people. When writing, choose images that fit the mood (*the trees were skeletons against the grey sky* = eerie). When reading, ask "**what picture does this create, and why did the author choose it?**"

**Worked example.** Describe a storm three ways: simile — "The thunder was **as loud as** a drum"; metaphor — "The sky **was** an angry beast"; personification — "The lightning **clawed** at the clouds." Each makes the storm feel more alive and threatening.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Describe the child's favourite place using all three devices.
- Hunt for figurative language in song lyrics or a poem.
- "Show don't tell" a feeling (fear) with an image instead of the word.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** calls a metaphor a simile (or vice versa). **Why:** confuses the two. **Fix:** simile has *like/as*; metaphor says it *is*.
- **Wrong:** takes a metaphor literally ("the classroom isn't really a zoo"). **Why:** misses the comparison. **Fix:** "it doesn't mean it *is* — it means it's *as chaotic as*."

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Simile or metaphor: "The moon was a silver coin." **A:** Metaphor (says it *is*).
- **Q:** Write a simile for "very fast". **A:** e.g. "as fast as lightning."
- **Q:** What is personification? **A:** Giving human qualities/actions to non-human things.

### Activity / practice
categorize: sort examples into simile / metaphor / personification; speak: invent one of each to describe the weather today.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Similes, metaphors and personification** (KS2).
- **Oak National Academy — Figurative language / writing to describe** units.

## 10. Reading non-fiction: skimming, scanning and retrieval

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Reading – non-fiction & retrieval

**Prerequisites:** Inference and retrieval with evidence (Y3), Summarising the main idea (Y4).

### Key concepts
- **Skim** to get the gist fast (read headings, first lines, key words); **scan** to find a specific fact (run your eye for a name, number or word).
- Non-fiction has **text features** that help you navigate: headings, subheadings, captions, glossary, index, bold key words.
- **Retrieval** questions want the stated fact, copied or paraphrased accurately — often "find and copy".

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Decoding fluently; alphabetical order (for an index); the idea of a heading.

### Teach it (the explanation)
You don't read every word of an information text. **Skim** first — eyes over headings and topic sentences to learn what it's about. To answer a question, **scan**: decide the key word you're hunting (a date, a name) and sweep for it. Use the **contents, index and glossary** to jump to the right place. For "find and copy" questions, copy the exact words that answer the question.

**Worked example.** Question: "How long can a giant tortoise live?" Strategy: scan the text for a *number + years* near the word "tortoise" or "live"; find "up to 150 years"; answer **"150 years"** — copied accurately from the text.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Race to find a fact in a non-fiction book using the index.
- Use a TV guide or recipe (real scanning).
- Fact-finding for the child's own topic of interest (dinosaurs, space).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** reads every word slowly to find one fact. **Why:** doesn't distinguish skim/scan. **Fix:** name the key word and sweep for *just* that.
- **Wrong:** "find and copy" answer is paraphrased and loses the exact word. **Why:** doesn't read the instruction. **Fix:** "find and copy" = copy the precise words.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What's the difference between skimming and scanning? **A:** Skim = gist quickly; scan = hunt for a specific fact.
- **Q:** Which text feature helps you find a topic's page quickly? **A:** The contents page or index.
- **Q:** What does a glossary do? **A:** Explains the meanings of key/technical words.

### Activity / practice
multipleChoice + fillBlank: scan a short fact box to answer "find and copy" questions; locate a topic via a mock index.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Skimming, scanning and non-fiction features** (KS2).
- **Oak National Academy — Reading non-fiction / retrieval** units.

## 11. Speech punctuation mastery (the new-speaker rule & punctuation inside the marks)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** VGP – punctuation (direct speech)

**Prerequisites:** Punctuating direct speech (inverted commas) (Y3), Integrating dialogue to show character (Y5).

### Key concepts
- The spoken words go **inside inverted commas** ("…"), and the **end punctuation goes inside** them too.
- A **reporting clause** (*she said*) is separated by a comma; **a new speaker = a new line** (new paragraph).
- Dialogue should reveal **character** — *how* it's said (the verb and any action) matters.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Basic inverted commas; sentence punctuation; the idea of a "speech verb".

### Teach it (the explanation)
Punctuate speech with a checklist: open the inverted commas, capital letter to start the spoken words, end punctuation (. ? !) **inside** the closing marks, then the reporting clause. If the reporting clause comes first, put a comma before the speech: `Sam shouted, "Look out!"`. The golden layout rule: **every time a new person speaks, start a new line.** Vary speech verbs (*whispered, demanded, muttered*) to show character.

**Worked example.** "Where have you been?" demanded Mum. // "I got lost," whispered Ben, staring at his feet. — note: each speaker on a new line; the ? and , sit *inside* the inverted commas; capital letters start each piece of speech.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Turn a comic strip's speech bubbles into punctuated dialogue.
- Act out a short conversation, then write it down correctly.
- Improve flat dialogue by choosing vivid speech verbs.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** puts the comma/full stop outside the inverted commas. **Why:** treats the marks as decoration. **Fix:** "the punctuation belongs to the spoken words, so it goes *inside*."
- **Wrong:** runs two speakers together in one paragraph. **Why:** doesn't know the new-line rule. **Fix:** "new speaker, new line — every time."

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Punctuate: i'm hungry said Tom. **A:** "I'm hungry," said Tom.
- **Q:** Where does the question mark go in spoken speech? **A:** Inside the closing inverted commas.
- **Q:** What do you do when a different person starts speaking? **A:** Start a new line (new paragraph).

### Activity / practice
fillBlank: punctuate three lines of unpunctuated dialogue; speak then write a short two-character exchange with the new-line rule.

## 12. Root words, prefixes and suffixes (morphology to unlock new words)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Spelling & vocabulary – morphology

**Prerequisites:** Prefixes that change meaning (Y3), Suffixes -ly and -ous (Y3).

### Key concepts
- A **root** carries the core meaning; a **prefix** adds before it, a **suffix** after — together they build and decode words.
- Knowing roots and affixes lets you **work out unfamiliar words** (*un + help + ful = unhelpful*) and spell longer ones.
- Some roots come from **Latin/Greek** (*aqua* = water, *port* = carry, *tele* = far) and appear across many words.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Some common prefixes (*un-, dis-, re-*) and suffixes (*-ful, -less, -ly*).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Most long words are **built**, not memorised whole. Find the **root** (the meaning core), then peel off the prefix and suffix. *Unbelievable* = *un* (not) + *believe* + *able* (can be). Greek/Latin roots are reusable keys: *aqua* (water) gives *aquarium, aquatic*; *port* (carry) gives *transport, portable, import*. Spotting the root helps you both **read** and **spell** an unfamiliar word.

**Worked example.** Break down *transportation*: prefix *trans-* (across) + root *port* (carry) + suffix *-ation* (the act of) → "the act of carrying across". And build from *help*: *helpful, helpless, unhelpful, helper* — one root, four words.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A "word-building factory" — make as many words as possible from one root.
- Spot the Latin/Greek root in subject words (*aquarium, telescope, dictionary*).
- Crack a long, scary-looking word by peeling off its parts.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** splits the word wrongly (*un + iverse*). **Why:** doesn't know the real root. **Fix:** check the *meaning* core — *universe* isn't *un + iverse*.
- **Wrong:** doubles or drops letters wrongly when adding a suffix. **Why:** forgets the suffix rules. **Fix:** revisit drop-e / double-consonant / y→i rules.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Break *disappear* into its parts. **A:** *dis-* (not) + *appear* (root).
- **Q:** What does the root *aqua* mean? **A:** Water.
- **Q:** Build three words from the root *play*. **A:** e.g. *player, playful, replay.*

### Activity / practice
categorize / matchPairs: split words into prefix + root + suffix; match Greek/Latin roots to their meanings and example words.
