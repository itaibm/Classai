# English — Year 4 (ages 8–9)

Year 4 completes Lower KS2. Spelling adds the *-ssion/-cian* and *-ous* families and finishes the **Y3/4 word list**; grammar embeds **fronted adverbials (with a comma)**, the **possessive apostrophe with plurals**, expanded noun phrases and Standard English; reading deepens **inference, summarising and how language creates effect**; writing develops **settings, characters and plot** and non-fiction with headings.

---

## 1. Fronted adverbials and the comma

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar & punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 adverbs and prepositions.

### Key concepts
- An **adverbial** is a word or phrase that tells *how, when or where* (*after lunch, suddenly, in the dark forest*).
- A **fronted adverbial** is one moved to the **start** of the sentence.
- A fronted adverbial is followed by a **comma**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows adverbs and prepositions.
- Can write full sentences.

### Teach it (the explanation)
An **adverbial** adds detail about *when, where or how* something happens. Usually it can go in different places. When we put it at the **front** of the sentence, it's a **fronted adverbial**, and we mark it off with a **comma**:
- Time: **Later that day,** we set off.
- Place: **In the deep, dark cave,** something stirred.
- Manner: **Without a sound,** she crept forward.
- Frequency: **Every morning,** the rooster crowed.
The comma tells the reader to pause after the scene-setting phrase. Fronted adverbials make writing flow and stop every sentence starting "The…".

**Worked example.** Front the adverbial in "We ate breakfast quickly before school."
1. Find the adverbial of time: *before school*.
2. Move it to the front: *Before school,*…
3. Add the comma: *Before school, we ate breakfast quickly.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Adventure stories:** "Deep in the jungle, …", "Suddenly, …".
- **Daily routine:** "Every morning, …", "After lunch, …".
- **Sport:** "In the final minute, …".

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** forgets the comma (*Later that day we left*).
  **Why:** doesn't hear the pause.
  **Fix:** "front it, then comma" — read aloud and pause where the comma goes.
- **Wrong:** thinks any opening word is a fronted adverbial (*The dog…*).
  **Why:** confuses the subject with an adverbial.
  **Fix:** an adverbial tells when/where/how — *The dog* is the subject, not an adverbial.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What punctuation follows a fronted adverbial? **A:** A comma.
- **Q:** Add a fronted adverbial of time to "the rain began". **A:** e.g. *Suddenly, the rain began.*
- **Q:** Is "In the cave," a fronted adverbial of time, place or manner? **A:** Place.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`/`ordering`: rewrite five sentences moving the adverbial to the front with a comma; sort adverbials by time/place/manner.

## 2. The possessive apostrophe with plural nouns

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 apostrophes; Year 1 plurals.

### Key concepts
- **Singular possession:** add **'s** (*the dog's lead* = one dog).
- **Plural possession** (regular plurals ending in *s*): add just an **apostrophe after the s** (*the dogs' leads* = many dogs).
- **Irregular plurals** (not ending in *s*): add **'s** (*the children's toys*).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can use a singular possessive apostrophe.
- Knows regular and some irregular plurals.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The apostrophe shows belonging — and *where* it goes tells you **how many owners** there are.
- One owner: apostrophe **before** the *s* → *the girl's bag* (one girl).
- Many owners (regular plural already ends in *s*): apostrophe **after** the *s*, no extra *s* → *the girls' bags* (lots of girls).
- Irregular plural (no *s*, like *children, men, women, people*): treat like singular → add **'s** → *the children's coats, the men's room*.
Trick: find the owner word first; if it already ends in *s* (plural), just add an apostrophe after it.

**Worked example.** "Toys belonging to the babies."
1. Owner = *babies* (plural, ends in *s*).
2. Add apostrophe **after** the *s*: *babies'*.
3. → *the babies' toys*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Sports teams:** *the players' kit, the team's badge*.
- **Family:** *my parents' car, the children's room*.
- **Signs:** spot apostrophes on real shop/road signs and judge if they're right.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *the dogs's leads* or *the childrens' toys*.
  **Why:** adds *'s* to a word that already ends in *s*, or apostrophe-after on an irregular plural.
  **Fix:** if the plural ends in *s* → apostrophe after; if it doesn't (children) → *'s*.
- **Wrong:** apostrophe for a plain plural (*the dog's are barking*).
  **Why:** confuses plural with possessive.
  **Fix:** ask "is something belonging here?" If not, no apostrophe.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Where does the apostrophe go: toys belonging to many boys? **A:** *the boys' toys* (after the s).
- **Q:** Coats belonging to the children? **A:** *the children's coats*.
- **Q:** Is *cats* in "the cats are asleep" possessive? **A:** No — it's a plain plural, no apostrophe.

### Activity / practice
`multipleChoice`/`fillBlank`: place the apostrophe correctly for singular, regular-plural and irregular-plural owners.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Apostrophes for possession (plurals)***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 4 possessive apostrophe**.

## 3. Standard English and verb tenses

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 past/present tense; spoken Standard English.

### Key concepts
- **Standard English** is the form used in writing and formal speech.
- Subject–verb agreement: *I was / we were*, *he doesn't* (not *he don't*), *I did* (not *I done*).
- Use the correct **past tense** of irregular verbs (*went, saw, caught*, not *goed, seed, catched*).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write in past and present tense.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Everyone has a home dialect — that's lovely. But for writing and formal speaking we use **Standard English**, the agreed "school/news" form. Two things to watch:
1. **Agreement:** the verb must match the subject — *we **were*** (not *we was*), *she **doesn't*** (not *she don't*), *they **aren't*** (not *they ain't*).
2. **Irregular past tenses:** many common verbs don't just add *-ed*: *go → went*, *see → saw*, *catch → caught*, *bring → brought*, *do → did*, *come → came*. Learn these, don't "regularise" them (*goed, catched* are wrong).

**Worked example.** Fix "We was happy and I done my best."
1. *We was* → *We **were*** (agreement).
2. *I done* → *I **did*** (irregular past).
3. → *We were happy and I did my best.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Writing a letter** to someone important (formal English).
- **News reporting** — read like a newsreader.
- **Editing dialogue** so the *narrator* is Standard but a character can speak naturally.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *we was, they was*.
  **Why:** dialect agreement.
  **Fix:** *I/he/she/it was*; *we/you/they **were***.
- **Wrong:** *goed, catched, seed*.
  **Why:** over-applies the *-ed* rule.
  **Fix:** learn irregular past forms as set pairs.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Standard form: "they ___ at the park." (was/were) **A:** *were*.
- **Q:** Past tense of *catch*? **A:** *caught*.
- **Q:** Fix: "He don't like it." **A:** *He doesn't like it.*

### Activity / practice
`multipleChoice`/`fillBlank`: correct ten non-Standard sentences; match irregular verbs to their past tense.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Standard English* and *Verb tenses***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 4 Standard English**.

## 4. The /shun/ and /zhun/ spelling families (-tion, -sion, -ssion, -cian)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Spelling

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 suffix families.

### Key concepts
- The ending sound `/shun/` is spelled four common ways.
- **-tion** is the most common (*station, attention*); **-ssion** after a short vowel-ish root (*mission, discussion*); **-sion** (*division, confusion*, often `/zhun/`); **-cian** for people/jobs (*musician, magician*).
- Look at the root to choose the spelling.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can read multi-syllable words.

### Teach it (the explanation)
That `/shun/` ending hides four spellings. Clues from the root help:
- **-tion** — the default; often the root ends in *-t* or *-te*: *act → action, complete → completion*.
- **-ssion** — root often ends in *-ss* or *-mit*: *discuss → discussion, permit → permission*.
- **-sion** — root often ends in *-d, -de, -se*; the sound may be `/zhun/`: *divide → division, confuse → confusion, explode → explosion*.
- **-cian** — for a person/job, root ends in *-c* or *-cs*: *music → musician, magic → magician, politics → politician*.

**Worked example.** Spell the noun from *divide* and *discuss*.
1. *divide* ends in *-de* and sounds `/zhun/` → **-sion** → *division*.
2. *discuss* ends in *-ss* → **-ssion** → *discussion*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **TV/quiz:** *television, competition, champion(ship)*.
- **Jobs:** *musician, electrician, magician*.
- **Maths:** *division, addition, subtraction*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** spells everything *-tion* (*divition, musition*).
  **Why:** *-tion* is most common, so over-used.
  **Fix:** use root clues — jobs end *-cian*; *-de/-se* roots take *-sion*; *-ss* roots take *-ssion*.
- **Wrong:** *discusion* (one *s*).
  **Why:** misses the double *s* from the root *discuss*.
  **Fix:** keep the root's double *s* → *-ssion*.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Noun from *act*? **A:** *action*.
- **Q:** A person who plays music? **A:** *musician*.
- **Q:** Spell the maths word for sharing into groups. **A:** *division*.

### Activity / practice
`categorize`: sort `/shun/` words into the four spellings; `fillBlank` from root words.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Words ending in -tion, -sion, -ssion, -cian***.
- **Spelling Frame — Year 3/4 /shun/ rules**.

## 5. Summarising the main idea

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Reading comprehension

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 inference and retrieval; paragraphs.

### Key concepts
- **Summarising** = saying what a text is *mostly* about, briefly, in your own words.
- Find the **main idea** of each paragraph, then combine them.
- Leave out small details and examples; keep the big points.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can read a multi-paragraph text.
- Understands paragraphs each hold one idea.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **summary** is the short version — the headline, not the whole story. To summarise:
1. Read the paragraph and ask "**What is this mostly about?**" — that's the **main idea**.
2. Say it in **one sentence**, in your own words.
3. Do this for each paragraph, then join the main ideas into a few sentences.
Drop the tiny details and examples — keep only what matters. A good test: could someone who hasn't read it understand the gist from your summary?

**Worked example.** Summarise a paragraph about hedgehogs.
- Paragraph (in full): "Hedgehogs are small, spiny mammals. They sleep all day and hunt insects at night. In winter they hibernate, curling up in leaves to stay warm."
- Main idea in one sentence: *Hedgehogs are night-time insect hunters that hibernate in winter.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Recap a film/book** for a friend in one sentence.
- **Headline writing:** turn a story into a newspaper headline.
- **Fact files:** summarise each section into a heading.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** retells *everything*, as long as the original.
  **Why:** thinks summarising = copying.
  **Fix:** "the BIG point only — one sentence per paragraph".
- **Wrong:** picks a tiny detail as the main idea.
  **Why:** grabs the first/most interesting fact.
  **Fix:** ask "what is the *whole* paragraph mostly about?" not "what's one thing in it?".

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a summary? **A:** A short version saying what a text is mostly about.
- **Q:** [hedgehog paragraph] Summarise it in one sentence. **A:** Hedgehogs hunt insects at night and hibernate in winter.
- **Q:** Should a summary include every example? **A:** No — keep only the main points.

### Activity / practice
`speak`/`fillBlank`: read a 2–3 paragraph text; write a one-sentence summary for each paragraph, then a single overall summary.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Summarising a text***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 4 reading comprehension**.

## 6. How writers create effect (word choice and figurative language)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Reading comprehension / Vocabulary

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 inference; expanded noun phrases.

### Key concepts
- Writers **choose words on purpose** to make you feel or picture something.
- **Simile** compares using *like* or *as* (*as quiet as a mouse*).
- **Metaphor** says something *is* something else (*the sky was a black blanket*); **personification** gives objects human actions (*the wind whispered*).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can read a descriptive passage.
- Knows adjectives and verbs.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Writers are choosy — they pick words to create a **feeling or picture** in your head. Notice the difference between "a big dog" and "a towering, snarling beast". Spot the tools:
- **Simile** — compares with *like* or *as*: "her smile was *as bright as* the sun".
- **Metaphor** — says one thing *is* another: "the classroom *was a zoo*".
- **Personification** — gives non-living things human behaviour: "the thunder *grumbled*", "the leaves *danced*".
When reading, ask: "Why did the writer choose *that* word? How does it make me feel?"

**Worked example.** Analyse: "The old house *glared* at us with its broken windows."
1. *glared* — a person's angry look given to a house = **personification**.
2. Effect: it makes the house feel **threatening and alive** — building tension/spookiness.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Spooky stories:** find the words that make it scary.
- **Song lyrics / poems:** spot similes and metaphors.
- **Sport commentary:** "he flew down the wing" (metaphor).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** confuses simile and metaphor.
  **Why:** both compare.
  **Fix:** simile uses *like/as*; metaphor says it *is*. Look for the signal words.
- **Wrong:** names the technique but can't say the *effect*.
  **Why:** stops at labelling.
  **Fix:** always add "…which makes the reader feel/picture…".

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Is "as cold as ice" a simile or metaphor? **A:** Simile (uses *as*).
- **Q:** Name the technique: "the moon smiled down". **A:** Personification.
- **Q:** Why might a writer choose "stomped" instead of "walked"? **A:** To show anger/heaviness.

### Activity / practice
`categorize`/`speak`: sort phrases into simile / metaphor / personification, then explain the effect of one in a full sentence.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Similes, metaphors and personification***.
- **Pobble 365** — descriptive image prompts for figurative writing.

## 7. Creating settings and characters in a story

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 figurative language; expanded noun phrases; fronted adverbials.

### Key concepts
- A **setting** is where and when a story happens — describe it with the senses.
- A **character** comes alive through how they look, act, speak and feel — "show, don't tell".
- Use expanded noun phrases, figurative language and varied sentence openers.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write in paragraphs.
- Knows adjectives, similes and noun phrases.

### Teach it (the explanation)
To pull a reader into a story, build the **setting** and **characters**.
- **Setting:** describe it through the **senses** — what you'd see, hear, smell, feel. Don't just say "a scary forest"; *show* it: "Twisted branches clawed at the grey sky, and somewhere an owl shrieked."
- **Character:** "**show, don't tell**" their feelings through actions: instead of "Sam was nervous", write "Sam's hands trembled as he reached for the door". Reveal personality through what they say and do.
Vary your sentence openers (use fronted adverbials) so the writing flows.

**Worked example.** Show a frightened character (don't just tell).
- Telling: *Mia was scared.*
- Showing: *Mia's heart hammered. She backed away, eyes darting, and her voice came out as a whisper.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Favourite worlds:** describe a setting from a game or film the child loves.
- **Pets/animals as characters:** give an animal a personality.
- **Real places:** describe a familiar place at night to make it mysterious.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** "telling" emotions flatly ("he was happy, she was sad").
  **Why:** easier than showing.
  **Fix:** "show it with an action or the body" — model "show, don't tell".
- **Wrong:** lists adjectives but no atmosphere ("a big, dark, cold, wet forest").
  **Why:** adjective-stacking without imagery.
  **Fix:** use the senses + a simile to build a *picture*, not just a list.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does "show, don't tell" mean? **A:** Reveal feelings through actions/details, not by stating them.
- **Q:** Turn "He was angry" into a "show" sentence. **A:** e.g. *He slammed the door and clenched his fists.*
- **Q:** Name two senses to use when describing a setting. **A:** any two of sight, sound, smell, touch.

### Activity / practice
Writing task: describe a setting using two senses and a simile, then "show" a character's emotion in a sentence (with a `speak` rehearsal first).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Describing settings and characters***.
- **Pobble 365** — daily picture stimulus for setting/character description.

## 8. Writing an explanation text

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition (non-fiction)

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 paragraphs; conjunctions of cause; Standard English.

### Key concepts
- An **explanation** tells **how or why** something happens (a process).
- It uses **cause-and-effect language** (*because, so, this causes, as a result*) and the **present tense**.
- It's organised with a title, an intro, ordered steps/paragraphs, and often headings or diagrams.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can organise writing into paragraphs.
- Knows cause conjunctions.

### Teach it (the explanation)
An **explanation text** answers "**How does it work?**" or "**Why does it happen?**" — like how a volcano erupts or why we have day and night. Features:
- **Present tense, third person** (*the water rises…*).
- **Cause-and-effect connectives:** *because, so, this means, as a result, which causes*.
- **Time/sequence order** for processes: *first, then, this leads to…*.
- A **title** (often a question), an **introduction**, then paragraphs explaining each stage. Diagrams and headings help.
Keep it factual and clear — the reader should understand the process by the end.

**Worked example.** Explain why we have day and night (one paragraph).
- *The Earth spins on its axis once every 24 hours. **Because** the Earth turns, the side facing the Sun has daytime. **As a result**, the side facing away is in darkness, **so** we have night. **This means** day and night keep swapping as the Earth rotates.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Science:** water cycle, how rainbows form, why leaves fall.
- **Machines:** how a bike's brakes work.
- **Body:** why we get hiccups / why we yawn.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** writes instructions ("First, get a volcano…") instead of explaining the process.
  **Why:** confuses explanation with how-to.
  **Fix:** explanation describes how/why it *happens by itself*, in present tense — not steps for the reader to do.
- **Wrong:** no cause-effect links — just a list of facts.
  **Why:** misses the "why".
  **Fix:** join ideas with *because/so/as a result* to show cause and effect.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What question does an explanation text answer? **A:** How or why something happens.
- **Q:** Give two cause-and-effect connectives. **A:** any of *because, so, as a result, this causes*.
- **Q:** What tense are explanations usually in? **A:** Present tense.

### Activity / practice
Writing task with `fillBlank` connectives: plan and write a short explanation of a chosen process using cause-and-effect language and headings.

- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Writing an explanation text***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 4, *Explanation texts***.
