# English — Year 3 (ages 7–8)

Year 3 begins Lower Key Stage 2. Reading shifts from decoding to **understanding and inference with evidence**; spelling tackles prefixes, suffixes and the Y3/4 word list; grammar introduces **conjunctions, adverbs and prepositions for time/place/cause**, *a/an*, and the start of **direct speech** with inverted commas. Writing becomes **planned and paragraphed**.

---

## 1. Prefixes that change meaning (un-, dis-, mis-, re-, pre-)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Spelling / Vocabulary

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 suffix rules; knows what a root word is.

### Key concepts
- A **prefix** goes at the front of a root word and changes its meaning.
- *un-/dis-/mis-* often make an opposite or "wrong/bad" meaning; *re-* = again; *pre-* = before.
- You don't change the root's spelling — just add the prefix (*happy → unhappy*).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows root words and that suffixes go on the end.
- Can read multi-syllable words.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **prefix** is a word-starter that changes meaning. Learn the common ones:
- **un-** = not / opposite: *happy → unhappy*, *lock → unlock*.
- **dis-** = not / opposite: *agree → disagree*, *appear → disappear*.
- **mis-** = wrongly / badly: *behave → misbehave*, *spell → misspell*.
- **re-** = again / back: *do → redo*, *play → replay*.
- **pre-** = before: *view → preview*, *heat → preheat*.
Spelling rule: just bolt the prefix onto the front — don't drop or change letters. That's why it's *misspell* (mis + spell = double *s*) and *disappear* (dis + appear).

**Worked example.** Add prefixes to *appear*.
1. *dis* + *appear* → *disappear* (means "stop being seen").
2. *re* + *appear* → *reappear* (means "appear again").

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Magic:** things *appear* and *disappear*; *reappear*.
- **Sports replays:** "let's *re*play that goal".
- **Mistakes:** *misbehave, misread, mishear*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *mispell, disapear* (drops a letter at the join).
  **Why:** assumes joins must "smooth out".
  **Fix:** prefix + whole root, keep all letters: *mis+spell = misspell*; *dis+appear = disappear*.
- **Wrong:** thinks the prefix is a separate word ("un happy").
  **Why:** sees it as two words.
  **Fix:** the prefix attaches — one word, no space.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does *re-* mean in *rewrite*? **A:** again.
- **Q:** Add a prefix to make the opposite of *kind*. **A:** *unkind*.
- **Q:** Spell "spell it wrongly" with a prefix. **A:** *misspell* (double *s*).

### Activity / practice
`matchPairs`/`fillBlank`: match prefixes to meanings, then build five words and use one in a sentence.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Prefixes***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 3/4 prefixes**.

## 2. Suffixes -ly and -ous, and the suffix -tion family

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Spelling

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 suffix rules; Adverbs (see topic 4).

### Key concepts
- **-ly** usually turns an adjective into an adverb (*quick → quickly*); change *y→i* (*happy → happily*); *-le → -ly* (*gentle → gently*).
- **-ous** means "full of / having" (*danger → dangerous*, *poison → poisonous*).
- **-tion/-sion/-ssion/-cian** all make a `/shun/` ending (*action, division, mission, magician*).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can add simple suffixes.
- Knows adjectives and adverbs.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Three useful endings:
1. **-ly**: makes an adverb (how something is done): *slow → slowly*. If the root ends in *y*, change to *i*: *happy → happily*. If it ends in *-le*, swap to *-ly*: *gentle → gently*, *simple → simply*.
2. **-ous**: "full of": *fame → famous* (drop *e*), *poison → poisonous*, *danger → dangerous*.
3. The **`/shun/` family**: the sound is spelled different ways — *-tion* (*station, action*), *-sion* (*division*), *-ssion* (*mission, discussion*), *-cian* for people/jobs (*musician, magician*).

**Worked example.** Spell *gentle* + *-ly* and *poison* + *-ous*.
1. *gentle* ends in *-le* → drop *le*, add *ly* → *gently*.
2. *poison* + *ous* → *poisonous*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Animals:** *dangerous, poisonous, enormous* creatures.
- **Movement:** describe how a creature moves — *slowly, silently, gracefully*.
- **Jobs:** *musician, magician, electrician*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *gentlely, simplely*.
  **Why:** added *-ly* without dropping *-le*.
  **Fix:** *-le* words swap *le → ly*.
- **Wrong:** spells the `/shun/` sound as *-shun* (*acshun*).
  **Why:** spells by sound.
  **Fix:** it's almost always *-tion*; learn the common *-sion/-ssion/-cian* exceptions.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Make an adverb from *happy*. **A:** *happily*.
- **Q:** Add *-ous* to *fame*. **A:** *famous*.
- **Q:** Spell the job: someone who does magic. **A:** *magician*.

### Activity / practice
`categorize`: sort `/shun/` words by spelling; `fillBlank`: complete adverbs with *-ly*.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Suffixes -ly, -ous and -tion***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 3 spelling**.

## 3. The Year 3/4 statutory word list & tricky spellings

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Spelling

**Prerequisites:** Prefixes; Suffixes -ly, -ous, -tion.

### Key concepts
- There is a set list of ~100 words to learn across Years 3–4 because they're common but hard.
- Use strategies: spot the tricky part, syllable-chop, "say it in a silly way", and mnemonics.
- Practise little and often, not all at once.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can spell with taught rules.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Some everyday words don't follow neat rules, so they're on a **must-learn list**. Examples: *because, different, important, favourite, February, length, particular, possess, quarter, separate, special, surprise, therefore, weight, woman, women*. Learn each by finding its **tricky bit** and using a trick:
- *because* → "**B**ig **E**lephants **C**an **A**lways **U**nderstand **S**mall **E**lephants".
- *separate* → "there's **a rat** in sep-**a-rat**-e".
- *February* → say "Feb-ROO-ary" to hear the *r*.

**Worked example.** Learn *surprise*.
1. Tricky bit: the first *r* (people drop it: "suprise").
2. Say it carefully: "sur-**PRISE**".
3. Write, cover, write again — check the *r* is there.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Birthdays:** *surprise, favourite, special*.
- **Calendar:** *February, quarter*.
- **Science/measuring:** *length, weight, height*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** *seperate, suprise, Febuary*.
  **Why:** spelling by careless pronunciation.
  **Fix:** over-pronounce the missing sound; use the mnemonic.
- **Wrong:** crams the whole list in one go and forgets.
  **Why:** no spaced practice.
  **Fix:** 3–5 words a week, revisit old ones (spaced repetition).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Spell *because*. **A:** b-e-c-a-u-s-e.
- **Q:** Which is right: *seperate* or *separate*? **A:** *separate*.
- **Q:** Spell the second month. **A:** *February*.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`/`speak`: dictate 5 list words in sentences; child writes them, then self-checks the tricky letter.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Common spellings (Year 3/4 word list)***.
- **Spelling Frame — Year 3/4 statutory words** (free word lists & games).

## 4. Adverbs and prepositions for time, place and cause

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 word classes and conjunctions.

### Key concepts
- **Adverbs** tell *how, when, where* something happens (*slowly, soon, here*).
- **Prepositions** show position or time relations (*under, before, during, behind*).
- **Conjunctions, adverbs and prepositions** can all express **time, place and cause**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows nouns, verbs, adjectives and conjunctions (*because, when*).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Three little jobs help us add detail about *time, place and cause*:
- **Conjunctions** join clauses: *when, before, after, while, so, because*.
- **Adverbs** describe a verb — often *when* (*then, soon, later, suddenly*) or *how* (*carefully*).
- **Prepositions** show where/when something sits in relation to another: *in, on, under, behind, before, during, after*.
The same idea can be shown three ways: *Before lunch* (preposition + noun), *He left, then ate* (adverb), *He ate after he played* (conjunction).

**Worked example.** Add detail to "The cat slept."
1. Where (preposition): *The cat slept **under the table**.*
2. When (adverb): *The cat slept **soundly** **afterwards**.*
3. Cause (conjunction): *The cat slept **because it was tired**.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Hide and seek:** prepositions of place — *behind, under, inside*.
- **A day's timetable:** time words — *before, after, during, then*.
- **Sport commentary:** *suddenly, quickly, finally*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** confuses adverbs and adjectives (*"he ran quick"*).
  **Why:** adverbs feel like adjectives.
  **Fix:** adjectives describe nouns; adverbs describe verbs and often end *-ly* (*ran quickly*).
- **Wrong:** thinks a preposition is a "joining word" like a conjunction.
  **Why:** both link things.
  **Fix:** a preposition is followed by a noun (*under the table*); a conjunction joins clauses (*because it was late*).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Find the preposition: *The ball rolled under the car.* **A:** *under*.
- **Q:** Add a "when" adverb to *We went home*. **A:** e.g. *We went home **later**.*
- **Q:** Is *because* a preposition, adverb or conjunction? **A:** Conjunction.

### Activity / practice
`categorize`/`fillBlank`: sort words into time/place/cause; complete sentences choosing the best preposition or adverb.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Adverbs* and *Prepositions***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 3 prepositions and adverbs**.

## 5. *a* or *an* — choosing the right article

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 15 · **Subject strand:** VGP – grammar

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 phonics (vowel sounds).

### Key concepts
- Use **a** before a word starting with a **consonant sound** (*a cat, a dog*).
- Use **an** before a word starting with a **vowel sound** (*an apple, an egg*).
- It's about the **sound**, not just the letter (*an hour, a unicorn*).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows vowels (a, e, i, o, u) and consonants.

### Teach it (the explanation)
*a* and *an* both mean "one of something", but we pick by the **sound** that follows. Before a vowel *sound* we use **an** so it's easy to say: *an apple, an igloo, an orange*. Before a consonant *sound* we use **a**: *a banana, a tree*. Watch the trick ones — go by sound, not spelling: *an hour* (the *h* is silent → vowel sound), *a unicorn* (sounds like "yoo" → consonant sound).

**Worked example.** Choose for *elephant* and *house*.
1. *elephant* starts with a vowel sound `/e/` → **an** elephant.
2. *house* starts with a consonant sound `/h/` → **a** house.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Animals:** *an octopus, a tiger, an emu*.
- **Snacks:** *an apple, a biscuit, an orange*.
- **Time:** *an hour, a minute*.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** "a apple", "a egg".
  **Why:** ignores the vowel-sound rule.
  **Fix:** say it aloud — "a apple" is awkward; *an* glides.
- **Wrong:** "an unicorn", "a hour".
  **Why:** went by the letter, not the sound.
  **Fix:** listen to the *sound*: "yoo-nicorn" → *a*; silent-*h* "our" → *an*.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** *a* or *an* + *umbrella*? **A:** *an umbrella*.
- **Q:** *a* or *an* + *hour*? **A:** *an hour* (silent h).
- **Q:** Why is it *a* unicorn? **A:** It starts with a consonant ("yoo") sound.

### Activity / practice
`multipleChoice`/`fillBlank`: choose *a* or *an* for ten nouns, including tricky *hour/unicorn/honest/university*.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Using a and an***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 3 determiners**.

## 6. Punctuating direct speech (inverted commas)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** VGP – punctuation

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 sentence types and end punctuation.

### Key concepts
- **Inverted commas** ("speech marks") go around the **exact words spoken**.
- Put a **punctuation mark inside** the closing speech mark (*"Run!" / "Where?"*).
- Start the spoken words with a **capital letter**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write sentences with capitals, full stops, ? and !.

### Teach it (the explanation)
When you write what someone *says*, you wrap their exact words in **inverted commas** "…" like a speech bubble. Three rules to start:
1. Speech marks hug only the **spoken words**: *"I'm hungry."*
2. The first spoken word gets a **capital letter**.
3. The end punctuation (. ? !) goes **inside** the closing speech marks: *"Stop!" shouted Dan.*
A comma separates speech from the rest: *"It's late," said Mum.*

**Worked example.** Punctuate: she said i am ready.
1. Spoken words: *I am ready.*
2. Capital + end mark inside: *"I am ready."*
3. Add who said it: *"I am ready," she said.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Comic strips:** turn speech bubbles into written speech.
- **Plays/films:** write what a favourite character says.
- **Interviews:** quote what someone actually said.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** puts the full stop outside the speech marks (*"Run".*).
  **Why:** treats speech marks like brackets.
  **Fix:** end punctuation lives *inside* the closing inverted comma.
- **Wrong:** wraps the whole sentence, including *said Tom*, in speech marks.
  **Why:** thinks the marks cover everything.
  **Fix:** only the *exact spoken words* go inside — not "said Tom".

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What do inverted commas go around? **A:** The exact words spoken.
- **Q:** Punctuate: stop said the guard. **A:** *"Stop!" said the guard.*
- **Q:** Inside or outside — where does the full stop go? **A:** Inside the closing speech marks.

### Activity / practice
`fillBlank`: add inverted commas, capitals and inside-punctuation to four lines of speech.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Inverted commas / speech marks***.
- **Grammarsaurus — Year 3 direct speech**.

## 7. Inference and retrieval with evidence

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Reading comprehension

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 making inferences.

### Key concepts
- **Retrieve** facts the text states directly.
- **Infer** characters' feelings, thoughts and motives from clues.
- **Justify** every inference with evidence — quote or point to the clue.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can read a short passage independently.
- Knows the difference between retrieval and inference.

### Teach it (the explanation)
At Year 3 you not only *make* an inference, you **prove it**. Read closely, then answer "How do you know?" with evidence from the text. Use a sentence stem: **"I think ___ because the text says ___."** Distinguish the two question types: *retrieval* answers are sitting on the page; *inference* answers are worked out from clues + your knowledge. Skim back to the text — don't answer from memory or imagination alone.

**Worked example.** Passage: *"Jack pressed his nose to the toy-shop window. His pocket money jingled, but it was not enough. He turned away slowly."*
- Retrieval: "What did Jack press to the window?" → his nose.
- Inference: "How does Jack feel?" → disappointed, **because** he didn't have enough money and turned away slowly.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Mystery stories:** infer the culprit from clues.
- **Sport reports:** infer how a team felt after a result.
- **Adverts:** infer what they want you to think.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** gives an inference but no evidence.
  **Why:** stops at the guess.
  **Fix:** always finish with "…because the text says…".
- **Wrong:** answers from imagination, contradicting the text.
  **Why:** not re-reading.
  **Fix:** "find the proof in the text first, then answer".

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** [Jack passage] How do we know Jack is sad? Give evidence. **A:** He turned away slowly / didn't have enough money.
- **Q:** What sentence stem proves an inference? **A:** "I think… because the text says…".
- **Q:** Retrieve: how much money did Jack have? **A:** Not enough (the text doesn't give a number — it says "not enough").

### Activity / practice
`speak`/`multipleChoice`: read a short passage; answer two retrieval and two inference questions, each with evidence.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Inference / reading between the lines***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 3 reading comprehension**.

## 8. Organising writing into paragraphs

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 writing; conjunctions and adverbs.

### Key concepts
- A **paragraph** is a group of sentences about **one idea or topic**.
- Start a new paragraph when the **topic, time, place or speaker changes**.
- Paragraphs make writing organised and easy to follow.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write several linked sentences.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **paragraph** keeps related sentences together, like a chapter keeps a section together. All the sentences in a paragraph are about **one thing**. You **start a new paragraph** when something changes — a new **topic**, a jump in **time** ("The next morning…"), a new **place**, or a new **speaker** in dialogue. On paper you show a new paragraph by starting on a new line (and indenting). Plan your paragraphs *before* you write — one box per idea.

**Worked example.** Plan a recount of a day at the seaside in 3 paragraphs.
1. Paragraph 1 — getting there (time: morning).
2. Paragraph 2 — on the beach (place/activity).
3. Paragraph 3 — going home (time: evening).
Each box becomes one paragraph.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **A trip/recount:** one paragraph per part of the day.
- **An animal fact file:** one paragraph per sub-topic (food, home, babies).
- **A story:** new paragraph when the scene changes.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** one giant block of text with no paragraphs.
  **Why:** doesn't plan or sees paragraphs as optional.
  **Fix:** plan idea-boxes first; "new idea = new paragraph".
- **Wrong:** new paragraph after every sentence.
  **Why:** confuses sentence with paragraph.
  **Fix:** group all sentences on the *same* idea together; only break on a change.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a paragraph about? **A:** One main idea or topic.
- **Q:** Name one reason to start a new paragraph. **A:** A change of topic / time / place / speaker.
- **Q:** True or false: every sentence is a new paragraph. **A:** False.

### Activity / practice
`ordering`/`categorize`: group jumbled sentences into the correct paragraphs by topic, then plan three paragraph-boxes for a recount.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Organising writing into paragraphs***.
- **Oak National Academy — Year 3 writing structure**.

## 9. Writing a recount (what happened)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Writing composition

**Prerequisites:** Organising writing into paragraphs; past tense.

### Key concepts
- A **recount** retells real events in the **order they happened**, in the **past tense**.
- It's written in the **first or third person** and uses **time words** (*first, then, after that, finally*).
- It usually has an opening (who/what/when/where), the events, and a closing thought.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can write in the past tense.
- Can organise sentences into paragraphs.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **recount** tells the reader what happened — like a diary, news report, or trip write-up. Key features:
- **Past tense** (*we went, she saw*).
- **Chronological order** — events in time order, joined with *first, next, after that, later, finally*.
- An **opening** that sets the scene: who, what, when, where.
- A **closing** with a thought or feeling: "It was the best day ever."
Keep it factual — what really happened — but make it interesting with good word choices.

**Worked example.** Opening of a recount of sports day.
- Opening: *Last Friday, our whole school went to the field for sports day.*
- First event: *First, we ran the sprint race.*
- Closing: *In the end, my team won — I felt so proud.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **A real trip / event** the child went on.
- **A historical recount** (a day in the life of a Roman child).
- **A diary entry** for a story character.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** drifts into present tense mid-recount ("we went… and then I see…").
  **Why:** tense slips under flow.
  **Fix:** check every verb is past tense on re-read.
- **Wrong:** events jump around out of order.
  **Why:** no time plan.
  **Fix:** plan a time-line first; use ordering words.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What tense is a recount written in? **A:** Past tense.
- **Q:** Give two time words for a recount. **A:** any of *first, then, after that, finally*.
- **Q:** What goes in the opening? **A:** Who, what, when and where.

### Activity / practice
Writing task with `ordering` plan: order events of a recent event, then write a 3-paragraph recount in the past tense with time words.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Writing a recount***.
- **Pobble 365** — image prompts to recount or imagine an event.

## 10. Speaking clearly: explaining and presenting

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Spoken language / Oracy

**Prerequisites:** none (an oracy lesson).

### Key concepts
- Speak in **full sentences** with a clear beginning, middle and end.
- Give a **well-structured explanation** using *because, so, for example*.
- Use **Standard English** (e.g. *we were*, not *we was*) when presenting.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can hold a simple conversation and answer questions.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Speaking well is a skill you can practise. When you **explain** something, structure it: state your point, give a reason (*because…*), and an example (*for example…*). Speak in **full sentences** — not just one word — and loudly and clearly enough to be heard. When presenting to others, use **Standard English**: "we **were** there" (not "we was"), "I **did** it" (not "I done it"), "those **books**" (not "them books"). Look up, slow down, and pause at full stops.

**Worked example.** Explain why you like a hobby.
- Point: *My favourite hobby is football.*
- Reason: *I love it **because** I get to play with my friends.*
- Example: *For example, last week we won a match together.*

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Show and tell:** present a favourite object.
- **Teach the tutor:** explain the rules of a game.
- **Mini-debate:** give a reasoned opinion.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** answers in single words ("football").
  **Why:** habit of minimal answers.
  **Fix:** require a full sentence: "My favourite sport is…".
- **Wrong:** non-Standard forms ("we was", "I done").
  **Why:** copies spoken dialect.
  **Fix:** model and rehearse the Standard form for presenting; both are fine at home, but "school English" for presentations.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which is Standard English: "we was" or "we were"? **A:** *we were*.
- **Q:** Give two words that help structure an explanation. **A:** *because, for example* (or *so*).
- **Q:** Why answer in full sentences? **A:** It's clearer and the listener understands better.

### Activity / practice
`speak` block: the child explains a chosen topic in 3 structured sentences (point, reason, example) using Standard English; the tutor gives feedback.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — *Speaking and listening / presenting***.
- **Voice 21 / Oracy resources** — free spoken-language guidance for families.
