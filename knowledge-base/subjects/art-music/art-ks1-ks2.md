# Art & Design — KS1 & KS2 (Years 1–6), UK National Curriculum

Eleven topics in the per-topic contract (`_TEMPLATE.md`). All are doable at home with basic materials. The "Misconceptions" field here records **common errors and difficulties**, not factual wrong answers. ## 1. Drawing & line

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–2 (ages 5–7) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Formal elements – line

**Prerequisites:** none

### Key concepts
- A line is any mark a tool makes; lines can be thick/thin, straight/curved, wavy, zig-zag, dotted, looped.
- Different lines have different "feelings" (a calm wavy line vs an angry jagged one).
- You hold and move your tool on purpose to control the line.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can hold a pencil or crayon and make marks.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Everything you draw is made of **lines**. A line is just the path a pencil takes for a walk! Let's make a "line zoo": a straight line, a wavy line (like the sea), a zig-zag (like lightning), a spiral (like a snail's shell), a dotted line, a loopy line. Press hard for a thick dark line, press softly for a thin pale one. Lines can show movement and feeling — fast scribbly lines feel busy; slow curvy lines feel calm.

**Worked example.** Draw a snail using only lines: 1) a slow spiral for the shell, 2) a smooth curved line for the body, 3) two straight lines with dots on top for the antennae. Same tool, different lines, a whole animal.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Trace the lines you see around you — the edge of a roof, a wiggly river, train tracks.
- "Line walks" — go for a walk and spot a straight line, a curved line, a zig-zag.
- Draw your favourite animal/character using only lines.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** gripping the pencil hard and pressing the same on every line. **Why:** not yet controlling pressure. **Fix:** practise "loud" (hard) and "whisper" (soft) lines side by side to feel the difference.
- **Error:** thinking you must draw the whole outline in one go. **Why:** fear of "wrong" lines. **Fix:** light, sketchy, build-up lines are fine — artists draw lightly first.
- **Error:** "I can't draw." **Why:** comparing to cartoons. **Fix:** there's no wrong line; we're practising marks, not perfection.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Show me three different kinds of line. **A:** any three, e.g. straight, wavy, zig-zag.
- **Q:** How do you make a line thicker and darker? **A:** press harder with the pencil.
- **Q:** Which line feels calm — a smooth curve or a sharp zig-zag? **A:** the smooth curve.

### Activity / practice
Make a **line pattern strip** in the sketchbook: divide a strip into 6 boxes and fill each with a different line (straight, wavy, zig-zag, spiral, dotted, looped). (`ordering` or free-make; describe each line aloud.)

## 2. Colour mixing & the colour wheel

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–3 (ages 5–8) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Formal elements – colour

**Prerequisites:** Drawing & line

### Key concepts
- **Primary** colours — red, yellow, blue — can't be mixed from others.
- Mixing two primaries makes a **secondary**: red+yellow=orange, blue+yellow=green, red+blue=purple.
- **Warm** colours (red, orange, yellow) vs **cool** colours (blue, green, purple).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can name basic colours and use paint or felt-tips.

### Teach it (the explanation)
There are three special colours you **can't** make by mixing — red, yellow and blue. We call them **primary** colours. Mix two of them and you get a new **secondary** colour:
- red + yellow → **orange**
- blue + yellow → **green**
- red + blue → **purple**
Arrange them in a circle and you have the **colour wheel**. One half feels warm (reds/oranges/yellows — like fire and sun); the other feels cool (blues/greens/purples — like water and ice).

**Worked example.** Make green step by step: 1) a blob of blue, 2) a blob of yellow next to it, 3) drag a little yellow into the blue and mix → green. Add *more* yellow → lighter, grassier green; more blue → deeper green.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Mix the exact green of a leaf or the orange of a sunset.
- A rainbow is the colour wheel stretched into an arc.
- Warm/cool: paint a "fire" picture vs an "ice" picture.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** mixing *all* the colours together expecting a bright new colour, getting brown mud. **Why:** every colour mixed cancels into grey-brown. **Fix:** mix only **two** primaries at a time for a clean secondary.
- **Error:** thinking you can mix a primary (e.g. "make red from yellow and blue"). **Why:** misunderstanding primary. **Fix:** primaries can't be mixed — they're the starting colours.
- **Error:** dipping a dirty brush between colours, muddying everything. **Fix:** rinse the brush between colours.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name the three primary colours. **A:** red, yellow, blue.
- **Q:** What two colours make green? **A:** blue and yellow.
- **Q:** Is blue a warm or cool colour? **A:** cool.

### Activity / practice
Mix and label a **colour wheel**: paint the 3 primaries in a triangle, then mix and add the 3 secondaries between them. (`matchPairs`: primary pair → secondary colour, e.g. red+blue → purple.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1/KS2 Art — *Colour / Colour wheel***.
- **Tate Kids — *Colour* games and activities**.

## 3. Painting

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–4 (ages 5–9) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Formal elements – colour, technique

**Prerequisites:** Colour mixing & the colour wheel

### Key concepts
- Control paint with brush size, amount of paint and amount of water.
- Add **white** to make a colour lighter (a *tint*); add a little **black** to make it darker (a *shade*).
- Let layers dry, or work wet-into-wet on purpose for blending.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can mix secondary colours and hold a brush.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Painting is mixing **plus control**. A big brush fills space fast; a small brush does details. Too much water makes runny pale paint; thick paint covers solidly. To make a colour **lighter**, add white (this is a *tint* — pink is a tint of red). To make it **darker**, add a tiny bit of black (a *shade*). Paint big shapes first, then details on top once dry.

**Worked example.** Paint a sky from light to dark: 1) mix blue + lots of white for a pale top, 2) blend down to plain blue, 3) add a touch more blue (or a speck of black) at the bottom for a deeper blue. Three tones of one colour.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Paint the sky at different times of day (dawn tints vs night shades).
- Mix the exact colour of your pet, your front door, a fruit.
- Paint to music — fast loud music = bold strokes.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** adding lots of black to lighten or "brighten" a colour. **Why:** confusing light/dark. **Fix:** **white** lightens; black only darkens — and a *tiny* bit goes a long way.
- **Error:** painting detail on top of wet paint and it smears. **Why:** layers not dry. **Fix:** let the base layer dry, or blend on purpose while wet.
- **Error:** flooding the brush with water so everything is pale and runny. **Fix:** wipe the brush; use less water for stronger colour.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How do you make a colour lighter? **A:** add white.
- **Q:** How do you make red into a darker shade? **A:** add a little black.
- **Q:** Why let a layer dry before adding detail? **A:** so the top layer doesn't smear/mix in.

### Activity / practice
Paint a **tints-and-shades strip**: one colour in the middle, getting lighter (add white) to the left and darker (add black) to the right — 5 boxes. (`ordering`: arrange tone swatches lightest → darkest.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Painting / Tints and shades***.
- **Oak National Academy — *Art: painting* unit**.

## 4. Printmaking

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–4 (ages 5–9) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Technique – printing, pattern

**Prerequisites:** Colour mixing & the colour wheel

### Key concepts
- Printing is making **repeated copies** of a shape by inking it and pressing it down.
- Everyday objects (sponges, leaves, a halved potato, fingertips) make great print blocks.
- Printing is the easiest way to make **pattern** (repeating, rows, rotating).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can use paint and recognises simple patterns.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **print** is a mark you can stamp again and again. You put paint on something with an interesting shape or texture, then press it onto paper — and you get the *same* shape every time. That's why printing is perfect for **patterns**: stamp a row, repeat, maybe turn the stamp each time. Leaves print their veins; a cut potato prints a clean shape; bubble wrap prints dots.

**Worked example.** Make a leaf-print pattern: 1) paint the bumpy underside of a leaf, 2) press it firmly onto paper and peel off — the veins show up, 3) repeat in a row, turning the leaf each time, to make a border pattern.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Wrapping paper and fabric are covered in printed patterns — make your own.
- Print with nature: leaves, twigs, the edge of a cabbage.
- Fingerprint a row of little creatures (add legs/eyes after).

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** too much paint, so prints blob and lose detail. **Why:** over-inking. **Fix:** a thin even layer; press, don't slide.
- **Error:** sliding/wiggling the stamp so the print smudges. **Fix:** press straight down and lift straight up.
- **Error:** thinking each print should look different. **Why:** confusing with drawing. **Fix:** the *point* of printing is repetition — same shape, many times = pattern.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What makes printing different from drawing? **A:** you repeat the same shape by stamping it.
- **Q:** Name something at home you could print with. **A:** sponge, leaf, potato, bubble wrap, fingertip (any).
- **Q:** Why use just a thin layer of paint? **A:** so the print stays clear/detailed.

### Activity / practice
Make a **repeating print pattern** strip using one found object (sponge/leaf/potato), stamping a neat row; try rotating the stamp every other print. (`speak`/describe the pattern rule you used.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Printing / Printmaking***.
- **Tate Kids — *Printmaking* make activity**.

## 5. Collage

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–4 (ages 5–9) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Technique – texture, shape

**Prerequisites:** Drawing & line (shape)

### Key concepts
- Collage = making a picture by **sticking** pieces (paper, fabric, found bits) onto a surface.
- You can build images from **shapes** and add real **texture**.
- Tearing vs cutting gives different edges and effects.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can use scissors and glue and recognises shapes.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Collage** means gluing things down to make a picture — torn paper, magazine scraps, fabric, leaves, foil. Instead of drawing a shape you *cut or tear* it and stick it. Because you use real materials, collage has real **texture** (rough sandpaper, smooth foil, soft cotton wool). Plan by laying pieces out first, *then* glue when you like it.

**Worked example.** Make a collage tree: 1) tear a brown strip for the trunk and stick it down, 2) tear many small green pieces for leaves, 3) overlap and glue them in a clump on top. Torn edges look leafy.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Make a collage of your favourite food from magazine cut-outs.
- A "texture monster" using as many different surfaces as you can find.
- Eric Carle's *Very Hungry Caterpillar* is collage — make a creature his way.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** using floods of glue so paper wrinkles and slides. **Why:** more glue ≠ better stick. **Fix:** a thin layer, press and hold.
- **Error:** gluing pieces down before planning, then regretting the layout. **Fix:** arrange everything dry first, glue last.
- **Error:** thinking collage must use only neat cut shapes. **Fix:** **torn** edges are a deliberate, lovely effect — mix tearing and cutting.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is collage? **A:** making a picture by sticking pieces of material onto a surface.
- **Q:** Which formal element does collage really bring out? **A:** texture (and shape).
- **Q:** Why arrange pieces before gluing? **A:** so you can change the layout while it's still loose.

### Activity / practice
Make a **texture collage** of one subject (an animal, a landscape) using at least three different materials; name the texture of each. (`categorize`: sort scraps into rough/smooth/soft.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1/KS2 Art — *Collage / Texture***.
- **Tate Kids — *Collage* make activity**.

## 6. Sculpture & 3-D (form)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–5 (ages 5–10) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Formal elements – form, space

**Prerequisites:** Drawing & line (shape)

### Key concepts
- **Form** is a 3-D shape you can walk around: a sphere, cube, cylinder — or a model.
- Sculpture is art you make in 3-D by joining, building, rolling or moulding.
- You can build *up* (junk modelling) or shape a lump (clay/play-dough).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child knows 2-D shapes and can handle materials.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A drawing is flat (2-D); a **sculpture** is solid — you can see it from all sides. A 3-D shape is called a **form**: a ball is a *sphere*, a box is a *cube*, a tin is a *cylinder*. You can make form two ways: **building up** (stick boxes and tubes together — junk modelling) or **moulding** (squeeze and roll a lump of clay/play-dough). Joining matters: tape, glue or, with clay, "score and slip" (scratch and dampen the join).

**Worked example.** Make a play-dough snail in form: 1) roll a fat sausage for the body, 2) roll a long thin sausage and coil it into a spiral shell, 3) press the shell onto the body, 4) two tiny balls on stalks for eyes. A 2-D spiral becomes a 3-D form.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Junk-model a rocket, robot or castle from recycling.
- Spot forms around you: a ball (sphere), a can (cylinder), a dice (cube).
- Make an animal from one lump of dough without adding pieces.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** mixing up shape and form (calling a clay ball a "circle"). **Why:** 2-D vs 3-D language. **Fix:** flat = shape (circle); solid = form (sphere).
- **Error:** pieces falling off because joins aren't secured. **Why:** weak joining. **Fix:** press firmly, tape/glue, or score-and-slip with clay.
- **Error:** only thinking about the front. **Why:** drawing habit. **Fix:** turn it round as you work — sculpture has a back and sides too.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What's the difference between a shape and a form? **A:** shape is flat/2-D, form is solid/3-D.
- **Q:** What 3-D form is a ball? **A:** a sphere.
- **Q:** Name two ways to make a sculpture. **A:** building up (junk modelling) and moulding (clay/dough).

### Activity / practice
Make a small **3-D model** of an animal or vehicle from play-dough or junk, checking it looks good from front, side and back. (`speak`: describe the forms you used.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Sculpture / 3-D / Form***.
- **Tate Kids — *Sculpture* explore/make activities**.

## 7. Sketchbooks & observational drawing

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Skill – observational drawing

**Prerequisites:** Drawing & line

### Key concepts
- **Observational drawing** = drawing a *real object in front of you*, slowly looking at it.
- A **sketchbook** is for collecting, practising, experimenting and recording ideas — not for "perfect" pictures.
- Look more than you draw: keep your eyes on the object, not just the paper.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can make different lines and is willing to draw lightly.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The fastest way to get better at drawing is to **draw from real life**. Put a real object in front of you — a leaf, a shoe, an apple — and draw what you *actually see*, not what you imagine. The secret is **slow looking**: spend more time looking at the object than at your paper. Start with light "sketchy" lines for the big shape, then add detail. Your **sketchbook** is where you do all this trying — wonky early attempts are exactly what it's for. Date your pages and add little notes.

**Worked example.** Draw a real leaf: 1) look hard for 10 seconds — notice its overall shape, 2) lightly draw the outline, eyes mostly on the leaf, 3) add the main central vein, then the side veins, 4) add the texture of the edge. Look, draw a bit, look again.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Keep a "nature sketchbook" — draw one found object a day.
- Draw your own hand, your shoe, your breakfast.
- Sketch a pet or sibling sitting still for one minute.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** drawing a *symbol* of the object from memory (a cartoon apple) instead of looking. **Why:** habit. **Fix:** keep your eyes on the real object — draw what's there, not the idea of it.
- **Error:** treating the sketchbook like a finished gallery, so afraid to "spoil" it. **Why:** misunderstanding its purpose. **Fix:** it's a practice book — mess, cross-outs and tests belong there.
- **Error:** pressing dark and committing instantly. **Fix:** light build-up lines first; darken once it's right.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is observational drawing? **A:** drawing a real object in front of you, looking carefully at it.
- **Q:** What should a sketchbook be used for? **A:** practising, experimenting, recording ideas — not just finished art.
- **Q:** Where should your eyes mostly be while observational drawing? **A:** on the object you're drawing.

### Activity / practice
Do a **two-minute observational drawing** of one real object using light build-up lines, then add notes about what was tricky in the sketchbook. (`speak`: explain one thing you noticed by looking closely.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Drawing / Observational drawing***.
- **Tate Kids — *Sketchbook* ideas and prompts**.

## 8. Studying artists

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Knowledge – artists & designers

**Prerequisites:** any earlier formal-element topic

### Key concepts
- Looking at real artists builds **knowledge** and gives you ideas and techniques to try.
- You can describe an artwork using the formal-element words (line, shape, colour…).
- Make something *inspired by* an artist — borrow their idea, don't just copy.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child knows the formal-element words and has made some art.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Artists give us ideas. Look at a famous artwork, talk about it ("What lines and colours do you see? How does it make you feel?"), then make your own piece **in that style**. Some great, child-friendly artists:
- **Wassily Kandinsky** — bright shapes, circles and lines; painted music as colour (*Concentric Circles*).
- **Vincent van Gogh** — swirly, thick, energetic brushstrokes and strong colour (*Sunflowers*, *Starry Night*).
- **Katsushika Hokusai** — Japanese woodblock prints; bold line, flat colour, pattern (*The Great Wave*).
- **Frida Kahlo** — bold self-portraits with bright colour, flowers and animals.
- **Henri Matisse** — cut-out collages of simple, joyful coloured shapes.

**Worked example.** Make a Kandinsky-style piece: 1) look at his concentric circles, 2) draw several squares lightly, 3) fill each with rings of different colours (one circle inside another), like he did — your own colours, his idea.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Find an artist who painted something you love (sunflowers, the sea, cats).
- "Art detective": describe a painting so well someone could picture it.
- Recreate a famous artwork with things around the house.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** thinking "good art" means a perfect realistic copy. **Why:** narrow idea of art. **Fix:** Van Gogh and Kandinsky aren't photo-real — art can be bold, swirly, abstract.
- **Error:** copying an artwork exactly. **Why:** confusing inspiration with copying. **Fix:** borrow the *idea/technique* (swirls, rings, cut-outs) and make it your own.
- **Error:** "I don't get it / it's just shapes." **Fix:** abstract art is about feeling and the formal elements, not telling a story.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name an artist and one thing they're known for. **A:** e.g. Van Gogh — swirly brushstrokes / *Starry Night*.
- **Q:** What does it mean to make art "inspired by" an artist? **A:** to borrow their idea or style, not copy exactly.
- **Q:** Describe Hokusai's *Great Wave* using a formal element. **A:** e.g. strong curved *lines*, flat *colour*, *pattern* in the foam.

### Activity / practice
Pick one artist above, describe their work in a sentence using a formal-element word, then make a small piece **inspired by** them. (`matchPairs`: artist → what they're famous for.)

### Resources
- **Tate Kids — *Who is…?* artist pages (Kandinsky, Van Gogh, Matisse, Kahlo, Hokusai)**.
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Famous artists / Art history***.

## 9. Pattern

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–4 (ages 5–9) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Formal elements – pattern

**Prerequisites:** Drawing & line, Printmaking

### Key concepts
- A **pattern** is a design that **repeats** in a predictable way.
- Patterns can repeat by **rows**, by **rotating**, or by **alternating** (ABAB).
- Patterns are everywhere — nature, clothes, buildings, art.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can draw simple shapes and recognise repetition.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **pattern** is something that repeats so you can predict what comes next. The simplest is **ABAB** — circle, square, circle, square… You can make patterns by **repeating** a unit in a row, by **alternating** two units, or by **rotating** a shape each time. Patterns are in nature (zebra stripes, honeycomb, leopard spots), on clothes, on tiles and in lots of art (Hokusai's prints, Islamic geometric tiles).

**Worked example.** Make an ABBA pattern: choose two shapes, ▲ and ●. Order them ▲ ● ● ▲ — then repeat the whole block: ▲●●▲ ▲●●▲. Predict the next: ▲. Reading and continuing the rule *is* the pattern.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Hunt for patterns at home — wallpaper, tiles, a jumper, brickwork.
- Animal patterns: stripes, spots, scales.
- Design wrapping paper or a tile with your own repeating rule.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** drawing random shapes and calling it a pattern. **Why:** missing "repeat/predictable." **Fix:** a pattern must follow a *rule* you can continue.
- **Error:** breaking the rule partway and not noticing. **Fix:** say the rule out loud as you go ("circle, square, circle, square…").
- **Error:** thinking patterns only go in straight lines. **Fix:** patterns can rotate, tile, or radiate too.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What makes something a pattern? **A:** it repeats in a predictable way (follows a rule).
- **Q:** Continue: red, blue, red, blue, ___ . **A:** red.
- **Q:** Name a pattern you can see in nature. **A:** e.g. zebra stripes, honeycomb, spots.

### Activity / practice
Design a **repeating pattern** for a tile or wrapping paper using two or three units and state the rule (e.g. ABBA, rotate-each-time). (`ordering`: continue a given pattern sequence.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1/KS2 Art — *Pattern***.
- **Tate Kids — *Pattern* make activity**.

## 10. Tone & shading

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Formal elements – tone

**Prerequisites:** Drawing & line, Sketchbooks & observational drawing

### Key concepts
- **Tone** is how light or dark something is.
- Shading with tone makes a flat drawing look **3-D and round**.
- Light comes from one direction; the side away from the light is darker (shadow).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can control pencil pressure and draw light build-up lines.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Tone** means how light or dark a part of a picture is. It's the trick that makes a drawing look solid and rounded instead of flat. Decide where the **light** is coming from. The side facing the light stays light; the side away from it gets **darker** — that's the shadow. You build tone by pressing harder, layering, or shading more densely. A **tone scale** runs from white (lightest) through greys to black (darkest).

**Worked example.** Shade a ball to make it look round: 1) draw a circle, 2) decide the light is top-left, 3) leave the top-left light/white, 4) shade gradually darker towards the bottom-right, 5) add a little shadow on the ground under it. The flat circle now looks like a 3-D sphere.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Shine a torch on an object and watch where the shadow falls before drawing it.
- Shade an apple or ball from observation to make it pop off the page.
- Spot light-and-dark sides on objects around the room.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** colouring everything the same flat darkness. **Why:** not thinking about a light source. **Fix:** pick one light direction; vary tone from light to dark across the object.
- **Error:** thinking "tone" means "colour." **Why:** word overlap. **Fix:** tone is *lightness/darkness* — you can have light and dark of one colour.
- **Error:** jumping straight to solid black. **Fix:** build tone gradually, leaving the lightest areas.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does tone mean in art? **A:** how light or dark something is.
- **Q:** Why do we use tone/shading? **A:** to make a flat drawing look 3-D / rounded.
- **Q:** If the light is on the left, which side of a ball is darker? **A:** the right side (away from the light).

### Activity / practice
Draw a **tone scale** (5 boxes white → black), then shade a simple object (ball/apple) with one light source to make it look 3-D. (`ordering`: arrange tones lightest → darkest.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Tone / Shading / Light and dark***.
- **Oak National Academy — *Art: drawing and tone* unit**.

## 11. Perspective & space (upper KS2)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Formal elements – space, perspective

**Prerequisites:** Drawing & line, Tone & shading

### Key concepts
- **Space** in a picture shows what's near and what's far away.
- Tricks to show depth: things further away are **smaller**, **higher up** the page, and **overlapped** by nearer things.
- **One-point perspective**: parallel lines (like a road) appear to meet at a single **vanishing point**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can draw shapes and has met tone/3-D drawing.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A flat page can show deep **space** — distance — if you use a few tricks. Things that are far away look **smaller**, sit **higher up** the page, and are partly **hidden (overlapped)** by closer things. The strongest trick is **one-point perspective**: draw a dot in the distance (the **vanishing point**); lines that are really parallel — the sides of a road, the edges of a corridor — appear to rush together towards that dot. That's why a straight road looks like it narrows to a point on the horizon.

**Worked example.** Draw a road going into the distance: 1) mark a **vanishing point** dot in the middle of the page, 2) draw two lines from the bottom corners up to that dot (the road edges), 3) draw trees beside it — big near the front, smaller and higher near the dot, 4) the road looks like it stretches far away.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Stand on a straight path and notice it "narrow" into the distance.
- Look down a corridor or railway track — the lines meet at a point.
- Draw your street or a city scene with buildings shrinking into the distance.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** drawing far-away things the same size as near things. **Why:** not applying scale. **Fix:** further = smaller and higher up the page.
- **Error:** putting the horizon/vanishing point off and angling lines randomly. **Why:** perspective lines not from one point. **Fix:** all the depth lines go to the *same* vanishing point.
- **Error:** thinking overlap "hides" detail wrongly. **Fix:** overlap is a clue — the thing in front is *closer*.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name two ways to show something is far away. **A:** make it smaller; place it higher up; overlap it with nearer things (any two).
- **Q:** In one-point perspective, what is the vanishing point? **A:** the single point in the distance where parallel lines appear to meet.
- **Q:** A tree near the front vs one far away — which is bigger? **A:** the near one.

### Activity / practice
Draw a **one-point perspective scene** (a road or corridor) with a marked vanishing point and at least two objects sized for distance. (`speak`: explain how you showed depth.)
