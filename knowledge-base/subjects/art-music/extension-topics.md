Ten **additional** topics that deepen the eleven art topics and eleven music topics already in `art-ks1-ks2.md` and `music-ks1-ks2.md`, without repeating them. They add **more artists & movements** (Impressionism, Pop Art, Aboriginal art, Islamic geometric art), **digital art**, **more art techniques** (clay, textiles, photography basics), plus **more music**: notation in more depth, **instruments** (ukulele/recorder/keyboard basics), **world music**, **composing with structure**, **music technology**, and **performance skills**. Same per-topic contract as `_TEMPLATE.md`. As in the core files, the "Misconceptions" field records **common errors and difficulties**, not factual wrong answers. Keep naming the **formal elements** (art) and the **inter-related dimensions** (music); run a **sketchbook** through art and the **Listen → Perform → Compose** cycle through music. Everything here is doable at home with basic materials, voice and body.

## 1. Impressionism — light, colour & quick brushstrokes (Monet)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Knowledge – artists & movements

**Prerequisites:** Painting; Colour mixing & the colour wheel; Studying artists (core)

### Key concepts
- **Impressionism** (about 150 years ago) tried to capture a **moment** — the *impression* of light and colour — rather than a sharp, detailed photo.
- Impressionists used **small, quick, visible brushstrokes** and **broken colour** (dabs of pure colour placed side by side), often painting **outdoors**.
- Up close it looks like dabs; step back and your eye blends them into shimmering light.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can mix colours and use a brush, and has met the idea of "inspired by" an artist.

### Teach it (the explanation)
About 150 years ago, some painters (like **Claude Monet**) tried something new. Instead of slow, smooth, photo-perfect pictures, they wanted to catch a fleeting **moment** — how the **light and colour** looked *right now*, before a cloud moved. People mockingly called it **Impressionism** (from Monet's painting *Impression, Sunrise*) — and the name stuck.

How did they do it? With **small, quick, visible brushstrokes** — you can *see* each dab. And with **broken colour**: instead of mixing green on the palette, they'd put little dabs of blue and yellow *next to each other* on the canvas, and let your **eye** mix them from a distance. They often painted **outdoors** ("en plein air") to catch the real light — water lilies, gardens, sunlight on a river.

The magic: stand close and it's a mess of dabs; step back and it shimmers into light and water. The brushstrokes themselves become part of the picture.

**Worked example.** Paint an Impressionist "water" patch: 1) don't mix a flat blue — instead dab **separate** small strokes of blue, white and a little green side by side, 2) leave them as visible dabs, 3) step back and watch your eye blend them into shimmering water. Quick dabs, broken colour, from life.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Paint your garden or a window view "Impressionist-style" — quick, loose, no outlines.
- Squint at a Monet: the dabs melt into light.
- Catch a "moment of light" — sunrise, a puddle reflection — before it changes.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** trying to make it neat and detailed with outlines. **Why:** thinks "good art = realistic." **Fix:** Impressionism is about **light and impression**, not detail — loose dabs are the point.
- **Error:** mixing every colour smooth and flat. **Why:** habit. **Fix:** try **broken colour** — dab pure colours side by side and let the eye blend them.
- **Error:** "it just looks messy / unfinished." **Fix:** step back — the visible brushstrokes *are* the style; up close it's meant to look like dabs.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What were the Impressionists trying to capture? **A:** A fleeting moment — the impression of light and colour — not photo-perfect detail.
- **Q:** What is "broken colour"? **A:** Placing small dabs of pure colour side by side so the eye blends them from a distance.
- **Q:** Name an Impressionist artist. **A:** Claude Monet (also Renoir, Degas).

### Activity / practice
Paint a small Impressionist scene (water, sky or garden) using **quick visible brushstrokes** and **broken colour**, then view it from across the room. (`speak`: explain how the dabs blend; optional `matchPairs`: artist → painting, Monet → *Water Lilies*.)

### Resources
- **Tate Kids — *Who is Claude Monet?* / Impressionism**.
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Impressionism / painting light***.

## 2. Pop Art — bold, bright & everyday (Warhol, Lichtenstein)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Knowledge – artists & movements

**Prerequisites:** Studying artists (core); Colour (warm/cool); Printmaking (repetition)

### Key concepts
- **Pop Art** (about 60 years ago) made art from **everyday popular things** — soup cans, comics, famous faces, adverts.
- It used **bold flat colours, strong outlines, repetition** and the look of **printing and comics** (e.g. dots).
- It blurred "high art" and "everyday stuff," making ordinary objects into striking images.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child knows warm/cool colour and the idea of repeating an image (printmaking).

### Teach it (the explanation)
In the 1960s, artists like **Andy Warhol** and **Roy Lichtenstein** looked around at **everyday popular culture** — supermarket products, comics, pop stars, adverts — and said "*that* can be art!" This is **Pop Art** ("pop" from *popular*).

Its look is instantly recognisable: **bold, flat, bright colours**, strong black **outlines**, and **repetition** — Warhol repeated the same soup can or face in a grid, in different colours. Lichtenstein copied the style of **comic strips**, even the tiny **dots** (Ben-Day dots) used in cheap printing. Pop Art is loud, fun and a bit cheeky — it takes ordinary things and makes them eye-catching.

**Worked example.** Make a Warhol-style repeat: 1) draw one simple everyday object (a can, an ice cream, your trainer), 2) copy it into a 2×2 grid (trace or redraw four times), 3) colour each one in **different bold flat colours**. Same object, four bright versions — that's the Pop Art "repetition" idea.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Turn a favourite snack, logo or face into Pop Art (bold colours, repeated).
- Add comic dots and a speech bubble (*POW!*) Lichtenstein-style.
- Spot Pop Art looks in adverts and packaging today.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** soft, shaded, realistic colouring. **Why:** habit. **Fix:** Pop Art is **flat and bold** — fill shapes with solid bright colour, strong outlines.
- **Error:** "everyday objects can't be art." **Why:** narrow idea of art. **Fix:** that's exactly the Pop Art idea — soup cans and comics *became* famous art.
- **Error:** thinking each repeat must look different and detailed. **Fix:** repetition with **colour changes** is the whole point — keep the shape the same.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What kind of subjects did Pop Art use? **A:** Everyday popular things — products, comics, famous faces, adverts.
- **Q:** Name two features of the Pop Art look. **A:** Any two: bold flat bright colours, strong outlines, repetition, comic dots.
- **Q:** Name a Pop Art artist. **A:** Andy Warhol or Roy Lichtenstein.

### Activity / practice
Make a Pop Art piece: repeat one everyday object in a grid with **bold flat colours** and strong outlines; optionally add comic dots/a speech bubble. (`matchPairs`: artist → famous work, Warhol → soup cans.)

### Resources
- **Tate Kids — *Who is Andy Warhol?* / Pop Art**.
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Pop Art***.

## 3. Aboriginal & Indigenous art — symbols, dots & storytelling (taught respectfully)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Knowledge – art from cultures around the world

**Prerequisites:** Pattern; Studying artists (core); Painting. **Tutor: teach with respect — this is a living, sacred tradition, not a "craft." Make art *inspired by* the ideas, don't copy sacred designs.**

### Key concepts
- **Aboriginal Australian art** is one of the world's **oldest continuous art traditions** (tens of thousands of years), used to **tell stories** and pass on knowledge of land, animals and ancestors.
- It often uses **symbols** (a circle for a waterhole/campsite, U-shapes for people, animal tracks) and, in some regions, **dot painting** in earthy colours.
- We learn about and are **inspired by** it respectfully — we don't copy specific sacred stories or designs, which belong to particular communities.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child knows about pattern and that art can "tell" or symbolise things; can paint dots/shapes.

### Teach it (the explanation)
*(Tutor: frame this with respect and care. Emphasise it is a living, often sacred tradition belonging to Aboriginal communities; we appreciate and take inspiration from its **ideas**, but do not copy specific sacred designs or claim its stories as our own.)*

On the other side of the world, **Aboriginal Australian** peoples have made art for **tens of thousands of years** — one of the oldest art traditions on Earth, and still living today. For them, art isn't just decoration: it **tells stories** and records important knowledge about the **land, animals, water and ancestors**, passed down through generations.

Aboriginal art often uses **symbols** rather than realistic pictures — for example a set of circles can mean a **waterhole or meeting place**, a **U-shape** can show a person sitting, and lines or dots can show **animal tracks** or journeys. In some regions, artists build pictures from thousands of tiny **dots** in **earthy colours** (ochre reds, yellows, browns, white, black). The patterns are beautiful *and* meaningful.

We learn about this with respect. We can make art **inspired by** the *ideas* — telling a story with our own symbols, using dots and earthy colours — but we don't copy sacred designs or take particular communities' stories, which belong to them.

**Worked example.** Tell *your own* simple story with symbols and dots: 1) invent a few of your own symbols (a circle for "home," a wavy line for "river"), 2) plan a tiny journey ("from home, along the river, to the park"), 3) paint it using **dots** in earthy colours to fill the shapes. Your story, your symbols — inspired by, not copied from, the tradition.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Tell a story from your own life using invented symbols and dots.
- Find Australia on a map; this art is tens of thousands of years old.
- Look (respectfully) at the earthy ochre colours and how dots build a whole picture.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** copying a specific Aboriginal painting or sacred symbol. **Why:** it looks like a fun style. **Fix:** be *inspired by* the ideas and make **your own** symbols and story — sacred designs belong to their communities.
- **Error:** "it's just dots / decoration." **Why:** missing the meaning. **Fix:** the symbols and patterns **tell stories** and carry real knowledge.
- **Error:** thinking it's "ancient and gone." **Fix:** it's a **living** tradition — Aboriginal artists make and sell art today.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is Aboriginal art often used for, beyond decoration? **A:** To tell stories and pass on knowledge about land, animals and ancestors.
- **Q:** What might a circle symbol represent in some Aboriginal art? **A:** A waterhole, campsite or meeting place.
- **Q:** Should we copy a sacred Aboriginal design exactly? **A:** No — we make our own art *inspired by* the ideas, respecting that the real designs belong to their communities.

### Activity / practice
Make a "story map" using **your own** symbols and **dot** technique in earthy colours, then tell its story aloud. (`speak`: explain your symbols and journey.)

### Resources
- **Tate Kids / National Gallery (online) — *Art from around the world*** (respectful introductions).
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Art from different cultures***.

## 4. Islamic geometric art & tessellation — pattern with maths

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Knowledge – pattern, culture, art–maths link

**Prerequisites:** Pattern; Drawing & line (shapes); links to maths (shape, symmetry)

### Key concepts
- **Islamic art** developed dazzling **geometric patterns** — repeating shapes that fit together perfectly, often based on circles, stars and polygons.
- A **tessellation** is a pattern of shapes that fit with **no gaps and no overlaps** (like tiles).
- These patterns use **symmetry and repetition**, linking art beautifully with maths.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child knows about pattern and basic 2-D shapes (square, triangle, hexagon).

### Teach it (the explanation)
In many parts of the Islamic world, artists created some of the most stunning **geometric patterns** ever made — covering mosques, tiles, books and screens. Because the tradition often avoided drawing people or animals in religious art, artists poured their skill into **shapes**: interlocking **stars, polygons and circles** repeating endlessly.

A key idea is **tessellation**: shapes that fit together with **no gaps and no overlaps**, like floor tiles. Squares tessellate; so do triangles and **hexagons** (think of a honeycomb). Islamic patterns combine these with **symmetry** (the same on each side) and **repetition** to make designs that seem to go on forever. It's art *and* maths at once — every pattern follows precise rules.

**Worked example.** Make a simple tessellation: 1) start with a shape that tiles — a **hexagon** or a square, 2) repeat it across the page so the edges **touch with no gaps**, 3) colour it in a repeating colour rule (e.g. ABAB). You've made a tessellating pattern, just as Islamic tile-makers did with more complex stars.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Honeycomb, bathroom tiles and football panels are tessellations — spot them.
- Use a hexagon or triangle template to tile a whole page.
- Look (with awe) at photos of patterned tiles from places like the Alhambra.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** leaving gaps or overlaps between shapes. **Why:** not all shapes tessellate. **Fix:** use shapes that fit perfectly (squares, triangles, hexagons); check for **no gaps, no overlaps**.
- **Error:** "patterns are just random pretty shapes." **Why:** missing the rules. **Fix:** these follow precise **symmetry and repetition** rules — it's maths as much as art.
- **Error:** thinking circles tessellate. **Why:** they look neat. **Fix:** circles leave gaps; that's why tile patterns use polygons and stars.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a tessellation? **A:** A pattern of shapes that fit together with no gaps and no overlaps.
- **Q:** Name a shape that tessellates. **A:** Square, triangle, or hexagon.
- **Q:** What two ideas do Islamic geometric patterns rely on? **A:** Symmetry and repetition (precise geometric rules).

### Activity / practice
Create a **tessellating pattern** from one tiling shape (hexagon/square/triangle) with no gaps, and colour it with a repeating rule. (`ordering`: continue a tessellation; `matchPairs`: shape → does/doesn't tessellate.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art / Maths — *Tessellation / geometric patterns***.
- **British Museum (online) — *Islamic geometric design*** learning pages (parent-guided).

## 5. Digital art — drawing and designing on a screen

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Technique – digital media

**Prerequisites:** Drawing & line; Colour; (links to computing – creating digital content)

### Key concepts
- **Digital art** is made on a device using a drawing app — with tools like **brush, fill, layers and undo**.
- The same **formal elements** apply (line, shape, colour, tone) — it's a new *tool*, not a different art.
- Digital art can be **edited and undone** freely, and **layers** let you change parts without ruining the rest.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can use a mouse/trackpad or tablet, and knows the formal elements.

### Teach it (the explanation)
You can make art on a **screen** too — that's **digital art**. Using a free drawing app, you paint with your finger, a stylus or a mouse. The same art knowledge applies: you still think about **line, shape, colour and tone** — the device is just a new **tool**, like swapping a pencil for paint.

Digital tools have superpowers paper doesn't: a **brush** of any size and colour, a **fill** tool to flood an area with colour instantly, **undo** to take back a mistake, and best of all **layers** — like clear sheets stacked on top of each other, so you can draw the background on one layer and the character on another, and change one without wrecking the other. You can also **zoom in** for detail and **save** to edit again later.

**Worked example.** Draw a simple character digitally with layers: 1) **Layer 1** — paint the background (sky), 2) **Layer 2** — draw the character on top, 3) decide the character's hat is wrong → just edit **Layer 2**, the sky stays perfect, 4) use **fill** to colour shapes fast. Layers + undo make experimenting safe.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Redraw a favourite doodle digitally and try ten colour versions in seconds (no waste).
- Make a digital birthday card or game character.
- Notice that the games and apps you love were drawn digitally.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** "digital art isn't 'real' art / it's cheating." **Why:** new medium. **Fix:** it uses the same formal elements and skill — it's a different tool, like paint vs pencil.
- **Error:** drawing everything on one layer, then unable to fix part of it. **Why:** doesn't use layers. **Fix:** put background and subject on **separate layers** so each can be edited alone.
- **Error:** "if I close it, it's gone." **Why:** doesn't save. **Fix:** save with a name; reopen and keep editing later.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name two tools a digital drawing app gives you that paper doesn't. **A:** Any two: undo, fill, layers, resizable brush, zoom, save-and-edit.
- **Q:** What are layers useful for? **A:** Drawing parts separately (e.g. background vs character) so you can change one without affecting the others.
- **Q:** Do the formal elements (line, colour, tone) still matter in digital art? **A:** Yes — it's the same art, just a new tool.

### Activity / practice
In a free drawing app, make a simple two-layer picture (background + subject), use **fill** and **undo**, and save it. (`speak`: explain why layers helped.)

### Resources
- **Tate Kids — *Digital art / make games & art online*** (free browser tools).
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art / Computing — *Digital art / creating images on a computer***.

## 6. Working with clay & textiles — 3-D making and joining

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Technique – clay (modelling) & textiles

**Prerequisites:** Sculpture & 3-D (form); Collage (texture)

### Key concepts
- **Clay** (or air-dry clay/salt dough) is shaped by **pinching, rolling (coils), and slabs**, and pieces are joined with **"score and slip"** (scratch and dampen).
- **Textiles** is art with **fabric, thread and yarn** — techniques include **weaving** (over-under), simple **sewing/running stitch**, and **appliqué** (stitching shapes on).
- Both are **3-D / tactile** crafts where **joining securely** is the key skill.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child has handled malleable material (dough) and used basic tools safely; knows about form and texture.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Two hands-on crafts that make real, touchable art.

**Clay.** Whether it's real clay, **air-dry clay** or **salt dough**, you shape it three main ways: **pinching** (squeeze a ball into a pot), **coiling** (roll long "sausages" and stack them into walls), and **slabs** (roll it flat like pastry and cut shapes). The trick to making pieces *stay* joined is **"score and slip"**: **scratch** both surfaces with crisscross lines and add a little water (**slip**), then press together — like glue made of clay. Smooth the join.

**Textiles.** This is art made with **fabric, thread and yarn**. **Weaving** crosses threads **over and under** each other (warp and weft) to make cloth — you can weave on a cardboard loom. **Sewing** uses a needle and thread; the simplest is **running stitch** (in-out, in-out, like a dashed line). **Appliqué** stitches or glues fabric shapes onto a background (like fabric collage). Textiles bring strong **texture and pattern**.

**Worked example.** Make a clay pot by **coiling**: 1) roll a flat base, 2) roll long coils, 3) **score and slip** the first coil onto the base and press, 4) stack more coils, scoring and slipping each join, 5) smooth the inside. Secure joins = a pot that won't fall apart when it dries.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Make a pinch-pot creature or a clay name tile.
- Weave a small mat or bookmark on a cardboard loom.
- Find weaving and stitching in clothes and blankets at home.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** pressing clay pieces together with no scoring, so they fall off when dry. **Why:** joins look stuck. **Fix:** always **score and slip** — scratch and dampen both sides first.
- **Error:** weaving that bunches up or has gaps. **Why:** uneven over-under or pulling too tight. **Fix:** keep a steady **over-under** rhythm and don't pull the threads tight.
- **Error:** "textiles isn't art, it's just sewing." **Fix:** weaving, stitching and appliqué are real art techniques full of texture, colour and pattern.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name two ways to shape clay. **A:** Any two: pinching, coiling, slabs (rolling).
- **Q:** What is "score and slip" for? **A:** To join clay pieces securely — scratch and dampen both surfaces, then press.
- **Q:** What does weaving do? **A:** Crosses threads over and under each other to make cloth/pattern.

### Activity / practice
Make a small clay piece using **coiling** with **score-and-slip** joins, OR weave a small mat on a cardboard loom with an over-under rhythm. (`speak`: explain how you joined/wove it securely.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Clay / 3-D modelling* and *Textiles / weaving***.
- **Tate Kids — *Make: clay and textiles* activities**.

## 7. Photography basics — framing, light & viewpoint

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Technique – photography & composition

**Prerequisites:** Sketchbooks & observational drawing (looking); Perspective & space (viewpoint)

### Key concepts
- A photograph is **composed** — you choose what's **in the frame** and what's left out.
- Simple ideas improve a photo: a clear **subject**, good **light** (avoid shooting into bright light), and an interesting **viewpoint** (high, low, close).
- The same **formal elements** (line, shape, colour, tone, pattern) make a photo strong.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can use a tablet/phone camera (with permission) and knows the formal elements.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A camera is an art tool, and a good photo is **composed**, not just snapped. **Composition** means choosing what goes **inside the frame** — move closer, step back, or shift sideways so the picture shows what you want and cuts out the clutter.

Three simple ideas:
1. **Subject** — decide what the photo is *of*, and make it clear (fill the frame, remove distractions behind it).
2. **Light** — photos love good light. Don't point the camera *into* a bright window or sun (the subject goes dark); have the light **on** your subject. Soft daylight is lovely.
3. **Viewpoint** — don't always shoot from standing height. Crouch **low**, climb **high**, or get **close** for a more interesting picture.
And the **formal elements** still apply: look for strong **line**, **shape**, **pattern** and **colour** in your shot.

**Worked example.** Photograph one object (a toy, a flower) three ways: 1) from **standing**, far away (boring, cluttered), 2) **close up** filling the frame with light on it, 3) from a **low** angle looking up. Compare — the close, well-lit, low shot is usually the strongest. You changed the *composition*, not the object.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A "photo safari" of textures or one colour around the house.
- Photograph a pet or plant from three viewpoints and pick the best.
- Notice composition in book covers, posters and adverts.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** "a good photo is just pointing and tapping." **Why:** cameras are easy. **Fix:** the choices — frame, light, viewpoint — make the difference; it's composition.
- **Error:** shooting into the light so the subject is a dark blob. **Why:** doesn't notice light direction. **Fix:** put the light **on** the subject, not behind it.
- **Error:** always standing far back. **Fix:** move **closer** and try **high/low** viewpoints.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does "composing" a photo mean? **A:** Choosing what's in the frame (and what's left out) — subject, light, viewpoint.
- **Q:** Why shouldn't you usually photograph straight into a bright window? **A:** The subject comes out dark; you want light *on* the subject.
- **Q:** Name one way to make a photo more interesting. **A:** Get closer / fill the frame; try a low or high viewpoint; find strong line, shape or colour.

### Activity / practice
Take three photos of one subject changing **viewpoint and framing**, then choose and explain the best using a formal-element word. (`speak`: justify your choice.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Art — *Photography / composition***.
- **Tate Kids — *Photography ideas / look closely***.

## 8. Playing an instrument — ukulele, recorder & keyboard basics

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Performing – instruments

**Prerequisites:** Pitch (high & low); Rhythm & note values; Pulse & beat

### Key concepts
- On a tuned instrument, **higher notes** sound higher and **lower notes** lower — you can play a **melody** by choosing pitches in time.
- A few **easy starting points**: one ukulele chord (e.g. **C**), the first recorder note (**B**), or finding **C–D–E** on a keyboard.
- Playing combines **pitch** (right note) and **pulse/rhythm** (right time) — start slow and steady.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can keep a pulse and tell high from low pitch. (Any one instrument, or a keyboard app, is enough.)

### Teach it (the explanation)
*(Tutor: adapt to whatever instrument the learner has — ukulele, recorder, keyboard, or a free keyboard app. The principles are the same.)*

Playing a tuned instrument brings **pitch** and **pulse** together in your hands. The golden rules are: **find the right note** (pitch) and play it **in time** (pulse), starting **slow and steady** before speeding up.

Easy starting points by instrument:
- **Keyboard/piano:** the white keys go **low (left) to high (right)**. Find a group of **two black keys** — the white key just to their **left** is **C**. Then **C–D–E** are the next three white keys going right. You can play a simple tune (like the first notes of "Mary Had a Little Lamb": E–D–C–D–E–E–E).
- **Ukulele:** strumming one easy **chord** (like **C** — just one finger) in time already makes music; strum on the **pulse**.
- **Recorder:** the first easy note is usually **B** (left thumb + first finger), blown **gently** (not hard) with the tongue saying "doo."

**Worked example.** Play "Mary Had a Little Lamb" on a keyboard: find **C–D–E** (the white keys by the two black keys). The tune starts **E–D–C–D–E–E–E**. Play it **slowly, one note per beat**, keeping the pulse, then try a little faster. Right notes + steady pulse = a recognisable tune.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Play the first few notes of a song you love.
- Strum a ukulele chord along to a song's pulse.
- Use a free keyboard app to find C–D–E if you have no instrument.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** rushing to play fast and hitting wrong notes. **Why:** excitement. **Fix:** **slow and steady first** — accuracy before speed; keep the pulse.
- **Error:** blowing a recorder hard (it screeches). **Why:** thinks louder = better. **Fix:** blow **gently**, like a warm "doo"; soft breath gives a clean note.
- **Error:** "I'll never learn an instrument." **Why:** it looks hard. **Fix:** start with **one** note or **one** chord — everyone can play that; skill grows with practice.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** On a keyboard, which way are the higher notes? **A:** To the right.
- **Q:** What two things must you get right to play a tune? **A:** The right notes (pitch) and the right timing (pulse/rhythm).
- **Q:** Should you start fast or slow when learning a new tune? **A:** Slow and steady — accuracy first.

### Activity / practice
**Perform:** play a 3–5 note tune (or strum one chord) **in time** with the pulse, slowly, then a little faster. (`speak`/perform; tutor checks notes and timing.)

### Resources
- **Chrome Music Lab — *Song Maker / Shared Piano*** (play notes in-browser, free, no instrument needed).
- **BBC Teach / Oak National Academy — *Recorder / ukulele / keyboard beginner* lessons**.

## 9. World music — rhythms and instruments from around the globe

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Listening & performing – world music

**Prerequisites:** Pulse & beat; Rhythm & note values; Timbre & instrument families; Listening & appraising (core)

### Key concepts
- Music sounds **different around the world** — different **instruments, rhythms and scales** (e.g. African drumming, Indian sitar/tabla, Caribbean steel pans, Spanish guitar).
- Many traditions are built on strong **rhythm and repetition**, often played **together as a group** (e.g. drum circles).
- You can join in by **clapping or drumming a repeating rhythm (an ostinato)** and **layering** parts.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can keep a pulse, clap rhythms and describe timbre.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Music isn't the same everywhere — every part of the world has its own sounds, **instruments and rhythms**. A quick world tour:
- **West African drumming** — powerful, layered **rhythms** on drums like the **djembe**, often played by a **group** with each player adding a part.
- **Indian classical music** — the **sitar** (a long-necked string instrument) and **tabla** (hand drums), with intricate rhythms.
- **Caribbean steel pans** — bright, ringing melodies on drums made from oil barrels (Trinidad).
- **Spanish flamenco** — passionate **guitar**, clapping (*palmas*) and dance.

Many world traditions are built on strong **rhythm, repetition and playing together**. A repeating rhythm pattern is called an **ostinato** — a short pattern you play over and over while others layer different parts on top. That's how a drum group builds a rich **texture** from simple repeating parts.

**Worked example.** Build a layered "drum circle" with body percussion: 1) Person A keeps a steady **pulse** (pat-pat-pat-pat), 2) Person B adds a short repeating **ostinato** on top ("clap—clap-clap"), 3) layer a third pattern. Each part is simple and **repeats**; together they make a rich, full texture — just like West African drumming.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Listen to music from a different country each week and describe its instruments and rhythm.
- Make a body-percussion drum circle with the family, each on a repeating part.
- Find where each instrument comes from on a world map.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** "real music is just the pop/classical I know." **Why:** narrow exposure. **Fix:** value **all** traditions — each has rich, skilful music.
- **Error:** playing a different rhythm each time instead of repeating. **Why:** misunderstanding ostinato. **Fix:** an **ostinato repeats** — keep the same short pattern going.
- **Error:** everyone playing the same thing, so no texture. **Fix:** **layer different** repeating parts to build a full sound.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is an ostinato? **A:** A short rhythm (or melody) pattern that repeats over and over.
- **Q:** Name a world instrument and where it's from. **A:** e.g. djembe (West Africa), sitar/tabla (India), steel pan (Caribbean), flamenco guitar (Spain).
- **Q:** How does a drum group make a rich sound from simple parts? **A:** By **layering** different repeating patterns together (building texture).

### Activity / practice
**Perform:** create a **layered ostinato** with body percussion — keep one steady pulse, add a repeating pattern on top, then layer a third part. Describe the world style it reminds you of. (`speak`/perform; optional `matchPairs`: instrument → country.)

### Resources
- **BBC Ten Pieces / BBC Bitesize KS2 Music — *World music / rhythms around the world***.
- **BBC Teach — *Music from around the world* clips (djembe, steel pans, sitar)**.

## 10. Composing with structure & music technology

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Composing – structure & music tech

**Prerequisites:** Composing a short piece (core); Simple notation; Dynamics & tempo; The elements together

### Key concepts
- Music has **structure** beyond beginning-middle-end: **verse–chorus**, **call-and-response**, and the **ABA** form (a section, a contrasting section, then the first again).
- A **motif** (a short memorable musical idea) can be **repeated and developed** to hold a piece together.
- **Music technology** (apps like Chrome Music Lab Song Maker, or a simple loop/garage app) lets you **build, layer, edit and play back** a structured piece without instruments.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can compose a short piece with a beginning–middle–end and choose dynamics/tempo on purpose.

### Teach it (the explanation)
You can already make a short piece with a beginning, middle and end. Now give your music a stronger **structure** — the *plan* of its sections:
- **Verse–chorus** — verses tell the story (words change), the chorus returns the same catchy bit (like most pop songs).
- **Call-and-response** — one part "asks," another "answers" (common in world and folk music).
- **ABA (ternary) form** — an **A** section, a **different B** section, then **A** again. The return of A makes it feel complete and satisfying.

A great trick is a **motif**: a short, memorable idea (just a few notes or a rhythm) that you **repeat and develop** so the piece feels joined-up — think of the famous *da-da-da-DAA* in Beethoven's 5th.

**Music technology** makes structured composing easy: apps like **Chrome Music Lab Song Maker** let you place notes on a grid (higher = higher pitch), **layer** a melody and a beat, set the **tempo**, then **play it back** and **edit** freely — like digital art for sound. You can build an ABA piece by making section A, a contrasting B, then copying A back.

**Worked example.** Compose an **ABA** piece in a music app: 1) **A** — a short, gentle 8-beat melody (your motif), 2) **B** — a contrasting section (faster, or higher, or louder), 3) **A again** — repeat the first melody. Play it back: the return of A makes it feel "finished." Structure planned on purpose.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Spot verse–chorus and repeated motifs in your favourite songs.
- Build a layered beat + melody in a free music app and remix it.
- Compose a short ABA "theme tune" for yourself or a game.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** a piece that wanders with no plan or repeated ideas. **Why:** improvising without structure. **Fix:** choose a **structure** (verse–chorus or ABA) and use a **motif** that returns.
- **Error:** in ABA, making the final A totally different. **Why:** misunderstanding the form. **Fix:** the last **A repeats the first A** — that return is the point.
- **Error:** "music tech does the composing for me." **Why:** the app plays sounds. **Fix:** *you* make the choices (notes, layers, tempo, structure); the app is a tool, like a digital instrument.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is ABA (ternary) form? **A:** A section A, a contrasting section B, then section A again.
- **Q:** What is a motif? **A:** A short, memorable musical idea that's repeated and developed to hold a piece together.
- **Q:** Name one thing music technology lets you do when composing. **A:** Layer parts, set tempo, edit, and play back a piece without instruments.

### Activity / practice
**Compose:** make an **ABA** piece (in a music app or with voice/body), using a clear **motif** in section A that returns at the end, and choose tempo/dynamics on purpose; play it back. (`speak`: explain your structure; optional `ordering`: label sections A–B–A.)

### Resources
- **Chrome Music Lab — *Song Maker*** (layer melody and beat, set tempo, play back; free).
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Music — *Structure / verse and chorus / composing*** and **BBC Ten Pieces — *motifs***.

## 11. Performance skills — sharing your art and music with confidence

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Performing & presenting (art & music)

**Prerequisites:** Singing; Composing; (art) Studying artists / making a finished piece

### Key concepts
- **Performing or presenting** your work is a skill: prepare, then share clearly and confidently.
- For **music**: practise, start together (a count-in "1-2-3-4"), keep going if you slip, and finish clearly.
- For **art**: you can **present** your work too — say what it is, how you made it, and one thing you're proud of (an "artist's statement").

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child has made a piece of music or art to share; has met nerves/excitement about performing.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Making art and music is wonderful — and so is **sharing** it. Performing or presenting is its own skill, and a little preparation makes it feel great instead of scary.

**Performing music:** First, **practise** until it's comfortable. Then: take a breath and **start together** with a count-in ("1-2-3-4"), keep a steady **pulse**, and — important — **if you make a slip, keep going** (don't stop; most listeners won't even notice). **Finish clearly** so people know it's the end. Stand or sit tall, and remember nerves are normal — even famous musicians feel them.

**Presenting art:** you can "perform" a picture too, by **presenting** it — this is an **artist's statement**. Say: *what* it is, *how* you made it (which techniques/formal elements), and *one thing you're proud of* or would improve. Speaking about your work helps others appreciate it and helps you reflect.

Either way, be a kind **audience** for others too: look, listen, and give a specific compliment ("I loved the bold colours / the strong pulse").

**Worked example.** Present a piece of music: 1) "This is a short ABA piece I composed about a storm." 2) Count-in: "1-2-3-4," play it, keeping going even if you slip. 3) Finish clearly and say "thank you." 4) Reflect: "I'm proud of the loud middle section." Prepare → start together → keep going → finish → reflect.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Put on a mini "concert" or "gallery opening" for the family.
- Watch how performers start together and finish a piece.
- Give a kind, specific compliment to someone else's work.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** stopping and restarting the moment you make a mistake. **Why:** wants it perfect. **Fix:** **keep going** — slips are normal and usually unnoticed; carry on to the end.
- **Error:** starting before everyone's ready (in a group). **Why:** no signal. **Fix:** use a clear **count-in** so everyone starts together.
- **Error:** "I'm too nervous / no good at performing." **Why:** fear. **Fix:** nerves are normal and improve with practice; preparing well builds confidence — and presenting art by *talking* about it is performing too.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What should you do if you make a small mistake while performing? **A:** Keep going — don't stop; most listeners won't notice.
- **Q:** How do musicians start a piece together? **A:** With a count-in (e.g. "1-2-3-4").
- **Q:** Name two things to say when presenting your art (an artist's statement). **A:** Any two: what it is, how you made it, one thing you're proud of or would improve.

### Activity / practice
**Perform/present:** rehearse, then perform a short piece (count-in, keep going, finish clearly) **or** present a finished artwork with a 3-part artist's statement; then give a specific compliment to another piece. (`speak`/perform.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Music — *Performing / performance skills***.
- **Tate Kids — *Talk about your art / artist statements***.
