# Music — KS1 & KS2 (Years 1–6), UK National Curriculum

Eleven topics in the per-topic contract (`_TEMPLATE.md`). All are doable at home with **voice, body and household sounds** — no instruments required (though they help). The "Misconceptions" field records **common errors and difficulties**. Name the **inter-related dimensions** (pulse, rhythm, pitch, tempo, dynamics, timbre, texture, structure) throughout, and run the cycle **Listen → Perform → Compose**.

## 1. Pulse & beat

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–2 (ages 5–7) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Dimensions – pulse

**Prerequisites:** none

### Key concepts
- The **pulse** is the steady heartbeat of music — even, regular, never speeding up randomly.
- You can clap, tap, march or nod to the pulse.
- Every piece of music has a pulse underneath it.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can clap and copy a simple action.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Music has a **pulse** — a steady beat, like a heartbeat or a clock ticking: *tick, tick, tick, tick.* It's even and regular. Put on any song and you can feel it: clap, tap your knees, nod your head, or march on the spot — all in time. The pulse keeps everyone together. Feel your own heartbeat (or a clock) — that steadiness is exactly what a musical pulse is.

**Worked example.** Find the pulse in a song: 1) play a steady song, 2) tap your knees on every beat — *tap, tap, tap, tap*, 3) try marching to it without rushing or dragging. You've locked onto the pulse.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Your heartbeat is a pulse — feel it, then march to a song's pulse.
- Marching, skipping, a ticking clock, a dripping tap.
- Keep the pulse to a favourite song while it plays.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** speeding up or slowing down the clapping. **Why:** not yet steady. **Fix:** clap along with a ticking clock or metronome to feel "even."
- **Error:** confusing pulse with rhythm (clapping the *words* instead of the steady beat). **Why:** the two feel similar at first. **Fix:** pulse = the unchanging heartbeat; words come later (Topic 2).
- **Error:** stopping when the music gets quiet. **Fix:** the pulse keeps going even in quiet bits.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is the pulse in music? **A:** the steady, regular beat — like a heartbeat.
- **Q:** Show me the pulse of this song. **A:** child taps/claps evenly in time.
- **Q:** Does the pulse speed up and slow down randomly? **A:** no — it stays steady.

### Activity / practice
**Perform:** keep a steady pulse (clap or tap knees) all the way through a short song without rushing. (`speak`/perform; tutor checks evenness.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Music — *Pulse / Beat***.
- **BBC Ten Pieces — *Keeping the pulse* listening activities**.

## 2. Rhythm & note values

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2–4 (ages 6–9) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Dimensions – rhythm

**Prerequisites:** Pulse & beat

### Key concepts
- **Rhythm** is the *pattern* of long and short sounds that sits on top of the pulse.
- Rhythm and pulse are different: pulse is steady; rhythm changes.
- Words and names have rhythms you can clap (e.g. "ba-na-na").

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can keep a steady pulse.

### Teach it (the explanation)
On top of the steady **pulse**, music has **rhythm** — a *pattern* of longer and shorter sounds. Clap the pulse with one hand on your knee, then clap a *word* on top: "**cat**" is one clap (a long beat — a **crotchet**); "**ti-ger**" is two quick claps in one beat (two **quavers**); "**el-e-phant**" is three quick sounds. Names are great: "**Tom**" = one beat, "**A-my**" = two, "**O-li-vi-a**" = four quick. Pulse stays steady underneath while the rhythm dances on top.

**Worked example.** Clap the rhythm of "I like jelly": say it in time over a steady pulse — *I (1) like (1) jel-ly (2 quick)* → clap, clap, clap-clap. The pulse counts 1-2-3; the rhythm fits inside it.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Clap the rhythm of everyone's names in your family.
- Clap the rhythm of foods: "ba-na-na", "choc-o-late", "pizza".
- Tap a song's rhythm vs its pulse and feel the difference.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** clapping the pulse when asked for the rhythm (or vice-versa). **Why:** the two are easily mixed. **Fix:** keep the pulse with your feet and clap the rhythm with your hands so you feel both at once.
- **Error:** rushing the quick (quaver) sounds out of time. **Why:** losing the pulse. **Fix:** keep the steady pulse underneath; fit two quick claps into one beat.
- **Error:** thinking longer words always = more beats. **Fix:** it's about how the sounds fit the pulse, not word length alone.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What's the difference between pulse and rhythm? **A:** pulse is the steady beat; rhythm is the pattern of long/short sounds on top.
- **Q:** Clap the rhythm of "ba-na-na." **A:** three quick even claps.
- **Q:** How many claps for "Tom" vs "A-my"? **A:** Tom = 1; A-my = 2.

### Activity / practice
**Perform/compose:** keep a pulse with your feet and clap the rhythm of three names or foods; then make up a short clapped rhythm for someone else to copy. (`speak`/perform; optional `ordering` of short/long sounds.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Music — *Rhythm / Note values***.
- **Oak National Academy — *Music: rhythm and pulse* unit**.

## 3. Pitch (high & low)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–3 (ages 5–8) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Dimensions – pitch

**Prerequisites:** Pulse & beat

### Key concepts
- **Pitch** is how **high** or **low** a sound is.
- Pitch is *not* the same as loud/quiet — high can be soft, low can be loud.
- A tune (melody) is a pattern of pitches going up and down.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can use their voice to make different sounds.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Pitch** means how **high** or **low** a sound is. A little bird or a mouse "**eee**" is high; a big lion or a drum "**rrr**" is low. Make your voice slide up high and down low — your hand can rise and fall to show it. A **melody** (tune) is just a journey of pitches going up, down or staying the same. Careful: high/low is about pitch, *not* volume — you can sing high *and* quietly.

**Worked example.** Sing "up the stairs": start low and step your voice up — "one, two, three, four" each note higher — then come back down. Raise your hand as the pitch rises, lower it as it falls. That up-and-down is pitch.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Animal voices: a mouse (high) vs a lion (low).
- A slide whistle / a finger up a piano = pitch going up.
- Hum a familiar tune and trace its ups and downs with your hand.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** saying a loud sound is "high" and a quiet one "low." **Why:** confusing pitch with dynamics. **Fix:** demonstrate a high-quiet note and a low-loud note — they're different things.
- **Error:** thinking "high" means "fast." **Why:** mixing dimensions. **Fix:** high is about pitch, not speed (tempo).
- **Error:** keeping the voice flat when asked to go high/low. **Fix:** exaggerate with a hand gesture rising/falling to map the pitch.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does pitch mean? **A:** how high or low a sound is.
- **Q:** Is a mouse's squeak high or low? **A:** high.
- **Q:** Can a high sound be quiet? **A:** yes — pitch and volume are different.

### Activity / practice
**Perform:** sing or hum a tune (e.g. "Twinkle Twinkle") and trace the pitch up/down with your hand; then make up a 3-note "up" pattern and a "down" pattern. (`speak`/perform.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1/KS2 Music — *Pitch (high and low)***.
- **Chrome Music Lab — *Melody Maker* (drag notes high/low)**.

## 4. Singing

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Performing – voice

**Prerequisites:** Pulse & beat, Pitch (high & low)

### Key concepts
- Singing uses **pitch** (in tune), **pulse** (in time) and **dynamics** together.
- Good singing starts with a breath, a tall posture and listening to match the note.
- Songs often have a **structure** (verse and chorus) you can follow.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can feel a pulse and slide their voice high/low.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Singing brings the dimensions together: stay **in time** with the pulse and **in tune** with the pitch, and shape the **dynamics** (louder/softer) to suit the song. Sit or stand tall, take a relaxed breath, and **listen** to the note you're matching before you sing. Many songs have a **structure**: a **verse** (the story bit, words change) and a **chorus** (the same catchy bit that returns). Warm up gently — a few "slides" and easy hums.

**Worked example.** Learn a short song in steps: 1) listen once and find the pulse (tap it), 2) echo one line at a time after the tutor, 3) join the lines together, 4) add dynamics — sing the chorus a little stronger. Listen, copy, join, shape.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sing a favourite song and spot the chorus that comes back.
- Echo songs / call-and-response — tutor sings, child answers.
- Add actions to a song to remember the words.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** shouting to sing louder, losing the tune. **Why:** confusing loud with strong. **Fix:** support with breath, keep the pitch — loud isn't shouting.
- **Error:** rushing ahead of the pulse. **Why:** excitement / not listening. **Fix:** tap the pulse while singing to stay in time.
- **Error:** "I can't sing." **Why:** fear of being out of tune. **Fix:** singing in tune is a *skill that improves* — start with easy echo songs; everyone can develop it.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name two things you do to sing well. **A:** stay in time (pulse), in tune (pitch); breathe; sit/stand tall (any two).
- **Q:** What's the difference between a verse and a chorus? **A:** verse changes/tells the story; chorus repeats and is the catchy returning part.
- **Q:** Should you shout to sing louder? **A:** no — use breath and stay in tune.

### Activity / practice
**Perform:** learn a short song by echoing it line by line, then sing it through, keeping the pulse and singing the chorus a little stronger. (`speak`/perform; tutor checks timing & pitch.)

### Resources
- **BBC Teach — *Primary singing assemblies / songs***.
- **Oak National Academy — *Music: singing* units**.

## 5. Dynamics & tempo

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2–4 (ages 6–9) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Dimensions – dynamics, tempo

**Prerequisites:** Pulse & beat

### Key concepts
- **Dynamics** = how **loud or quiet** the music is (and getting louder/softer).
- **Tempo** = how **fast or slow** the music is (and getting faster/slower).
- Dynamics and tempo are *different* — you can be loud-and-slow, or quiet-and-fast.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can keep a steady pulse.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Two more dimensions control the *feel* of music. **Dynamics** is the **volume** — *loud* (musicians say *forte*) or *quiet* (*piano*), and you can get gradually louder or softer. **Tempo** is the **speed** — *fast* or *slow*, and you can speed up or slow down. They're independent: a lullaby is quiet **and** slow; a marching band can be loud **and** fast; but you can also have a loud, *slow* drum, or a quiet, *fast* tiptoe. Don't mix them up.

**Worked example.** Perform a "tiptoe to stomp" pulse: 1) start tapping **quiet and slow** (sneaking), 2) gradually get **louder and faster** (a giant approaching), 3) end **loud and fast** (stomp!). You changed both dynamics and tempo on purpose.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Tiptoe music (quiet) vs giant music (loud); race music (fast) vs lullaby (slow).
- A police siren getting louder as it nears = a dynamics change.
- Conduct: your hands big = loud, small = quiet.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** thinking "louder" automatically means "faster." **Why:** they often happen together, so feel linked. **Fix:** demonstrate loud+slow and quiet+fast to prove they're separate.
- **Error:** using "loud/quiet" and "fast/slow" interchangeably. **Fix:** dynamics = volume words; tempo = speed words. Keep two separate labels.
- **Error:** jumping straight from quiet to loud with no in-between. **Fix:** practise *gradually* getting louder/softer (crescendo / diminuendo).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does dynamics mean? **A:** how loud or quiet the music is.
- **Q:** What does tempo mean? **A:** how fast or slow the music is.
- **Q:** Can music be loud and slow at the same time? **A:** yes — dynamics and tempo are different.

### Activity / practice
**Perform/compose:** clap or play a pulse that starts quiet & slow and gets loud & fast, then describe what you changed using the words *dynamics* and *tempo*. (`categorize`: sort sounds into loud/quiet and fast/slow.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Music — *Dynamics and Tempo***.
- **Chrome Music Lab — experiment with speed/volume**.

## 6. Timbre & instrument families

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2–5 (ages 6–10) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Dimensions – timbre

**Prerequisites:** Pitch (high & low)

### Key concepts
- **Timbre** is the *type* of sound — what makes a drum, a flute and a voice sound different even on the same note.
- Instruments group into **families**: strings, woodwind, brass, percussion (and voices, keyboards).
- You can identify instruments by their timbre when listening.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child has heard a few different instruments.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Timbre** (say "TAM-ber") is the *colour* or *character* of a sound. A drum, a flute and your voice can play the same pitch but still sound totally different — that difference is timbre. Instruments fall into **families** by how they make sound:
- **Strings** — bowed or plucked strings (violin, cello, guitar, harp).
- **Woodwind** — blown, often with reeds/holes (flute, clarinet, recorder).
- **Brass** — buzzed lips into metal tubes (trumpet, trombone, horn).
- **Percussion** — hit or shaken (drum, tambourine, xylophone, shaker).
At home, household objects have timbres too: a wooden spoon on a pot (percussion-ish), a comb-and-paper buzz, a tapped glass.

**Worked example.** Sort sounds by timbre: tap a metal pan (bright, ringing), then a cushion (dull, soft), then a glass of water (clear "ting"). Same action (a tap), three different timbres — describe each in words.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Make a "kitchen band" and sort the sounds into bright/dull, ringing/thuddy.
- Listen to a piece and guess the instrument by its sound.
- Match animal/cartoon characters to instrument timbres.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** thinking timbre = pitch (high/low). **Why:** mixing dimensions. **Fix:** two instruments can play the *same* pitch but have different timbres.
- **Error:** putting a piano in "strings" or a recorder in "brass." **Why:** unclear family rules. **Fix:** group by *how* the sound is made (hit/blown/bowed) — piano is keyboard (strings struck), recorder is woodwind.
- **Error:** describing timbre only as "nice/horrible." **Fix:** use richer words — bright, dull, ringing, buzzy, soft, harsh.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is timbre? **A:** the type/character of a sound — what makes instruments sound different.
- **Q:** Which family is a trumpet in? **A:** brass.
- **Q:** A drum and a flute play the same note — why do they sound different? **A:** different timbre.

### Activity / practice
**Listen/perform:** make three different sounds with household objects and describe each timbre (bright/dull/ringing/buzzy); sort them by family-like type. (`categorize`: sort instruments into strings/woodwind/brass/percussion.)

### Resources
- **BBC Ten Pieces — *Instruments of the orchestra* clips**.
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Music — *Timbre / Instruments***.

## 7. The elements together

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–5 (ages 7–10) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Dimensions – combined; texture

**Prerequisites:** Pulse & beat, Rhythm & note values, Pitch, Dynamics & tempo, Timbre

### Key concepts
- Real music uses many **dimensions at once** — pulse, rhythm, pitch, tempo, dynamics, timbre, texture.
- **Texture** is how many layers of sound play together — thin (one) to thick (many).
- You can describe any piece by naming several dimensions.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child knows pulse, rhythm, pitch, dynamics, tempo and timbre separately.

### Teach it (the explanation)
You've met the dimensions one at a time — now hear them **together**, because real music uses them all at once. A new one to add is **texture**: how many layers of sound are playing. One voice alone is a **thin** texture; a whole band or choir is a **thick** texture. When you listen, you can describe a piece by naming several dimensions: "fast (tempo), loud (dynamics), with a strong drum pulse and a high singing melody (pitch/timbre), and a thick texture (lots of layers)."

**Worked example.** Listen and label a short piece: ask in turn — Pulse fast or slow? Loud or quiet? High or low melody? What instruments (timbre)? Many layers or few (texture)? Build a full description from the answers. That's "appraising" music.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Describe your favourite song using four dimension words.
- Compare a quiet thin lullaby with a loud thick anthem.
- "Music detective" — listen and report what's happening.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** describing music only with one word ("good"/"fast"). **Why:** small vocabulary. **Fix:** use a checklist of dimensions to say *several* things.
- **Error:** confusing texture (layers) with timbre (sound type). **Fix:** texture = *how many* sounds at once; timbre = *what kind* of sound.
- **Error:** thinking a thick texture is always "better." **Fix:** thin textures (a solo) can be just as powerful.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does texture mean in music? **A:** how many layers of sound play at once (thin = few, thick = many).
- **Q:** Name three dimensions you could use to describe a song. **A:** any three, e.g. tempo, dynamics, pitch, timbre, texture.
- **Q:** Is one singer alone a thin or thick texture? **A:** thin.

### Activity / practice
**Listen:** play a short clip and describe it using at least four dimension words (pulse/tempo, dynamics, pitch, timbre, texture). (`speak`: give the spoken description.)

### Resources
- **BBC Ten Pieces — listening clips with the *elements of music***.
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Music — *Elements / dimensions of music***.

## 8. Simple notation

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Reading – notation

**Prerequisites:** Rhythm & note values, Pitch (high & low)

### Key concepts
- **Notation** is writing music down so it can be played again the same way.
- **Graphic notation** uses pictures/symbols; **standard notation** uses notes on lines (a stave).
- Note shapes show how long a sound lasts; higher on the stave = higher pitch.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can clap rhythms and tell high from low pitch.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Notation** means writing music down so someone else (or future-you) can play it the same way. The easy start is **graphic notation** — invent your own symbols: a long line for a long sound, a dot for a short one, a wavy line for a slide, big = loud. Then there's **standard notation**: notes sit on a **stave** (5 lines); the note's *shape* tells you how long it lasts (a filled note with no tail = one beat, a **crotchet**; two with a tail = two quick **quavers**); the note's *height* on the stave tells you the pitch — higher up = higher sound.

**Worked example.** Write a rhythm in graphic notation: for "I like jelly" draw — long dash (I), long dash (like), then two small dots joined (jel-ly). Anyone can read your dashes and dots and clap it back exactly.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Invent secret music symbols and swap with a sibling to "decode" and clap.
- Notice that higher notes sit higher on the page — like a staircase.
- Write down a clapping pattern so you can play it again tomorrow.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** thinking you must read full standard notation to make music. **Why:** notation looks hard. **Fix:** start with graphic notation — your own symbols are real notation too.
- **Error:** ignoring the *shape* of a note and just playing every note the same length. **Fix:** the shape (filled/stemmed/tailed) tells you the duration.
- **Error:** reading the stave like writing (left-right only) and missing the up/down pitch. **Fix:** higher on the stave = higher pitch.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is notation for? **A:** to write music down so it can be played the same way again.
- **Q:** On a stave, what does a note higher up mean? **A:** a higher pitch.
- **Q:** What's the difference between graphic and standard notation? **A:** graphic uses pictures/symbols; standard uses notes on lines (a stave).

### Activity / practice
**Compose/read:** write a 4-beat rhythm in your own graphic notation (long/short symbols), then swap and clap each other's. (`ordering`: arrange note symbols to make a given rhythm.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Music — *Reading music / Notation***.
- **Chrome Music Lab — *Song Maker* (place notes on a grid; higher = higher pitch)**.

## 9. Composing a short piece

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Composing – creating music

**Prerequisites:** Rhythm & note values, Pitch, Dynamics & tempo, Structure (start–middle–end)

### Key concepts
- **Composing** is making up your own music and being able to repeat it.
- A simple piece has a **structure**: a beginning, a middle and an end.
- You choose dimensions on purpose: tempo, dynamics, instruments (timbre), a repeating pattern.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can clap rhythms, change dynamics/tempo, and has met simple notation.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Composing** is inventing your own music — and the key is being able to **play it the same way twice** (so record it or write it down). Give your piece a **structure**: a clear **beginning**, **middle** and **end**. Decide on purpose: How fast (tempo)? Loud or quiet, and does it change (dynamics)? What sounds (timbre — voice, claps, pots)? Maybe a short pattern that **repeats** so it feels like "your tune." Compose to a picture or story for ideas (a storm, a journey, a creature).

**Worked example.** Compose a "weather" piece in three parts: 1) **Beginning** — quiet, slow finger-taps (light rain), 2) **Middle** — louder, faster claps and a pan-drum (a storm), 3) **End** — gradually quiet again, slow taps fading out (rain stops). Play it twice the same way — now it's composed.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Compose music for a story, a video game, or a weather scene.
- Make a theme tune for yourself or a pet.
- Compose with a kitchen band and perform it for the family.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** random noise with no plan, never the same twice. **Why:** improvising without structure. **Fix:** plan a beginning–middle–end and *repeat* it — composing means you can play it again.
- **Error:** forgetting to use the dimensions on purpose. **Fix:** decide tempo, dynamics and timbre deliberately, not by accident.
- **Error:** thinking it must be long/complicated. **Fix:** a great short piece (8–16 beats) with a clear shape is plenty.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does composing mean? **A:** making up your own music that you can play the same way again.
- **Q:** Name the three parts a simple piece should have. **A:** a beginning, a middle and an end.
- **Q:** Name two things you choose on purpose when composing. **A:** any two: tempo, dynamics, timbre, a repeating pattern.

### Activity / practice
**Compose:** create a short piece with a beginning, middle and end (e.g. a weather or journey piece), choosing tempo and dynamics on purpose, then perform it twice the same way. (`speak`: explain your structure and choices.)

### Resources
- **Chrome Music Lab — *Song Maker* (compose and play back a short piece)**.
- **Oak National Academy — *Music: composing* units**.

## 10. Listening & appraising (a range of styles)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Listening – appraising

**Prerequisites:** The elements together

### Key concepts
- **Appraising** = listening carefully and describing/judging music using the dimensions.
- Listen to a **wide range of styles** — classical, folk, pop, jazz, world music.
- There's no "right" taste, but you can explain *why* using musical words.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can name several dimensions of music.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Appraising** means really listening and then describing or giving an opinion about music — backed up with **musical reasons**. The curriculum wants children to hear a **wide range** of music, so listen to lots of styles: orchestral/classical, folk, pop, jazz, and music from different cultures and countries. After a clip, say what you *noticed* (pulse, tempo, dynamics, instruments, mood), then what you *think* and *why* ("I liked it because the fast pulse felt exciting"). Liking or disliking is fine — explaining is the skill.

**Worked example.** Appraise a clip in three steps: 1) **Notice** — "steady fast pulse, loud, lots of brass (timbre), thick texture," 2) **Feel** — "it sounded triumphant/exciting," 3) **Judge** — "I liked it because the brass and fast pulse felt powerful." Notice → feel → judge with reasons.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Listen to music from a different country each week and describe it.
- Compare two styles (a lullaby vs a marching band) using the dimensions.
- "Soundtrack detective" — what mood does this film music create, and how?

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** "it's good/bad" with no reason. **Why:** no musical vocabulary used. **Fix:** insist on a *because* with a dimension word.
- **Error:** thinking only one style is "real music." **Why:** narrow exposure. **Fix:** value many styles — folk, pop, classical and world music are all music.
- **Error:** confusing "I don't like it" with "it's bad." **Fix:** taste is personal; describe what's actually happening regardless of preference.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does appraising music mean? **A:** listening carefully and describing/judging it using musical words.
- **Q:** Give an opinion about a clip *with a reason*. **A:** e.g. "I liked it because the slow tempo felt calm."
- **Q:** Name two different styles of music. **A:** any two: classical, pop, folk, jazz, world, etc.

### Activity / practice
**Listen:** appraise a short clip with *notice → feel → judge*, using at least three dimension words and one *because* reason. (`speak`: give the spoken appraisal.)

### Resources
- **BBC Ten Pieces — clips across orchestral & world styles, with listening prompts**.
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Music — *Listening and appraising***.

## 11. Basic music history (genres & eras, child level)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Knowledge – music history

**Prerequisites:** Listening & appraising, Timbre & instrument families

### Key concepts
- Music has changed over time — different **eras** and **genres** sound different.
- A few child-friendly landmarks: old/classical (orchestra), then jazz, then pop/rock, then electronic/today.
- You can place a piece roughly by its instruments and style (timbre, texture).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can appraise music and identify some instruments.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Music has a **history** — it sounds different in different times and places. A simple, child-level map:
- **"Classical" / orchestral** (old, e.g. Mozart, Beethoven) — orchestras of strings, woodwind, brass, percussion; no electric instruments.
- **Jazz** (about 100 years ago) — swinging rhythms, trumpets, saxophones, double bass, lots of improvising.
- **Pop & rock** (last ~70 years) — singers with electric guitars, bass, drum kit; catchy verse–chorus songs.
- **Electronic / today** — computers, synths and beats; the music many of us hear now.
You can often *guess the era* from the instruments (timbre): orchestra → old; electric guitar → rock; computer beats → modern. A **genre** is a style/family of music (jazz, pop, folk, classical).

**Worked example.** Place a clip in time: hear lots of synths and a drum machine → "this is electronic/modern, because those instruments are new." Hear only an orchestra → "older/classical, no electric instruments." The timbres are your clues.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Listen to a song from each era (a symphony, some jazz, a pop hit, a dance track) and put them in order.
- Ask grown-ups what music they grew up with.
- Spot genres in films, adverts and games.

### Misconceptions (common errors → why → fix)
- **Error:** thinking "classical" just means "old/boring." **Why:** label confusion. **Fix:** classical is one *genre*; it can be dramatic and exciting — and lots of old music isn't "classical."
- **Error:** assuming all old music sounds the same. **Fix:** styles vary hugely across times and cultures.
- **Error:** guessing the era from the *song's* age rather than its sound. **Fix:** use the **instruments/timbre** as clues (orchestra vs electric vs computer).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a genre of music? **A:** a style/family of music (e.g. jazz, pop, classical, folk).
- **Q:** If a clip has only an orchestra and no electric instruments, is it likely old or modern? **A:** old/classical.
- **Q:** Name two different eras or genres of music. **A:** any two: classical, jazz, pop/rock, electronic.

### Activity / practice
**Listen:** hear short clips from two different eras/genres, name each genre, and explain which is older using the instruments (timbre) as evidence. (`ordering`: put era clips oldest → newest; or `matchPairs`: genre → typical instrument.)
