# Year 1 Science (ages 5–6, KS1)

Real NC units: **Plants · Animals, including humans · Everyday materials · Seasonal changes.** Year 1 is about *careful looking and naming* the everyday world. Working Scientifically focus: observe closely, identify and classify, ask simple questions.

## 1. Naming plants and their parts

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – plants

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- A plant is a living thing that usually grows in soil and needs light and water.
- Most plants have the same main parts: **roots, stem (or trunk), leaves, flower.**
- We can name common wild and garden plants (daisy, dandelion, rose, grass) and common trees.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows what "alive" roughly means and has seen plants and trees outdoors.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Plants are living things that grow almost everywhere — in gardens, parks, cracks in the pavement, and as great big trees. Most plants share the same body parts, so once you know them you can name them on any plant. The **roots** grow down into the soil and hold the plant still and drink up water. The **stem** holds the plant up and carries water to the rest (on a tree the woody stem is called a **trunk**). The **leaves** are flat and catch sunlight. The **flower** is often colourful and makes seeds for new plants. Point at a real daisy: roots underground, thin green stem, leaves near the bottom, white-and-yellow flower on top.

**Worked example.** Look at a dandelion. *Roots?* — underground, long and white. *Stem?* — the hollow green tube. *Leaves?* — the jagged green ones at the base. *Flower?* — the bright yellow top (which later turns into the white "clock" of seeds). So a dandelion has all four parts, just like a rose or a daisy.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A favourite food that's a plant part: carrots are *roots*, lettuce is *leaves*, broccoli is a *flower*.
- Trees in a football park or by a sports pitch — find the trunk, branches and leaves.
- A flower a child has picked or seen — name its parts together.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Trees aren't plants — they're too big."
  **Why:** child links "plant" only to small flowers in pots.
  **Fix:** a tree has roots, a stem (the trunk), leaves and flowers too — it's just a very big plant.
- **Wrong idea:** "The roots are the dirty bottom we don't need."
  **Why:** roots are hidden and look unimportant.
  **Fix:** show that roots hold the plant up and drink water — pull a weed and look at them.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name the part of a plant that grows underground and drinks water. **A:** The roots.
- **Q:** Which part of the plant catches the sunlight? **A:** The leaves.
- **Q:** Is an oak tree a plant? How do you know? **A:** Yes — it has roots, a stem (trunk), leaves and grows like a plant.

### Activity / practice
Go outside (or use a houseplant) and label a real plant. Child points to and names roots, stem, leaves, flower. As a Classai block: a **matchPairs** of plant-part words to pictures, or a **categorize** of foods into root / leaf / flower.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *What are the parts of a plant?*** (names roots, stem, leaves, flower).
- **Oak National Academy — Year 1 Plants** (lesson sequence on parts of a plant).

## 2. Trees: deciduous and evergreen

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Biology – plants

**Prerequisites:** Naming plants and their parts.

### Key concepts
- **Deciduous** trees drop their leaves in autumn and grow new ones in spring (e.g. oak, horse chestnut).
- **Evergreen** trees keep green leaves all year (e.g. pine, holly).
- We can sort trees into these two groups by what their leaves do.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows that trees have leaves and that there are seasons.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Not all trees behave the same in winter. **Deciduous** trees lose all their leaves when the weather turns cold — in autumn the leaves go yellow, orange and brown and fall off, leaving bare branches, then fresh green leaves grow back in spring. **Evergreen** trees stay green all year; their leaves are often tough or needle-shaped (like a Christmas pine tree or a spiky holly). A good clue word: *ever-green* means "green forever."

**Worked example.** It's January. You see one tree with bare branches and no leaves, and one tree right next to it still covered in green needles. The bare one is **deciduous** (it dropped its leaves for winter). The green one is **evergreen** (it keeps leaves all year).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A Christmas tree (pine) is evergreen — that's why it's green in midwinter.
- Crunchy autumn leaves to kick through come from deciduous trees.
- Holly with red berries at Christmas — evergreen and prickly.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "A tree with no leaves in winter is dead."
  **Why:** bare branches look lifeless.
  **Fix:** it's resting (deciduous) — buds and new leaves come back in spring; the tree is alive.
- **Wrong idea:** "Evergreens never lose any leaves."
  **Why:** they always look green.
  **Fix:** they do drop old leaves/needles, but a few at a time all year, so they always look green.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does a deciduous tree do in autumn? **A:** Drops (loses) its leaves.
- **Q:** Give an example of an evergreen tree. **A:** Pine, fir, holly (any keep-green-all-year tree).
- **Q:** In winter a tree has bare branches. Deciduous or evergreen? **A:** Deciduous.

### Activity / practice
On a walk, find one deciduous and one evergreen tree and say how you know. Classai block: **categorize** tree pictures into "loses leaves / keeps leaves."

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *Deciduous and evergreen trees*** (compares the two with photos).
- **Mystery Science — *Why do leaves change colour in fall?*** (mini-lesson on deciduous leaf change).

## 3. Animals: grouping and what they eat

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – animals including humans

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- Animals can be grouped: **fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals** — and **insects/minibeasts.**
- Animals can be grouped by what they eat: **carnivore** (eats meat), **herbivore** (eats plants), **omnivore** (eats both).
- Each group has clues you can spot (feathers = bird; fur and milk = mammal; scales and lives in water = fish).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can name some common animals (dog, fish, bird, cow, lion).

### Teach it (the explanation)
There are millions of animals, so scientists sort them into groups. **Mammals** have fur or hair and feed their babies milk (dogs, cats, cows, humans). **Birds** have feathers, wings and a beak. **Fish** have scales, live in water and breathe with gills. **Reptiles** have dry scaly skin (snakes, lizards). **Amphibians** live partly in water and on land (frogs, newts). We can also group animals by *what they eat*: a **carnivore** eats other animals (a lion, a shark); a **herbivore** eats only plants (a cow, a rabbit); an **omnivore** eats both plants and animals (a human, a pig, a bear).

**Worked example.** A rabbit: it has fur and feeds its babies milk → **mammal**. It eats grass, carrots and leaves, never meat → **herbivore**. So a rabbit is a herbivore mammal.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A pet at home — work out its group and whether it's a carnivore, herbivore or omnivore.
- A favourite zoo or wildlife animal (lion = carnivore, giraffe = herbivore).
- Dinosaurs: a T. rex was a carnivore; a Diplodocus was a herbivore.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "A whale is a fish because it lives in the sea."
  **Why:** child groups by *where it lives*, not body features.
  **Fix:** a whale breathes air, has no scales, and feeds its babies milk — it's a **mammal** that lives in water.
- **Wrong idea:** "All big animals are carnivores."
  **Why:** big = scary = meat-eater in a child's mind.
  **Fix:** elephants and cows are huge but eat only plants — they're herbivores.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What do we call an animal that eats only plants? **A:** A herbivore.
- **Q:** A robin has feathers, wings and a beak. What group is it? **A:** A bird.
- **Q:** Is a human a carnivore, herbivore or omnivore? Why? **A:** Omnivore — we eat both plants and meat.

### Activity / practice
Sort a pile of animal pictures (or toys) into mammals/birds/fish, then into carnivore/herbivore/omnivore. Classai block: **categorize** animals by diet, or **matchPairs** animal → group.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *What are the different animal groups?*** (mammals, birds, fish, reptiles, amphibians).
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *Carnivores, herbivores and omnivores*** (grouping by diet).

## 4. The human body and the five senses

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – animals including humans

**Prerequisites:** Animals: grouping and what they eat.

### Key concepts
- We can name parts of the human body: head, neck, arms, legs, hands, feet, and so on.
- We have **five senses**: sight (eyes), hearing (ears), smell (nose), taste (tongue), touch (skin).
- Each sense uses a particular body part to find out about the world.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can point to basic body parts on themselves.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Your body has many parts, each with a job. We also have **five senses** — special ways of finding out about the world around us. You **see** with your eyes, **hear** with your ears, **smell** with your nose, **taste** with your tongue, and **touch/feel** with your skin (especially your fingertips). Senses keep us safe and tell us about things — your nose smelling smoke, your skin feeling something hot, your ears hearing a car.

**Worked example.** You bite an apple. Which senses are working? You **see** it's red (eyes), you **smell** it's fresh (nose), you **feel** it's smooth and cold (skin), you **taste** it's sweet (tongue), and you **hear** the crunch (ears). All five senses at once!

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Eating a favourite snack — name which senses you're using.
- A sport: which senses does a goalkeeper use to catch a ball? (sight, touch, hearing).
- Animals with amazing senses: a dog's nose, an owl's eyes.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "We taste with our nose" (or smell with the tongue).
  **Why:** smell and taste feel mixed up because they work together.
  **Fix:** taste is the tongue, smell is the nose — try tasting with your nose held: flavour fades, proving they're different.
- **Wrong idea:** "Touch only happens in the hands."
  **Why:** we touch most with our hands.
  **Fix:** all your skin can feel — touch tests a knee or cheek too.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which body part do you use to hear? **A:** Your ears.
- **Q:** Name the five senses. **A:** Sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch.
- **Q:** You're in a dark room and smell toast. Which sense told you? **A:** Smell (your nose).

### Activity / practice
A **senses hunt**: with a parent, find one thing to see, hear, smell, touch and (safely) taste, and name the sense each time. Classai block: **matchPairs** sense → body part, or **multipleChoice** "which sense?" scenarios.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *The five senses*** (each sense and its body part).
- **Crash Course Kids (YouTube channel) — *Senses* episode** (five senses for young learners).

## 5. Everyday materials and their properties

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Chemistry – materials

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- A **material** is what an object is *made of* (wood, plastic, metal, glass, fabric, paper, rock, water).
- We can describe materials by their **properties**: hard/soft, bendy/stiff, rough/smooth, shiny/dull, waterproof or not, see-through or not.
- The same object can be made of different materials, and one material can make many objects.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can name everyday objects (spoon, cup, chair, window).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Everything around you is made of a **material**. A window is made of *glass*, a spoon of *metal*, a jumper of *wool* or *fabric*, a chair of *wood* or *plastic*. We describe materials by their **properties** — words that tell us what they're like. Is it **hard or soft**? **Bendy or stiff**? **Rough or smooth**? **Shiny or dull**? **See-through** (transparent) or not? **Waterproof** (water can't soak through) or not? Knowing the difference between an *object* (the thing, e.g. a cup) and the *material* (what it's made of, e.g. plastic) is the big idea here.

**Worked example.** A glass window: the **object** is a window; the **material** is glass. Its properties: hard, smooth, shiny, see-through (transparent), waterproof, and breakable. That's why glass is good for windows — you can see through it and rain can't get in.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A child's shoe: rubber sole (bendy, waterproof), fabric top, metal eyelets.
- Toys at home — what material is each made of, and why?
- A water bottle: plastic because it's light, bendy and waterproof.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "A cup is a material."
  **Why:** child confuses the *object* with what it's *made of*.
  **Fix:** a cup is the object; the material is the plastic/china/glass it's made from.
- **Wrong idea:** "Metal and wood are the same — both hard."
  **Why:** properties get lumped together.
  **Fix:** test them — metal is shiny and cold; wood is dull and warmer; both hard but different.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What material is a window usually made of? **A:** Glass.
- **Q:** Give two words to describe metal. **A:** e.g. hard and shiny (also: cold, strong).
- **Q:** Is a spoon a material? **A:** No — a spoon is an object; metal/plastic is the material.

### Activity / practice
**Material hunt:** collect five objects around the house and say the material and one property of each. Classai block: **categorize** objects by material, or **matchPairs** object → material.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *Everyday materials and their properties*** (names materials and describes properties).
- **Oak National Academy — Year 1 Everyday Materials** (lesson sequence).

## 6. Seasonal changes and the weather

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Earth science / seasons

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- There are **four seasons**: spring, summer, autumn, winter — they happen in the same order every year.
- Each season has its own weather and changes (warm and long days in summer; cold, dark, short days in winter).
- We can observe and describe the weather and how it changes through the year.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has experienced different weather and knows day and night.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The year goes through **four seasons** in the same order: **spring** (new leaves, baby animals, getting warmer), **summer** (warm/hot, long sunny days, leaves full), **autumn** (cooler, leaves turn brown and fall, getting darker), **winter** (cold, short days, sometimes frost or snow). The biggest changes are the **temperature** (warm in summer, cold in winter) and how much **daylight** there is — summer days are long, winter days are short and dark. We watch the weather change: sunny, cloudy, rainy, windy, snowy.

**Worked example.** It's December. The days are very short, it's dark by teatime, it's cold and there might be frost. Which season? **Winter** — cold, short days. Now imagine July: long sunny evenings, warm, leaves on every tree → **summer**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A child's birthday — which season is it in, and what's the weather usually like?
- Clothes: why a coat in winter and a sun hat in summer?
- Seasonal foods or sports (sledging in winter, swimming in summer).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "It's summer because we're closer to the Sun."
  **Why:** sensible guess — closer should mean warmer.
  **Fix (Y1 level):** seasons come from the tilt of the Earth and longer daylight, not from getting nearer the Sun. (Full explanation comes in Year 5.)
- **Wrong idea:** "Winter means it always snows."
  **Why:** snow is the storybook image of winter.
  **Fix:** winter is cold with short days; in many places it rains far more than it snows.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name the four seasons in order, starting with spring. **A:** Spring, summer, autumn, winter.
- **Q:** In which season are the days shortest and coldest? **A:** Winter.
- **Q:** Name two kinds of weather. **A:** Any two: sunny, rainy, cloudy, windy, snowy, foggy.

### Activity / practice
Keep a **weather diary** for a week: each day draw or pick a weather symbol and note if it's warm or cold. Classai block: **ordering** the four seasons, or **multipleChoice** matching a description to a season.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *The four seasons*** (changes and weather in each season).
- **BBC Teach — *Seasons and weather* (KS1)** (short classroom clips on seasonal change).
