# Year 2 Science (ages 6–7, KS1)

Real NC units: **Living things and their habitats · Plants · Animals, including humans · Uses of everyday materials.** Year 2 moves from naming to *how living things grow and live where they do, and why we choose particular materials.* Working Scientifically focus: simple comparative tests, gathering and recording data, observing growth over time.

## 1. Living, dead, and never-alive

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – living things & habitats

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 — Animals: grouping; Naming plants and their parts.

### Key concepts
- Things are **living**, **dead** (once alive), or **never alive**.
- Living things do the seven life processes (a child-sized list: they **move, grow, feed, and have babies**; also breathe, sense and get rid of waste).
- A dead leaf was once alive; a rock or a plastic toy was never alive.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can tell plants and animals from non-living objects in an everyday way.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Everything can be sorted into three groups: **living**, **dead**, or **never alive**. **Living** things grow, need food and water, move (even plants grow and turn toward light), and can make young. **Dead** things were once alive but aren't any more — a fallen leaf, a log, a feather. **Never alive** things were never living at all — a rock, a metal spoon, a plastic toy, water. A handy test: ask "Does it (or did it ever) grow, feed and make babies?" If yes → living or dead; if it never could → never alive.

**Worked example.** Sort three things. A **butterfly fluttering** → it moves, feeds and grows → **living**. A **dead leaf on the path** → it was part of a living tree but isn't growing now → **dead**. A **pebble** → never grew, ate or had young → **never alive**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A wooden chair vs a tree — both wood, but is each living, dead or never alive?
- A pet (living) vs a teddy bear (never alive) — what's the difference?
- A seashell on the beach — was once part of a living animal → dead/once-living.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "A car is alive because it moves."
  **Why:** child uses *movement* as the test for life.
  **Fix:** moving isn't enough — living things also grow, feed and make babies; a car does none of these.
- **Wrong idea:** "Wood is alive because it came from a tree."
  **Why:** confusing material origin with current life.
  **Fix:** a wooden table is *dead* (once part of a living tree); it isn't growing now.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Is a rock living, dead, or never alive? **A:** Never alive.
- **Q:** Name two things all living things do. **A:** Any two: move, grow, feed, make babies (also breathe, sense).
- **Q:** A leaf on the ground that fell from a tree — living, dead or never alive? **A:** Dead (it was once alive).

### Activity / practice
Sort objects from around the house and garden into living / dead / never alive and give a reason. Classai block: **categorize** into the three groups.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *Living, dead and never been alive*** (the three groups with examples).
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *What do living things need?*** (life processes for KS1).

## 2. Habitats and microhabitats

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – living things & habitats

**Prerequisites:** Living, dead, and never-alive.

### Key concepts
- A **habitat** is the place where an animal or plant lives and gets what it needs (food, water, shelter).
- Different habitats suit different living things (a fish needs water; a camel suits a desert).
- A **microhabitat** is a tiny habitat within a larger one (under a log, in a crack, under a stone).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows that living things need food and water.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **habitat** is the home of a living thing — the place that gives it everything it needs to survive: food, water, shelter and a place to raise young. A polar bear's habitat is the cold Arctic (thick fur keeps it warm); a fish's habitat is water (it breathes with gills); a cactus's habitat is the hot dry desert (it stores water). Living things are **suited** to their habitat. Inside a big habitat there are tiny ones called **microhabitats** — under a log, under a stone, in a hedge — where small creatures like woodlice, worms and beetles live because it's damp and dark and safe.

**Worked example.** Lift a log in the garden (with a parent). Underneath it's dark and damp. You find woodlice and worms. That damp, dark space is a **microhabitat**. The woodlice live there because they like damp, dark places and find food in the rotting wood — they wouldn't survive on a hot, dry, sunny path.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A pet's habitat — what does a goldfish tank or a hamster cage provide?
- A favourite wild animal — describe its habitat and how it's suited to it.
- The garden as many habitats: pond, soil, hedge, under stones.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Any animal could live anywhere if it tried."
  **Why:** child doesn't yet see the link between animal and habitat.
  **Fix:** a polar bear would overheat in a desert; a cactus would rot in a pond — each is suited to its home.
- **Wrong idea:** "A microhabitat isn't a real habitat because it's so small."
  **Why:** "habitat" sounds big.
  **Fix:** size doesn't matter — under a stone is a full home for a woodlouse, with food, water and shelter.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a habitat? **A:** The place where a living thing lives and gets what it needs.
- **Q:** Name a microhabitat where you might find woodlice. **A:** Under a log or stone (any damp, dark spot).
- **Q:** Why does a polar bear live in the Arctic and not the desert? **A:** Its thick fur suits the cold; it would overheat in the desert.

### Activity / practice
**Microhabitat hunt:** with a parent, lift a log or stone, see what's underneath, then put it back gently. Record what you find. Classai block: **matchPairs** animal → habitat.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *Habitats*** (what a habitat is; matching animals to habitats).
- **Oak National Academy — Year 2 Living things and their habitats** (lesson sequence incl. microhabitats).

## 3. Food chains

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Biology – living things & habitats

**Prerequisites:** Habitats and microhabitats; Year 1 — carnivore/herbivore/omnivore.

### Key concepts
- A **food chain** shows what eats what, with arrows that mean "is eaten by" / "energy goes to."
- Most food chains start with a **plant** (the plant makes its own food using sunlight).
- The arrow always points *from* the food *to* the eater.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows herbivore/carnivore and that animals eat plants or other animals.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **food chain** is a simple picture of who eats whom. It almost always starts with a **plant**, because plants can make their own food from sunlight. An animal eats the plant, and another animal might eat *that* animal. We join them with **arrows**, and the arrow means "is eaten by" — it points to whoever does the eating, showing which way the food (energy) travels.

**Worked example.** Grass → rabbit → fox. Read it as: *grass is eaten by the rabbit, and the rabbit is eaten by the fox.* The arrows point grass-to-rabbit and rabbit-to-fox, because that's the direction the food goes. The plant (grass) is first because it makes its own food and feeds the whole chain.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A garden chain: lettuce → caterpillar → bird.
- A pond chain: pondweed → tadpole → fish → heron.
- The child's own breakfast — trace it back: cereal comes from a plant (a grass!).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The arrow points to what the animal eats."
  **Why:** children read arrows as "eats."
  **Fix:** the arrow means "is eaten by" — it points *to the eater*, the way the food goes.
- **Wrong idea:** "Food chains can start with any animal."
  **Why:** child picks a favourite animal to go first.
  **Fix:** chains start with a plant, because only plants make their own food from sunlight.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What usually comes first in a food chain? **A:** A plant.
- **Q:** In "grass → rabbit → fox," what eats the rabbit? **A:** The fox.
- **Q:** What does the arrow in a food chain mean? **A:** "Is eaten by" (food/energy goes that way).

### Activity / practice
Build a food chain with cards or drawings, putting the plant first and arrows the right way. Classai block: **ordering** the parts of a food chain correctly.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *What is a food chain?*** (plants first; arrows mean "eaten by").
- **Generation Genius — *Food Chains and Food Webs* (K–2)** (animated explainer).

## 4. How seeds and bulbs grow into plants

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – plants

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 — Naming plants and their parts.

### Key concepts
- Plants grow from **seeds** and **bulbs**.
- To grow well, plants need **water, light, and a suitable temperature** (warmth).
- A growing seed first sends out a root (down) and then a shoot (up).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the parts of a plant and that plants are living.

### Teach it (the explanation)
New plants come from **seeds** (like a sunflower seed or a bean) and from **bulbs** (like a daffodil or onion). A seed has a tiny baby plant inside and a store of food. When a seed gets **water, warmth and (soon) light**, it **germinates**: first a **root** grows downward to drink water and hold on, then a **shoot** grows upward toward the light and opens its first leaves. For a plant to keep growing healthily it needs water, light and a suitable temperature — too cold and it won't grow; no water and it dries up; no light and it goes pale and weak.

**Worked example.** Plant a bean in a clear jar with damp paper. After a few days the bean cracks open and a white **root** pushes *down*. A day or two later a green **shoot** pushes *up* and unfolds leaves. Give it water and a warm, light windowsill and it keeps growing. Take away the water and it would wilt and die.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Grow cress on a windowsill and eat it in a sandwich.
- An onion or garlic bulb sprouting in the kitchen.
- A seed a child planted before — what did it need?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Seeds need soil to grow." (only)
  **Why:** we usually plant in soil.
  **Fix:** seeds mainly need water, warmth and light — many sprout on wet paper with no soil at all (soil adds support and minerals later).
- **Wrong idea:** "The more water, the better."
  **Why:** plants need water, so more must help.
  **Fix:** too much water drowns the roots and rots the seed; it needs *enough*, not flooding.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name three things a seed needs to grow. **A:** Water, warmth (suitable temperature), light.
- **Q:** Which grows first from a seed — the root or the shoot? **A:** The root (it grows down first).
- **Q:** Name a plant that grows from a bulb. **A:** Daffodil, tulip, onion (any bulb plant).

### Activity / practice
**Grow a bean in a jar** (clear jar, damp kitchen paper, bean against the side). Watch the root and shoot over a week and draw it each day — an *observing over time* enquiry. Classai block: **ordering** the stages of germination.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *What do plants need to grow?*** (water, light, warmth).
- **Mystery Science — *How do plants grow from seeds?*** (germination mini-lesson).

## 5. Animals and their offspring; staying healthy

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – animals including humans

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 — Animal groups; The human body and senses.

### Key concepts
- Animals (including humans) have **offspring (young)** which grow up to look like their parents.
- All animals have basic needs: **food, water, air, and shelter.**
- Humans stay healthy with the right **food, exercise, and hygiene** (washing, brushing teeth).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows animals have babies and that we eat and drink to live.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Animals have **young**, and the young grow up into adults that look like their parents — a kitten becomes a cat, a chick becomes a hen, a baby becomes an adult human. Some young look very like their parents from the start (a foal looks like a small horse); others change a lot as they grow. Every animal needs the same basic things to stay alive: **food, water, air to breathe, and shelter.** Humans also need to keep healthy: eat a **balanced variety of food** (not just treats), get plenty of **exercise**, and keep clean (**hygiene**) — washing hands, bathing, and brushing teeth to stop germs and tooth decay.

**Worked example.** Think of a dog through its life: a tiny **puppy** → a half-grown **young dog** → a full-grown **adult dog**. To stay healthy it needs the right food, fresh water, air, a warm place to sleep (shelter), exercise (walks) and to be kept clean. Humans need exactly the same kinds of things.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Match baby animals to their parents (lamb–sheep, calf–cow, joey–kangaroo).
- The child's own routine — handwashing, toothbrushing, sports and meals.
- A pet's needs at home.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Baby animals are a different kind of animal from the adult."
  **Why:** a caterpillar and a butterfly look nothing alike (this is for Y5, but Y2 children meet it).
  **Fix:** the young grow into the adult — a puppy *is* a baby dog; it just changes as it grows.
- **Wrong idea:** "Healthy food means no fat or sugar at all."
  **Why:** "healthy" gets oversimplified.
  **Fix:** health comes from *variety and balance* — a bit of everything, with lots of fruit, veg and water.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name three things every animal needs to stay alive. **A:** Any three: food, water, air, shelter.
- **Q:** What is a baby sheep called, and what does it grow into? **A:** A lamb; it grows into a sheep.
- **Q:** Name two things humans do to stay healthy. **A:** Any two: eat a balanced diet, exercise, wash, brush teeth.

### Activity / practice
Make a **"stay healthy" plan**: list one good food, one exercise and one hygiene habit for the day. Classai block: **matchPairs** baby → adult animal, or **categorize** activities as healthy / not healthy.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *How do animals grow and change?*** (offspring and life stages).
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *How to keep healthy*** (food, exercise, hygiene).

## 6. Choosing the right material for the job

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Chemistry – uses of materials

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 — Everyday materials and their properties.

### Key concepts
- A material is chosen for an object because its **properties suit the job**.
- Some materials can have their shape **changed** by squashing, bending, twisting or stretching.
- We can test materials fairly to decide which is best for a purpose.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can name materials (wood, metal, plastic, glass, rubber, fabric) and describe properties.

### Teach it (the explanation)
We don't pick materials randomly — we choose each one because its **properties** fit the job. A window is **glass** because glass is transparent (you can see through it). A raincoat is **plastic** because plastic is waterproof. A spoon is **metal** because metal is hard and easy to clean. A tyre is **rubber** because rubber is bendy and grippy. Some materials can have their **shape changed** by **squashing, bending, twisting or stretching** — like modelling clay, a rubber band, or a pipe cleaner. To decide which material is best, we can do a simple **fair test**: try each one the same way and compare.

**Worked example.** Which material makes the best umbrella? Test paper, cotton fabric and plastic by dripping water on each (same amount, same height — a fair test). Paper goes soggy, cotton soaks through, plastic stays dry. **Plastic** is best because its property — waterproof — suits the job of keeping rain off.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Why are saucepans metal but oven gloves fabric?
- Design a boat: which material floats and stays waterproof?
- A child's own coat, shoes and lunchbox — why each material?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "You could make a window out of anything."
  **Why:** child doesn't yet link property to purpose.
  **Fix:** a wooden window would block your view — glass is chosen *because* it's transparent.
- **Wrong idea:** "Bending a paperclip makes it a new material."
  **Why:** changing shape feels like changing the thing.
  **Fix:** it's still metal — only the *shape* changed, not the material.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Why is a raincoat made of plastic and not paper? **A:** Plastic is waterproof; paper soaks up water.
- **Q:** Name a material whose shape you can change by squashing. **A:** Clay / playdough / sponge (any squashable material).
- **Q:** Why is a saucepan made of metal? **A:** Metal is hard, strong and good with heat.

### Activity / practice
**Best-material test:** choose a job (e.g. wrap a cold drink to keep it cold, or mop up a spill) and fairly test two or three materials, then say which won and why. Classai block: **matchPairs** object → best material, with a "why."

- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Science — *Why we use different materials*** (choosing materials for a purpose).
- **Oak National Academy — Year 2 Uses of everyday materials** (lesson sequence incl. changing shape).
