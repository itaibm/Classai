## 1. Classifying living things into broad groups

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Biology – living things & habitats

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 — classification keys; Year 1–2 animal groups.

### Key concepts
- Living things are classified into broad groups: **animals, plants, and micro-organisms** (and fungi).
- **Animals** split into **vertebrates** (with a backbone: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals) and **invertebrates** (no backbone: insects, spiders, worms, snails).
- We classify by **shared characteristics**, which also shows how living things are related.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can use a classification key and name animal groups.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Scientists classify all living things into **broad groups** based on **shared characteristics**. The biggest split most children meet: **plants** (make their own food), **animals** (eat food, move), and **micro-organisms** (tiny living things you need a microscope to see — bacteria, some are useful like yeast, some cause disease), plus **fungi** (like mushrooms and mould). **Animals** are then divided into **vertebrates** — animals with a **backbone** — which fall into five classes: **fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals**; and **invertebrates** — animals with **no backbone** — such as insects, spiders, worms, snails and jellyfish. Classifying this way isn't just tidy: shared features show how living things are **related**.

**Worked example.** Classify three creatures. A **frog**: it's an animal, has a backbone (**vertebrate**), lives partly in water and on land → **amphibian**. A **spider**: animal, **no backbone** (**invertebrate**), eight legs → an arachnid, not an insect. **Yeast** (in bread): a tiny living **micro-organism (a fungus)** you can't see without a microscope. Three living things, three very different broad groups, each sorted by its characteristics.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Backbone check: feel your own spine — you're a vertebrate; a worm or snail isn't.
- Useful microbes: yeast makes bread rise; bacteria turn milk into yoghurt.
- Sorting minibeasts from the garden into vertebrate/invertebrate.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "All small crawling animals (spiders, woodlice) are insects."
  **Why:** "bug" = insect in everyday talk.
  **Fix:** insects have 6 legs and 3 body parts; spiders have 8 legs — they're **invertebrates** but not insects.
- **Wrong idea:** "All micro-organisms are germs that make you ill."
  **Why:** we hear about harmful germs.
  **Fix:** many microbes are **helpful** (yeast for bread, bacteria for yoghurt); only some cause disease.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is the difference between a vertebrate and an invertebrate? **A:** A vertebrate has a backbone; an invertebrate doesn't.
- **Q:** Name the five vertebrate groups. **A:** Fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals.
- **Q:** Give an example of a useful micro-organism. **A:** Yeast (bread) or bacteria (yoghurt).

### Activity / practice
**Big sort:** classify a set of living things into plant / animal / micro-organism, then animals into vertebrate/invertebrate. Classai block: **categorize** living things into broad groups; **multipleChoice** vertebrate vs invertebrate.

## 2. The circulatory system

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Biology – animals including humans

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 — nutrition; Year 4 — digestion.

### Key concepts
- The **heart** pumps **blood** around the body through **blood vessels** (arteries, veins, capillaries).
- Blood **transports** oxygen (from the lungs), nutrients (from digestion) and water to every part, and carries away waste.
- **Diet, exercise, and avoiding harmful drugs** affect how well the body works.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows we breathe in oxygen and eat for nutrients; knows the heart beats.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The **circulatory system** delivers everything the body needs. The **heart** is a muscular pump that pushes **blood** all around the body through tubes called **blood vessels**: **arteries** carry blood **away** from the heart, **veins** carry it **back**, and tiny **capillaries** reach every cell. Blood is a delivery service: it picks up **oxygen** at the **lungs** and **nutrients** from digested food in the gut, carries them (and water) to every part of the body, and takes away **waste** like carbon dioxide (dropped off at the lungs to breathe out). How well this system works depends on our **lifestyle**: a balanced **diet**, regular **exercise** (which strengthens the heart), and **avoiding harmful drugs/too much alcohol and not smoking** all keep the heart and blood healthy.

**Worked example.** Follow a breath of oxygen. You breathe in → oxygen enters the **lungs** → blood there picks it up → the **heart** pumps that oxygen-rich blood out through **arteries** → tiny **capillaries** deliver it to your leg muscles so they can work → the now oxygen-poor blood returns through **veins** to the heart and lungs to collect more. Meanwhile food you digested adds **nutrients** to the same blood. Now run on the spot: your heart **beats faster** to deliver oxygen quicker — that's why exercise makes your pulse race.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Feel your pulse at rest, then after star jumps — the heart pumping faster.
- Why athletes train: a stronger heart pumps more with each beat.
- The journey of the food you ate and the air you breathed meeting in your blood.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Blood just sits in the body / fills it up like a tank."
  **Why:** we don't feel it moving.
  **Fix:** blood is constantly **pumped in a loop** by the heart through vessels — it's a delivery service, not a still pool.
- **Wrong idea:** "The heart and lungs do the same job."
  **Why:** they're side by side and both about staying alive.
  **Fix:** the **lungs** get oxygen from the air; the **heart** pumps the blood that carries it round.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is the job of the heart? **A:** To pump blood around the body.
- **Q:** Name two things blood transports. **A:** Any two: oxygen, nutrients, water, waste (carbon dioxide).
- **Q:** Why does your heart beat faster when you exercise? **A:** To deliver oxygen to the muscles more quickly.

### Activity / practice
**Pulse investigation:** measure your pulse for 15s at rest, do 1 minute of star jumps, measure again, and explain the rise — a fair, measured test. Classai block: **numberEntry** for pulse counts; **matchPairs** vessel → its job.

## 3. Evolution and inheritance

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Biology – evolution & inheritance

**Prerequisites:** Year 5 — life cycles & reproduction; Year 3 — fossils.

### Key concepts
- Living things produce **offspring that vary** — not exact copies of their parents (**inheritance** with variation).
- **Adaptation**: features that suit a living thing to its environment can help it survive and reproduce.
- Over many generations, helpful features become more common — this is **evolution**, and **fossils** are evidence of it.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows about reproduction and that fossils are remains of ancient living things.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Offspring **inherit** features from their parents, but they're **not identical copies** — there's **variation** (you might have your dad's eye colour but your own height). Living things are **adapted** to where they live: a polar bear's thick fur and white coat suit the snowy Arctic; a cactus's spines and water-storing stem suit the desert. Here's the powerful idea: because individuals vary, some happen to have features that help them **survive and have more young** in their environment. Those helpful features get **passed on** to more offspring, so over **many, many generations** they become common — the species gradually **changes**. That slow change over time is **evolution**. We know it happened because of **fossils**: by comparing fossils of different ages, we can see how living things changed over millions of years.

**Worked example.** Imagine beetles on dark tree bark, some pale and some dark (variation). Hungry birds spot the **pale** beetles easily and eat them, but the **dark** beetles are camouflaged and survive to have young — who inherit the dark colour. Over many generations there are more and more dark beetles. The beetle population has **evolved** to be darker, because the dark colour was an **adaptation** that helped survival in that environment. Fossils of beetles from long ago would show this change.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Family resemblance and variation — looking a bit like parents but not identical.
- Dog breeds: how humans bred dogs for different features (selective breeding).
- Camouflaged animals (stick insects, snowy owls) hiding from predators.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "An animal changes its own body to suit the environment in its lifetime (a giraffe stretched its neck)."
  **Why:** adaptation sounds like an individual effort.
  **Fix:** individuals don't redesign themselves — those **born** with helpful variations survive and **pass them on**; change happens across **generations**.
- **Wrong idea:** "Offspring are exact copies of their parents."
  **Why:** children look like their parents.
  **Fix:** there's always **variation** — that's the raw material evolution needs.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does "adaptation" mean? **A:** A feature that helps a living thing survive in its environment.
- **Q:** Why do offspring vary from their parents? **A:** They inherit a mix of features, so they're similar but not identical.
- **Q:** What evidence tells us living things have changed over time? **A:** Fossils (comparing them across ages).

### Activity / practice
**Camouflage hunt:** scatter coloured paper "beetles" on a patterned background and time how fast you find each colour — model how camouflage aids survival. Classai block: **multipleChoice** adaptation scenarios; **matchPairs** animal → its adaptation.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Evolution, inheritance and adaptation*** (variation, adaptation, evolution, fossils).
- **Crash Course Kids (YouTube channel) — *Adaptations / Inherited Traits* episodes** (KS2 level).

## 4. How we see: light travels in straight lines

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Physics – light

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 — light, shadows & reflection.

### Key concepts
- Light travels in **straight lines** from a light source.
- We see objects because **light bounces off them and travels into our eyes** — not because anything leaves our eyes.
- Straight-line travel explains **shadows** (light is blocked) and **reflection** (light bounces at an angle).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows light comes from sources, reflects, and that opaque objects make shadows (Year 3).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Light always travels in **straight lines** — it never bends round corners by itself. This one fact explains a lot. **How we see:** light from a source (the Sun, a lamp) travels in straight lines and hits an object; some of it **bounces (reflects) off the object and travels in a straight line into your eyes**, and that's when you see the object. The crucial point: **light goes *into* your eyes — nothing comes *out* of them.** You can't see in a totally dark room because there's no light to bounce off anything into your eyes. Straight-line travel also explains **shadows** (an opaque object blocks the straight beams, leaving a dark shape) and **reflection** (light hits a mirror and bounces off in a straight line at an angle).

**Worked example.** Why can't you see a black cat in a pitch-dark room? Because seeing needs **light to travel from a source, bounce off the cat, and go into your eyes** — and there's **no light** in the dark. Switch on a lamp: light travels in straight lines to the cat, reflects off its fur, and travels straight into your eyes, so now you see it. Your eyes didn't "send out" anything; they **received** the light. (This corrects the common idea that we see by rays leaving the eye.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A periscope (two mirrors) lets you see over a wall — light bouncing in straight lines.
- Why you literally cannot see in a completely blacked-out room, however hard you stare.
- Laser pointers / torch beams showing light going in a dead-straight line.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "We see because light/rays come out of our eyes onto things."
  **Why:** it feels like our eyes "reach out" to look.
  **Fix:** light goes **into** the eye — it travels from a source, **reflects off the object, into your eyes**. (See `misconceptions.md`.)
- **Wrong idea:** "Light can bend round corners to let us see hidden things."
  **Why:** we sometimes see around things (via reflections).
  **Fix:** light travels **straight**; to see round a corner you need it to **bounce** off a mirror or surface.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which way does light travel? **A:** In straight lines.
- **Q:** How do your eyes see a book? **A:** Light reflects off the book and travels into your eyes.
- **Q:** Does light come out of your eyes when you look at something? **A:** No — light goes into your eyes.

### Activity / practice
**Straight-line / periscope test:** line up three cards with holes — you can only see the torch when the holes line up straight (light travels straight). Or build a simple two-mirror periscope. Classai block: **multipleChoice** "how do we see?"; **ordering** the light's path source → object → eye.

## 5. Electricity: voltage, brightness and circuit symbols

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Physics – electricity

**Prerequisites:** Year 4 — simple circuits, conductors & insulators.

### Key concepts
- Changing the **number or voltage of cells** changes how a circuit behaves: more cells → brighter bulb, louder buzzer, faster motor.
- Other changes (more bulbs, different wire) also affect brightness — you can investigate this fairly.
- Scientists draw circuits with **standard recognised symbols** (cell, bulb, switch, buzzer, motor, wire).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can build a simple series circuit and knows conductors/insulators (Year 4).

### Teach it (the explanation)
In Year 4 you built a simple loop. Now we change things and see what happens. The **voltage** of a cell is the "push" it gives the electricity. If you add **more cells** (or higher-voltage cells), there's a bigger push, so a **bulb shines brighter**, a **buzzer is louder**, and a **motor spins faster**. If you add **more bulbs** to the same single loop (in series), each one shares the push, so they all get **dimmer**. To record and share circuits clearly, scientists don't draw pictures — they use **standard circuit symbols**: a **cell** (a long line and a short line), a **bulb** (a circle with a cross), an **open/closed switch**, a **buzzer**, a **motor**, and straight lines for **wires**. A **circuit diagram** uses these symbols.

**Worked example.** Build a circuit with **one cell and one bulb** — note how bright it is. Now add a **second cell** (same direction): the bulb shines **brighter**, because there's more voltage pushing the electricity. Add a **second bulb** instead (one cell, two bulbs in series): both bulbs go **dimmer**, because they share the same push. Then draw the bright circuit as a **circuit diagram** using the cell, bulb and wire symbols. **Safety: batteries (cells) only — never experiment with mains electricity.**

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A torch with one vs two batteries — brighter with two.
- Toys that "go faster with fresh/extra batteries" (more voltage → faster motor).
- Reading a real circuit diagram like an electrician or engineer.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "More cells always means it's better / nothing can break."
  **Why:** more = better.
  **Fix:** more voltage makes bulbs **brighter** but can also **burn them out**; it's about the right amount for the components.
- **Wrong idea:** "Adding more bulbs makes them all brighter."
  **Why:** more bulbs seems like more light.
  **Fix:** in a **series** loop, more bulbs **share** the push, so each one is **dimmer**.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What happens to a bulb's brightness if you add another cell? **A:** It gets brighter (more voltage).
- **Q:** In a series circuit, what happens to two bulbs compared with one? **A:** Each is dimmer (they share the voltage).
- **Q:** Why do scientists use circuit symbols? **A:** To draw and share circuits clearly with standard, recognised diagrams.

### Activity / practice
**Brightness investigation:** change the number of cells (one variable) and rate the bulb's brightness each time; then draw the circuit with symbols. **Batteries only — never mains.** Classai block: **matchPairs** component → symbol; **multipleChoice** predict brightness from a setup.
