## 1. Grouping living things with classification keys

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – living things & habitats

**Prerequisites:** Year 1–2 animal groups; Year 2 habitats.

### Key concepts
- Living things can be **classified** (sorted) into groups by their features.
- A **classification key** asks yes/no questions to identify a living thing.
- The same set of things can be grouped in different sensible ways.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can name animal groups (mammal, bird, fish, etc.) and observe features.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Scientists **classify** living things — sort them into groups based on features they share (does it have legs? wings? fur?). A **classification key** is a clever tool for *identifying* something: it asks a chain of **yes/no questions**, and each answer splits the group in two until only one thing is left. To make or use a key, you pick clear features you can actually see ("Does it have wings?" not "Is it nice?"). Good questions give a definite yes or no.

**Worked example.** Identify a robin with a key.
*Does it have feathers?* → Yes → it's a bird.
*Does it have a red front?* → Yes.
*Is it smaller than a pigeon?* → Yes → **robin**.
Each yes/no question narrowed it down. The same key, answered differently (no feathers → fur → mammal), would lead to a different animal.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A "20 questions / guess the animal" game is basically a classification key.
- Sorting bugs found in the garden by legs and wings.
- An app or chart that identifies a leaf or bird by yes/no features.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "There's only one right way to group living things."
  **Why:** keys feel like fixed answers.
  **Fix:** you can group sensibly in several ways (by habitat, by diet, by body features) — what matters is a **clear, observable** rule.
- **Wrong idea:** "Key questions can be about opinions ('is it cute?')."
  **Why:** any question seems fine.
  **Fix:** key questions must have a definite **yes/no** based on **observable features**.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does a classification key do? **A:** Identifies a living thing using yes/no questions about its features.
- **Q:** Is "Does it have wings?" a good key question? Why? **A:** Yes — it has a clear yes/no answer you can observe.
- **Q:** Is "Is it friendly?" a good key question? **A:** No — it's an opinion, not an observable feature.

### Activity / practice
**Make a key:** with a parent, pick 4 household objects or toys and write yes/no questions to tell them apart. Classai block: **ordering** key questions; **multipleChoice** good vs poor key questions.

- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Classification and classification keys*** (grouping; using a key).

## 2. Environments and dangers to living things

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – living things & habitats

**Prerequisites:** Grouping living things; Year 2 habitats & food chains.

### Key concepts
- **Environments can change**, sometimes naturally and sometimes because of humans.
- Changes can put living things in **danger** (losing habitat, pollution, climate change).
- Humans can both **harm and help** the environment (litter & pollution vs. nature reserves & recycling).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows habitats and that living things depend on their environment.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The **environment** — the conditions around living things — can **change**. Some changes are **natural** (seasons, drought, floods); some are caused by **humans** (cutting down forests, building towns, pollution, litter, climate change). When a habitat changes too much, the living things there can be put in **danger**: they may lose their food, water or shelter, and their numbers can drop. But humans can also **help**: planting trees, making nature reserves and ponds, cleaning up litter, recycling and protecting endangered animals all give living things a better chance.

**Worked example.** A pond near a town gets filled with litter and the water gets dirty (a human-caused change). The frogs and fish lose clean water and food, so fewer survive — they're now in **danger**. Then local people clean the pond, stop the litter and plant water plants (a human-caused *help*). Over time the frogs and fish return. Same pond, two kinds of human impact.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A local park or river — what helps wildlife there, what harms it?
- Endangered animals a child likes (pandas, tigers) and why.
- Recycling and litter-picking the child already does at home.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Humans only harm the environment."
  **Why:** the news focuses on damage.
  **Fix:** humans also **help** — reserves, recycling, replanting, conservation.
- **Wrong idea:** "If a habitat changes, animals just move somewhere else easily."
  **Why:** moving seems simple.
  **Fix:** suitable habitats may be far away or full; many animals can't move and are put in danger.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name one human-caused change that can endanger living things. **A:** Pollution / cutting down forests / litter / climate change.
- **Q:** Name one way humans help the environment. **A:** Nature reserves / recycling / planting trees / cleaning litter.
- **Q:** Why can a habitat change put an animal in danger? **A:** It can lose its food, water or shelter.

### Activity / practice
**Help-or-harm sort:** list actions and sort them into "helps wildlife" / "harms wildlife," then choose one helpful thing to do this week. Classai block: **categorize** actions help/harm.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Environments and changes that affect living things*** (dangers and protection).
- **BBC Teach — *Looking after the environment* (KS2)** (short clips on human impact).

## 3. Digestion and teeth

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Biology – animals including humans

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 — Nutrition.

### Key concepts
- The **digestive system** breaks food down so the body can use it: mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine.
- We have different **teeth for different jobs**: incisors (cut), canines (tear), molars (grind).
- Looking after teeth (brushing, less sugar) prevents tooth decay.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows we eat food and have teeth; basic body awareness.

### Teach it (the explanation)
When you eat, food travels through your **digestive system**, which breaks it down so your body can absorb the goodness. The journey: **mouth** (teeth and saliva start breaking food down) → **oesophagus** (a tube that pushes food down) → **stomach** (churns it with juices) → **small intestine** (where the useful nutrients are absorbed into the blood) → **large intestine** (takes back water; the rest becomes waste). Your **teeth** do different jobs: at the front, **incisors** are flat and sharp for **biting and cutting**; the pointier **canines** **tear** food; the big back **molars** **grind and crush**. To keep teeth healthy, **brush twice a day** and cut down on **sugary** food and drink, which cause decay.

**Worked example.** Eat an apple and follow it. Your **incisors** bite a chunk; your **molars** grind it; saliva makes it mushy; you swallow and it slides down the **oesophagus** to the **stomach** to be churned; in the **small intestine** the nutrients pass into your blood to give you energy; what's left goes to the **large intestine** and out as waste. Different teeth, then a step-by-step journey.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A carnivore's sharp canines (a dog, a lion) vs a herbivore's flat grinding molars (a cow).
- Why a dentist says "less sugar, brush twice a day."
- Mirror check: find your own incisors, canines and molars.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Food goes to the stomach and that's digestion done."
  **Why:** the stomach is the famous part.
  **Fix:** most nutrients are absorbed later, in the **small intestine** — digestion is a whole journey.
- **Wrong idea:** "All teeth do the same job."
  **Why:** teeth look similar.
  **Fix:** incisors cut, canines tear, molars grind — different shapes for different jobs.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which teeth grind your food? **A:** Molars (the back teeth).
- **Q:** Where are most nutrients absorbed? **A:** In the small intestine.
- **Q:** Name two ways to keep teeth healthy. **A:** Brush twice a day; eat/drink less sugar.

### Activity / practice
**Tooth map:** in a mirror, point to incisors, canines and molars and say each job. Or order the parts of the digestive system. Classai block: **ordering** the digestive journey; **matchPairs** tooth → job.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *The digestive system* and *Types of teeth*** (journey of food; tooth jobs).
- **Generation Genius — *Digestive System*** (animated, topic level).

## 4. States of matter and the water cycle

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Chemistry / physics – states of matter

**Prerequisites:** Year 1–2 materials.

### Key concepts
- Matter comes in three **states**: **solid**, **liquid**, **gas**, each with different behaviour.
- **Heating and cooling** change the state: melting, freezing, evaporating, condensing.
- The **water cycle** recycles water using evaporation and condensation.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows ice, water and steam are all water; has felt hot and cold things.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Everything is made of tiny particles, and how those particles are arranged gives us three **states of matter**. In a **solid** the particles are packed tightly in place, so a solid keeps its **shape** (ice, wood, rock). In a **liquid** the particles can slide past each other, so a liquid **flows and takes the shape of its container** (water, milk). In a **gas** the particles zoom around freely and spread out to fill any space (steam, air). **Heating** gives particles energy: a solid **melts** into a liquid, and a liquid **evaporates** into a gas. **Cooling** does the reverse: a gas **condenses** into a liquid, and a liquid **freezes** into a solid. Water shows all of this — ice (solid), water (liquid), water vapour/steam (gas). The **water cycle** uses these changes: the Sun **evaporates** water from seas and rivers into vapour; high up it cools and **condenses** into clouds; the water falls as **rain** (precipitation) and flows back to the sea.

**Worked example.** Leave an ice cube on a saucer in a warm room. First it **melts** (solid → liquid: the puddle). Leave the puddle for a day and it disappears — it has **evaporated** (liquid → gas) into the air. If that water vapour meets a cold window, it **condenses** back into water droplets (gas → liquid). Heating melts and evaporates; cooling condenses and freezes.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Ice lolly melting on a hot day; a steamy bathroom mirror (condensation).
- Puddles drying up after rain (evaporation).
- Boiling a kettle — water turning to steam.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Condensation is air turning into water."
  **Why:** the water seems to appear from nowhere on a cold glass.
  **Fix:** it's **water vapour** (a gas already in the air) **cooling and condensing** into liquid — not air becoming water. (See `misconceptions.md`.)
- **Wrong idea:** "When a puddle dries, the water just vanishes / is destroyed."
  **Why:** you can't see where it went.
  **Fix:** it **evaporated** into invisible water vapour in the air; the water still exists.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What happens to a solid when you heat it enough? **A:** It melts into a liquid.
- **Q:** What is it called when a liquid turns into a gas? **A:** Evaporation.
- **Q:** In the water cycle, what makes water evaporate from the sea? **A:** Heat from the Sun.

### Activity / practice
**Melt-and-evaporate watch:** time how long an ice cube takes to melt in two places (warm vs cool) — a fair test, measure with a timer. Then leave the water to evaporate. Classai block: **categorize** objects as solid/liquid/gas; **ordering** the water cycle.

- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *States of matter* and *The water cycle*** (solids/liquids/gases; changes of state; cycle).
- **Generation Genius — *States of Matter* / *Water Cycle*** (animated, topic level).

## 5. Sound: vibrations, pitch and volume

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Physics – sound

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- Sound is made by **vibrations**; the vibrations travel through a **medium** (air, water, solids) to our ears.
- **Pitch** = how high or low a sound is (faster vibration = higher pitch).
- **Volume** = how loud or quiet (bigger vibration = louder); sound gets **fainter with distance**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows we hear with our ears; has made sounds.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Every sound is made by something **vibrating** — shaking back and forth very fast. A guitar string, a drum skin, your voice box: they all vibrate. Those vibrations make the air (or water, or a solid) vibrate too, and the vibrations travel as a **sound wave** until they reach your **ears**, which turn them into the sounds you hear. Sound needs a **medium** to travel through (air, water, or solids) — it can't travel through empty space with nothing in it. Two things describe a sound: **pitch** (how **high or low** it is — faster vibrations make a higher pitch) and **volume/loudness** (how **loud or quiet** — bigger vibrations make a louder sound). As you move **further** from a sound source, the sound gets **quieter**.

**Worked example.** Stretch a rubber band over a box and pluck it. You can **see** it vibrating, and you **hear** the note. Pluck it **harder** — it vibrates more (bigger movement) and sounds **louder** (more volume). Now **tighten** the band and pluck again — it vibrates **faster** and the note is **higher** (higher pitch). Vibration size changes volume; vibration speed changes pitch.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Feel your throat vibrate when you hum.
- A guitar or ukulele: thin/tight strings = high notes; thick/loose = low notes.
- A "string telephone" made from two cups and a taut string (sound through a solid).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Sound can travel through empty space (like in space-movie battles)."
  **Why:** films play sound in space.
  **Fix:** sound needs a medium (air/water/solid); in a vacuum there's nothing to vibrate, so no sound.
- **Wrong idea:** "Louder and higher mean the same thing."
  **Why:** both are "more" of the sound.
  **Fix:** **volume** = loud/quiet (vibration **size**); **pitch** = high/low (vibration **speed**) — different things.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What makes a sound? **A:** Something vibrating.
- **Q:** What changes if vibrations get faster — pitch or volume? **A:** Pitch (it gets higher).
- **Q:** Can sound travel through outer space? Why? **A:** No — there's no air or medium to carry the vibrations.

### Activity / practice
**Rubber-band guitar:** make pitch and volume change by tightening the band and plucking harder/softer. Or a **string telephone**. Classai block: **multipleChoice** pitch vs volume scenarios; **matchPairs** action → effect on sound.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Sound: vibrations, pitch and volume*** (how sound is made and travels).
- **Generation Genius — *Sound: How Sound Travels / Pitch and Volume*** (animated, topic level).

## 6. Simple circuits, conductors and insulators

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Physics – electricity

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- A **simple series circuit** needs a **complete loop** from a **cell (battery)** through wires to a component (bulb, buzzer, motor) and back.
- If the loop is **broken** (a gap or open switch), nothing works; a **switch** opens and closes the loop.
- Some materials are **conductors** (let electricity flow — metals) and some are **insulators** (don't — plastic, wood, rubber).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has seen batteries, bulbs and switches; knows mains is dangerous.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Electricity flows around a **circuit**, which must be a **complete loop**. A simple **series circuit** has a **cell (battery)** to push the electricity, **wires** to carry it, and a **component** like a **bulb, buzzer or motor** to use it. The electricity must be able to go all the way around the loop and back to the cell — if there's a **gap** anywhere (a loose wire, or a **switch turned off**), the loop is **broken** and the bulb won't light. A **switch** is just a controllable gap: closed = loop complete = on; open = gap = off. Materials that let electricity flow are **conductors** (metals like copper, steel — that's why wires are metal); materials that stop it are **insulators** (plastic, rubber, wood, glass — that's why wires have a plastic coating to keep us safe). **Safety: only ever use batteries (cells) — never mains plugs or sockets, which are dangerous.**

**Worked example.** Build a circuit: cell → wire → bulb → wire → back to cell. The bulb lights because the loop is **complete**. Now snip a gap and put a paperclip across it as a switch: when the metal paperclip bridges the gap (closed), the bulb lights; lift it (open), the bulb goes out. Replace the paperclip with a plastic ruler in the gap — the bulb **won't** light, because plastic is an **insulator** and breaks the flow.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A torch — battery, bulb and a switch in a loop.
- Why fairy lights all go out if the loop breaks.
- Why electrical wires are metal inside but plastic outside.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Electricity only needs to reach the bulb from one wire."
  **Why:** children draw a single wire to the bulb.
  **Fix:** it must be a **complete loop** — out from the cell, through the bulb, and **back** to the cell.
- **Wrong idea:** "All metals' opposite — wood/plastic — could carry electricity too."
  **Why:** unsure which materials conduct.
  **Fix:** test them: metals conduct (bulb lights); plastic, wood, rubber are insulators (bulb stays off).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What three things does a simple circuit need? **A:** A cell (battery), wires, and a component (e.g. bulb).
- **Q:** Why won't a bulb light if there's a gap in the circuit? **A:** The loop is broken, so electricity can't flow round.
- **Q:** Is plastic a conductor or an insulator? **A:** An insulator (it doesn't let electricity flow).

### Activity / practice
**Conductor test:** build a battery-and-bulb circuit with a gap, then put different objects (coin, paperclip, plastic spoon, pencil lead) across the gap and see which light the bulb. **Batteries only — never mains.** Classai block: **categorize** materials conductor/insulator; **multipleChoice** "will this circuit light?"
