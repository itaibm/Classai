## 1. Variables and fair testing (changing one thing at a time)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Working Scientifically – fair testing

**Prerequisites:** Forces: pushes, pulls and friction (Y3), Sound: vibrations, pitch and volume (Y4).

### Key concepts
- A **fair test** changes **one** thing (the **independent variable**), keeps everything else the **same** (the **control variables**), and measures the result (the **dependent variable**).
- If you change two things at once, you can't tell which one caused the result.
- A good investigation question is testable: "Does ___ affect ___?"

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Making a prediction; measuring with a ruler/timer; the idea of "the same / different".

### Teach it (the explanation)
To find out if one thing affects another, you must be **fair**: change only the thing you're testing, hold everything else still, and measure what happens. Name the three variable jobs: the one you **change**, the ones you **keep the same**, and the one you **measure**. If a car rolls further on wood than carpet but you also pushed it harder, the test is spoiled — you changed two things.

**Worked example.** Question: "Does the slope's height affect how far a toy car rolls?" Change: ramp height (10, 20, 30 cm). Keep the same: same car, same ramp surface, same starting push (just release). Measure: distance rolled. Now any difference in distance is caused by the height alone.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Ramp races with toy cars (height vs distance).
- "Which paper towel soaks up most water?" (same water, same time).
- Which conditions make a paper aeroplane fly furthest.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** changes several things at once and still claims a cause. **Why:** doesn't isolate the variable. **Fix:** "change ONE thing, keep the rest the same" — list the controls out loud.
- **Wrong:** thinks "fair" means "everyone has a turn". **Why:** everyday meaning of fair. **Fix:** in science, *fair test* = only one variable changes.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** In a fair test, how many things do you change? **A:** One.
- **Q:** Testing if water amount affects plant growth — what must stay the same? **A:** Light, soil, pot, temperature, plant type.
- **Q:** Why keep everything else the same? **A:** So you know the change you made caused the result.

### Activity / practice
categorize: sort items into "change / keep the same / measure" for a given question; design one fair test (speak).

## 2. Recording and reading data: tables, bar charts and line graphs

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Working Scientifically – data handling

**Prerequisites:** Variables and fair testing, States of matter and the water cycle (Y4).

### Key concepts
- Results go in a **table** first (label columns with the quantity **and unit**), then into a chart.
- A **bar chart** compares **separate categories** (types of surface); a **line graph** shows **continuous change**, usually over time (temperature as ice melts).
- A graph needs a **title, labelled axes with units, and a sensible scale**; read values by tracing to the axes.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Reading axes; the idea of a category vs a measurement over time; using standard units.

### Teach it (the explanation)
First **record** in a neat table — each column headed with what it is and its unit (Time / minutes; Temperature / °C). Then pick the right chart: **bars for categories** you can't put in order numerically, a **line for something measured continuously** (so the line between points has meaning). Always title it and label both axes. To read a value, go up from the x-axis and across to the y-axis.

**Worked example.** Cooling water measured every 2 minutes: time goes on the x-axis (it's continuous), temperature on the y-axis → a **line graph** (falling line). To compare how far a car rolled on carpet, wood and tile (three separate surfaces) → a **bar chart**, because the categories aren't a continuous scale.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Graphing the temperature of a melting ice lolly over time.
- A bar chart of favourite playground games (a survey).
- Plotting a plant's height each day.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** uses a line graph for separate categories. **Why:** "line graphs look neater". **Fix:** a line means values *between* points exist — only true for continuous data.
- **Wrong:** forgets units / labels on axes. **Why:** rushes the chart. **Fix:** "no label, no marks" — always title and label with units.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Bar chart or line graph for temperature every minute? **A:** Line graph (continuous over time).
- **Q:** What two things must every axis have? **A:** A label and a unit.
- **Q:** Why does a table come before the graph? **A:** To organise the results so you can plot them accurately.

### Activity / practice
numberEntry / multipleChoice: choose the right chart type for three data sets; read three values off a line graph.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Bar charts and line graphs / recording data** (KS2).
- **Oak National Academy — Recording and presenting data** units.

## 3. Prediction and conclusions (the heart of an investigation)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Working Scientifically – prediction & evaluation

**Prerequisites:** Variables and fair testing, Recording and reading data.

### Key concepts
- A **prediction** is a *reasoned* guess ("I think ___ will ___ **because** ___") made **before** the test — not a wish.
- A **conclusion** says what the results show and links back to the prediction, using the evidence ("The results show… which means…").
- An **evaluation** asks "how reliable was this, and what would I improve?" — repeats reduce the effect of mistakes.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The predict→explain→check loop; reading results from a table or chart.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Before doing anything, **predict and give a reason** based on what you already know — this exposes your mental model. After the test, write a **conclusion** that states the pattern in the results and whether it matched the prediction, quoting actual numbers. Then **evaluate**: were there odd results (anomalies)? Did you repeat to be sure? What would make it more reliable? The surprise when a prediction is wrong is the best learning moment — the model updates.

**Worked example.** Prediction: "The higher the ramp, the further the car rolls, **because** a steeper slope gives it more speed." Results: 10 cm → 50 cm; 20 cm → 95 cm; 30 cm → 140 cm. Conclusion: "The results show that as ramp height increased, distance increased, which supports my prediction." Evaluation: "I'd repeat each height three times and average them to be more reliable."

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Predict which ice cube melts first (in hand vs on a plate) and check.
- "I bet the bigger seed grows tallest" — predict, plant, measure.
- Predict which ball bounces highest, then test.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** prediction is just "I don't know" or "it'll work". **Why:** no reasoning. **Fix:** force the "because" — a prediction needs a reason.
- **Wrong:** when results contradict the prediction, the child changes the results. **Why:** thinks being "wrong" is bad. **Fix:** a surprising result is a *discovery*, not a failure — report it honestly.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What word should every prediction include? **A:** "Because" (a reason).
- **Q:** What does a conclusion do? **A:** States what the results show and links to the prediction.
- **Q:** Why repeat a test? **A:** To check the results are reliable and spot odd ones.

### Activity / practice
speak / fillBlank: write a "because" prediction for a given question, then a matching conclusion frame after seeing sample results.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Predictions and conclusions** (KS2 working scientifically).
- **Generation Genius — Asking questions and making predictions**.

## 4. Food chains, food webs and energy from the Sun

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Living things – ecosystems

**Prerequisites:** Food chains (Y2), Digestion and teeth — producer/predator/prey (Y4).

### Key concepts
- Energy flows from the **Sun → producers (plants) → consumers**; arrows point in the **direction the energy/food goes** (eaten → eater).
- **Producer** (makes its own food), **herbivore/primary consumer**, **predator/carnivore**, **prey**, **top predator**, **decomposer**.
- A **food web** is many food chains linked — most animals eat (and are eaten by) more than one thing, so removing one species affects many.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Simple food chains; carnivore/herbivore/omnivore; that plants make food using light.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Almost all food energy starts at the **Sun**. Plants (**producers**) capture it; animals get it by eating. In a food chain the **arrow points to who gets the energy** (grass → rabbit → fox). Real ecosystems are **webs**, not single chains, because animals have several food sources. If one link is removed (e.g. all the rabbits), everything connected is affected — predators lose food, plants are less grazed. **Decomposers** (worms, fungi) recycle dead matter back to the soil.

**Worked example.** Chain: **grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk.** Grass is the producer; grasshopper the primary consumer (herbivore); frog/snake/hawk are predators; the hawk is the top predator. The arrows show energy moving up the chain. Remove the frogs and grasshopper numbers would rise while snakes would have less to eat.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Build a food web for a local habitat (pond, woodland, garden).
- "What if all the bees disappeared?" — knock-on effects.
- A favourite predator (shark, eagle) and what it depends on.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** draws the arrow from eater to eaten (fox → rabbit). **Why:** thinks the arrow means "eats". **Fix:** the arrow follows the **energy/food** — point it *to* the one that gains.
- **Wrong:** thinks plants "eat" soil for food. **Why:** confuses nutrients with food. **Fix:** plants are producers — they *make* food using light (photosynthesis); soil gives minerals, not food.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which way does a food-chain arrow point? **A:** Towards the animal that eats (the direction energy flows).
- **Q:** What is a producer? **A:** A plant that makes its own food using light.
- **Q:** In grass → rabbit → fox, what is the fox? **A:** A predator / consumer (top of this chain).

### Activity / practice
ordering / matchPairs: arrange organisms into a correct food chain with arrows; build a small food web and predict the effect of removing one species.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Food chains and food webs** (KS2).
- **Generation Genius — Food chains and food webs / energy flow**.

## 5. The water cycle (the science of evaporation and condensation)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Materials – states of matter / Earth

**Prerequisites:** States of matter and the water cycle (Y4 — this deepens the *why*).

### Key concepts
- Water moves in a never-ending cycle: **evaporation → condensation → precipitation → collection**, driven by the **Sun's heat**.
- **Evaporation** = liquid water gains heat and becomes invisible water **vapour** (a gas); **condensation** = vapour cools and turns back into liquid droplets (clouds).
- The **same water** is recycled — it is not created or used up.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Solids, liquids and gases; melting/freezing/evaporation as state changes; that the Sun gives heat.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The Sun heats water in seas, rivers and puddles; some **evaporates** into invisible vapour and rises. High up it's colder, so the vapour **condenses** into tiny droplets, forming clouds. When droplets join and get heavy, they fall as **precipitation** (rain, snow, hail). Water **collects** in rivers and seas, and the cycle repeats. Crucially, it's the **same water** going round — the puddle that "disappears" hasn't vanished; it became vapour.

**Worked example.** A puddle after rain shrinks on a sunny day — not gone, but **evaporated** into vapour. The bathroom mirror "fogs" during a hot shower because warm vapour **condenses** on the cool glass. Both are the same processes that drive the global water cycle. (Demo safely: an adult boils a kettle; cold-spoon held *near, not in* the steam shows condensation — adult handles anything hot.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Why a puddle disappears on a sunny day.
- Mist/fog on a cold window or a cold drink "sweating".
- Where the rain came from and where it goes.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** "the puddle dried up and the water is gone." **Why:** can't see the vapour. **Fix:** it became invisible water **vapour** — evaporation, not disappearance.
- **Wrong:** "the droplets on a cold glass came through the glass." **Why:** misreads condensation. **Fix:** vapour in the air **cooled and condensed** on the outside of the cold glass.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is evaporation? **A:** Liquid water heating up and turning into water vapour (gas).
- **Q:** What makes clouds form? **A:** Water vapour cooling and condensing into droplets.
- **Q:** Is new water made in the water cycle? **A:** No — the same water is recycled.

### Activity / practice
ordering: sequence the cycle (evaporation → condensation → precipitation → collection); fillBlank label a water-cycle diagram.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — The water cycle** (KS2).
- **Generation Genius — The water cycle**.

## 6. Reversible vs irreversible change (physical vs chemical change)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Materials – changes

**Prerequisites:** Reversible and irreversible changes (Y5 — this sharpens physical vs chemical and the "new material" test).

### Key concepts
- A **reversible (physical) change** can be undone — the material is the same, just in a different form (melting, dissolving, freezing). No **new material** is made.
- An **irreversible (chemical) change** makes a **new material** and usually can't be undone (burning, cooking an egg, rusting, mixing vinegar + bicarb).
- **Clues to a chemical change:** a new colour, a gas (bubbles/fizz), heat/light given out, or a smell — and you can't get the originals back.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- States of matter; dissolving; that some changes can be reversed.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Ask: **is a new material made?** If the stuff is the *same* afterwards (just melted, dissolved or frozen), it's a **reversible/physical** change — you can get the original back (melt chocolate, then re-set it; evaporate salty water to recover the salt). If a **new** substance forms — a different colour, a gas, heat, a smell, ash — it's an **irreversible/chemical** change and you generally can't undo it (a burnt match, a fried egg, rust).

**Worked example.** Melting ice → water → freeze again: same material (water) in different states = **reversible**. Mixing **vinegar + bicarbonate of soda**: it fizzes (a new gas, carbon dioxide, is made) and you can't get the originals back = **irreversible/chemical**. (Safety: an adult supervises; never taste; never burn anything without an adult.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Baking — raw batter to a cooked cake (you can't un-bake it).
- The fizzy "volcano" (vinegar + bicarb).
- Rusting bikes / browning apples vs melting and re-freezing ice.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks dissolving sugar "destroys" it. **Why:** it disappears from view. **Fix:** it's still there (taste/evaporate to recover) — reversible, no new material.
- **Wrong:** thinks all changes can be undone. **Why:** over-generalises from melting. **Fix:** look for a *new material* (gas, colour, ash) → irreversible.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Reversible or irreversible: melting butter? **A:** Reversible (same material, can re-set).
- **Q:** What's a clue that a chemical change happened? **A:** A new material — fizzing/gas, new colour, heat or a smell.
- **Q:** Is burning toast reversible? **A:** No — a new material (carbon/ash) is made.

### Activity / practice
categorize: sort changes into reversible / irreversible and tick the "new material" clues; predict-and-explain for a vinegar + bicarb demo.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Reversible and irreversible changes** (KS2).
- **Generation Genius — Chemical vs physical changes**.

## 7. Deeper circuits: what changes the brightness of a bulb?

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Physics – electricity

**Prerequisites:** Simple circuits, conductors and insulators (Y4), Electricity: voltage, brightness and circuit symbols (Y6 — this deepens with investigation & symbols).

### Key concepts
- A bulb only lights if it's part of a **complete circuit** (an unbroken loop from a cell, through components, and back).
- **More cells (more voltage)** make a bulb **brighter** and a buzzer **louder**; **more bulbs** in the same loop share the voltage, so each is **dimmer**.
- Standard **circuit symbols** (cell, bulb, switch, buzzer, wire) let scientists draw circuits clearly. **Only batteries/cells — never mains** for children.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Complete circuits; conductors/insulators; switches; the idea of a cell/battery.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A circuit must be a **complete loop** with a power source (cell). Adding **more cells** pushes harder (higher **voltage**), so the bulb glows brighter — until too many burn it out. Adding **more bulbs** to the same single loop divides the voltage between them, so each glows **more dimly**. Drawing circuits with **standard symbols** (a long+short line for a cell, a cross-in-circle for a bulb, a gap for an open switch) makes them universal. **Safety:** investigate only with batteries/cells and low-voltage bulbs — mains electricity is for adults and is dangerous.

**Worked example.** One cell + one bulb → normal brightness. Add a **second cell** in the loop (in line, +→−) → the bulb is **brighter** (more voltage). Now go back to one cell but add a **second bulb** in the same loop → both bulbs are **dimmer** (the voltage is shared). Drawn with symbols, the change is obvious at a glance.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Torches with one vs two batteries (brightness).
- Fairy lights / a string of bulbs going dim.
- Designing a circuit for a model (a lit-up house, a quiz buzzer).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks electricity is "used up" so the bulb nearest the cell is brightest. **Why:** a flow-runs-out model. **Fix:** in a simple series loop the current is the same everywhere; brightness depends on voltage and the number of bulbs.
- **Wrong:** thinks one wire is enough. **Why:** doesn't grasp the loop. **Fix:** it must be a *complete circuit* — out of one end, back to the other.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What happens to a bulb if you add a second cell (in line)? **A:** It gets brighter (more voltage).
- **Q:** Add a second bulb to the same loop — brighter or dimmer? **A:** Dimmer (voltage shared).
- **Q:** Why won't a bulb light if the circuit is broken? **A:** No complete loop, so no current flows.

### Activity / practice
multipleChoice / matchPairs: predict brightness for different cell/bulb combinations; match components to their circuit symbols and draw a simple circuit.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Circuits, voltage and circuit symbols** (KS2).
- **Generation Genius — Electricity and circuits**.

## 8. Classification keys (sorting living things by questions)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Living things – classification

**Prerequisites:** Grouping living things with classification keys (Y4), Classifying living things into broad groups (Y6).

### Key concepts
- A **classification key** sorts living things by asking a series of **yes/no questions** about observable features.
- Each question splits the group in two; following the path leads to a single organism's name.
- Good key questions are about **clear, observable** features (Does it have wings? Are there six legs?), not vague ones (Is it nice?).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Vertebrate/invertebrate groups; observing physical features; sorting by a rule.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A key is a sorting machine made of **yes/no questions**. Start with all the organisms; the first question splits them into two groups; each further question splits again until each branch ends at one named thing. To **build** a key, pick a feature only some share and ask about it; to **use** one, answer each question by looking carefully at the creature and follow the matching branch.

**Worked example.** To identify a minibeast: *Does it have six legs?* — No → *Does it have eight legs?* Yes → it's a **spider**. Yes (six legs) → *Does it have wings?* No → **ant**; Yes → *Does it have a hard wing-case?* Yes → **beetle**. Each answer narrows it to one organism.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Identify minibeasts found in the garden with a key.
- A "guess who" of animals using yes/no questions.
- Sorting leaves or shells by features.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** uses vague questions ("is it scary?"). **Why:** subjective, not observable. **Fix:** keys must use clear, observable features everyone would agree on.
- **Wrong:** an organism fits two branches. **Why:** overlapping questions. **Fix:** each question must split the group cleanly into yes/no.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What kind of questions does a classification key use? **A:** Yes/no questions about observable features.
- **Q:** A creature has 8 legs and no wings — likely group? **A:** A spider/arachnid.
- **Q:** Why is "is it pretty?" a poor key question? **A:** It's subjective, not an observable feature.

### Activity / practice
categorize: follow a branching key to identify three minibeasts; build a 3-question key to separate four animals.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Classification keys** (KS2).
- **Generation Genius — Classification of living things**.

## 9. Adaptation and survival (how living things fit their habitat)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Living things – adaptation & evolution

**Prerequisites:** Habitats and microhabitats (Y2), Evolution and inheritance (Y6 — this builds toward it).

### Key concepts
- An **adaptation** is a feature or behaviour that helps a living thing **survive** in its habitat (a camel's hump, a cactus's spines, an owl's night vision).
- Adaptations can be **physical** (body parts) or **behavioural** (what it does — migrating, hibernating).
- Living things suited to their environment are **more likely to survive and reproduce** — the seed of natural selection.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- What a habitat is; basic animal/plant features; that animals have needs (food, water, shelter).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Every habitat sets challenges (heat, cold, predators, little water), and living things have **adaptations** that meet them. A polar bear has thick fur and fat for the cold; a cactus stores water and has spines instead of leaves to lose less water and deter eaters. Some adaptations are **behaviours** — birds migrate, hedgehogs hibernate. Animals best suited to their surroundings survive and pass on those features, which (over very long times) is how species change — linking to evolution.

**Worked example.** A **camel** in the desert: long eyelashes and closable nostrils keep out sand (physical); a hump stores fat for energy when food is scarce; wide feet stop it sinking in sand; it can go days without water. Each feature answers a desert challenge. Compare a **polar bear**: opposite habitat, opposite adaptations (insulating blubber, white camouflage).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "Design an animal" for a made-up habitat (volcano, deep sea).
- Compare a desert vs a polar animal's features.
- Why a cactus looks so different from a rainforest plant.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks an animal "chooses" or "tries" to adapt in its lifetime. **Why:** treats adaptation as a decision. **Fix:** adaptations are inherited features that helped ancestors survive — they build up over generations.
- **Wrong:** thinks any feature is an adaptation. **Why:** over-applies the word. **Fix:** an adaptation specifically **helps survival** in that habitat.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name one camel adaptation and what it's for. **A:** e.g. hump stores fat for energy when food is scarce.
- **Q:** Is hibernating a physical or behavioural adaptation? **A:** Behavioural.
- **Q:** Why do well-adapted animals survive better? **A:** Their features help them get food/avoid danger, so they live to reproduce.

### Activity / practice
matchPairs: link adaptations to the habitat challenge they solve; speak — "design an animal" for a given extreme habitat and justify each feature.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Adaptation and habitats** (KS2).
- **Generation Genius — Adaptations / inherited traits and the environment**.

## 10. Shadows and how we see (light, straight lines and reflection)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Physics – light

**Prerequisites:** Light, dark and shadows (Y3), How light reflects (Y3), How we see: light travels in straight lines (Y6 — this consolidates and investigates).

### Key concepts
- **Light travels in straight lines** from a source; a **shadow** forms where an **opaque** object blocks it.
- We **see** an object because light **reflects off it into our eyes** — we do **not** see by light leaving our eyes.
- A shadow's **size changes** with the distance between the light, the object and the screen — closer to the light = bigger shadow.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Light sources; opaque/transparent; that light reflects; shadows form when light is blocked.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Light goes in **straight lines** (rays). When an opaque object blocks rays, the light can't bend round it, so a **shadow** (a dark area the shape of the object) appears behind it. We **see** things because light from a source bounces **off** them and **into our eyes** — a common wrong idea is that our eyes send out light; they don't (you can't see in a totally dark room, no matter how hard you "look"). Move a torch **closer** to an object and its shadow grows **larger** (the rays spread out more before hitting the screen).

**Worked example.** Hold a hand between a torch and a wall. The shadow is the hand's shape because the hand blocks the straight rays. Move the hand **towards the torch** → the shadow gets **bigger**; move it towards the wall → **smaller**. To "see" the hand at all, light from the torch reflects off it into your eyes — turn the torch off in a sealed dark room and you see nothing, proving sight needs light *entering* the eye. (Safety: never look directly at the Sun or a bright light source.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Shadow puppets — change the size by moving the torch.
- Why your shadow is long in the early morning/evening (low Sun).
- A pinhole or a peep into a totally dark box (you can't see in the dark).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** "we see because light comes out of our eyes." **Why:** it *feels* like looking is active. **Fix:** the dark-room test — no light entering = no sight; we see reflected light *entering* the eye.
- **Wrong:** thinks a shadow is a "reflection" of the object. **Why:** confuses shadow with image. **Fix:** a shadow is just *where light is blocked* — no detail, no colour, the object's outline.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Why can't you see in a completely dark room? **A:** No light is entering your eyes; we see by reflected light, not light from our eyes.
- **Q:** Move an object closer to the light — what happens to its shadow? **A:** It gets bigger.
- **Q:** Why does a shadow form? **A:** An opaque object blocks light, which travels in straight lines.

### Activity / practice
predict-and-check (speak) + multipleChoice: predict how a shadow changes as the object moves, test with a torch; draw straight-line light rays from source → object → eye.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize — Light, shadows and how we see** (KS2).
- **Generation Genius — Light and how we see / shadows**.

## 11. Sound: how it travels and how we hear (deeper)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Physics – sound

**Prerequisites:** Sound: vibrations, pitch and volume (Y4 — this deepens travel, medium and distance).

### Key concepts
- Sound is made by **vibrations**; it travels as the vibration passes through a **medium** (solid, liquid or gas) to our ears.
- Sound **needs a medium** — it can't travel through a vacuum (empty space), unlike light.
- Sound gets **quieter with distance** (the energy spreads out); **pitch** depends on how *fast* something vibrates, **volume** on how *big* the vibration (energy) is.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Vibrations make sound; higher pitch vs louder volume; the ear hears sound.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A vibrating object (a drum skin, a guitar string, vocal cords) makes the particles around it vibrate, passing the sound along through a **medium** to your ear, where it's detected. Sound travels through solids and liquids too — often faster — but **can't travel through empty space**, because there are no particles to pass the vibration on (that's why space is silent). The further you are, the **quieter** the sound, as the energy spreads out. **Fast** vibrations = **high pitch**; **big** vibrations (more energy) = **loud** volume.

**Worked example.** Tap a tuning fork and touch it to water — the water sprays, showing the vibration. Put a ticking phone alarm in a sealed jar and pump the air out (a classic demo): as air is removed, the sound **fades**, because sound needs particles. Two strings: pluck a tight, thin one (fast vibration → **high pitch**); pluck it harder (bigger vibration → **louder**, same pitch).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A "string telephone" (two cups and a taut string — sound through a solid).
- Why music sounds muffled underwater or through a wall.
- Why space films should be silent (no air to carry sound).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** thinks sound can travel through empty space. **Why:** films show explosions in space. **Fix:** sound needs a medium; with no particles, no sound.
- **Wrong:** confuses pitch and volume (thinks louder = higher). **Why:** muddles the two. **Fix:** pitch = how *fast* it vibrates (high/low); volume = how *big* the vibration (loud/quiet).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Can sound travel through empty space? **A:** No — it needs a medium (particles to vibrate).
- **Q:** What makes a sound higher in pitch? **A:** Faster vibrations.
- **Q:** Why does a sound get quieter as you move away? **A:** The sound energy spreads out.

### Activity / practice
predict-and-check / matchPairs: make a string telephone and test through string vs air; sort "changes pitch" vs "changes volume" actions.

- **BBC Bitesize — How sound travels / pitch and volume** (KS2).
- **Generation Genius — Sound waves and how we hear**.

## 12. Asking scientific questions and choosing the right enquiry

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Working Scientifically – enquiry types

**Prerequisites:** Variables and fair testing, Prediction and conclusions.

### Key concepts
- The **five enquiry types**: observing over time, identifying/classifying/grouping, pattern seeking, comparative/fair testing, and researching using secondary sources.
- A good scientific question is **testable** — you can answer it by observing, measuring or researching, not just by opinion.
- **Match the enquiry to the question**: some questions need a fair test, others need watching over time, sorting, surveying, or a book/website.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The idea of a fair test; making observations; using books/sites to find out.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Not every question is answered the same way. First, make sure it's **testable** ("Does light affect how fast cress grows?" — yes; "Is cress nice?" — no, that's opinion). Then choose the right **enquiry type**: a *fair test* to find what affects what; *observing over time* to watch a change (a germinating seed, the Moon); *classifying* to sort; *pattern seeking* to spot a relationship you can't control (are woodlice found in damp places?); *secondary research* for things you can't observe (what's inside the Earth). Naming the enquiry helps a child think like a scientist.

**Worked example.** Question: "Does the temperature of water affect how fast sugar dissolves?" → **comparative/fair test** (change temperature, keep amount of sugar/water the same, measure dissolving time). Question: "How does the Moon's shape change over a month?" → **observing over time** (record nightly). Question: "What did a T. rex eat?" → **secondary research** (you can't observe it).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Sort a list of the child's own "I wonder…" questions by enquiry type.
- Decide how to answer "are bigger conkers heavier?" (pattern seeking).
- Plan an investigation for a question the child genuinely wants answered.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong:** treats every question as a fair test. **Why:** fair testing is the most-taught type. **Fix:** some questions need observing, sorting or research instead — match the method.
- **Wrong:** asks an opinion question and calls it scientific. **Why:** confuses testable with likeable. **Fix:** "can we observe, measure or research the answer?" If not, it's not (yet) a scientific question.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name the five enquiry types. **A:** Observing over time; classifying/grouping; pattern seeking; comparative/fair testing; secondary research.
- **Q:** Which enquiry suits "how does a shadow change through the day?" **A:** Observing over time.
- **Q:** Is "is football the best sport?" a testable scientific question? **A:** No — it's an opinion.

### Activity / practice
categorize: sort questions by enquiry type; turn one "I wonder…" question into a testable form and name the right enquiry.

- **BBC Bitesize — Types of scientific enquiry** (KS2 working scientifically).
- **Generation Genius — Scientific method / asking questions**.
