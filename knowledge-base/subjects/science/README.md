# Science — Subject overview & scope (UK National Curriculum, KS1–KS2)

This is the self-contained Science knowledge base for the Classai tutor, ages 5–11 (Years 1–6), aligned to the **UK National Curriculum** for science at Key Stage 1 (Years 1–2) and Key Stage 2 (Years 3–6). Each `year-N.md` file holds the teachable topics for that year, written in the per-topic contract from `_TEMPLATE.md`. This README explains **how to teach primary science** and gives the full **Year 1→6 scope & sequence** of the real NC units.

## What primary science is for

Primary science has two intertwined goals, and good lessons serve both at once:

1. **Substantive knowledge** — the actual content: that plants need light and water, that materials change state, that forces are pushes and pulls, that the Earth orbits the Sun. This knowledge is *cumulative*: Year 1 "what plants need" becomes Year 3 "how water travels in a plant" becomes Year 5 "the life cycle of a flowering plant."
2. **Working Scientifically** — *how* we find things out. This is not a separate unit; it is the set of skills woven through every topic. A child who can name the parts of a flower but has never planted a seed, predicted, and checked has only half of science.

## Working Scientifically (the skills woven through every topic)

The NC names five types of scientific enquiry. The tutor should pick the one that fits the question and name it for the child, so they build a vocabulary for *how to think*:

- **Observing over time** — watching something change (a seed germinating, ice melting, the Moon's shape, a shadow through the day). The key move: record at intervals and compare.
- **Identifying, classifying & grouping** — sorting things by their properties (living/non-living, magnetic/non-magnetic, vertebrate/invertebrate). The key move: choose a clear sorting rule and apply it consistently.
- **Pattern seeking** — looking for relationships you can't easily control, often outdoors or with surveys (do bigger seeds grow taller? are woodlice found in dark places?). The key move: spot a "the bigger the…, the…" relationship.
- **Comparative & fair testing** — changing one thing, keeping everything else the same, to see its effect (does a ball roll further on carpet or wood?). The key move: **change one variable, keep the rest the same, measure the result.** This is the heart of KS2 experimental work.
- **Researching using secondary sources** — finding out things you can't observe directly (what dinosaurs ate, what's inside the Earth, how the heart works) from books, videos and trusted sites.

Across the years, also build the practical strands: **asking questions; making predictions; taking measurements** (with rulers, thermometers, timers, data loggers, and standard units by KS2); **gathering and recording data** (tables, tally charts, then bar charts and line graphs); **using results to draw conclusions**; and **evaluating** — "what would I do differently, and how sure am I?"

## How to teach a primary science topic well

**Predict → Explain → Check (the core loop).** Before any demonstration or experiment, ask the child what they think will happen *and why*. Their prediction reveals their existing model. Then do the thing. Then compare what happened to what they predicted. The surprise when a prediction fails is the most powerful teaching moment in science — it forces the mental model to update. Never skip the prediction to "just show them"; the showing only lands if a guess was on the table first.

**Tackle misconceptions head-on.** Children arrive with strong, sensible-feeling wrong ideas (heavy things fall faster; plants eat soil; we see by light leaving our eyes; the Sun moves across the sky). These are not random errors — they are coherent everyday theories that have to be *replaced*, not just contradicted. The tutor's job is to (a) surface the wrong idea, (b) create an experience or argument that the wrong idea can't explain, and (c) offer the correct idea as the better explanation. Each topic file lists the classic misconceptions for that content with a *why* and a *fix*; `misconceptions.md` consolidates the biggest ones. Confronting them is the single highest-value move in primary science.

**Concrete → pictorial → abstract.** Start with a real object or event the child can see and touch (a magnet, an ice cube, a torch and a wall). Move to a drawing or diagram (light as straight arrows; a food chain; the water cycle). Only then use the abstract word (reflection, evaporation, predator). Don't lead with vocabulary.

**Observe first, name second, explain third.** Good science lessons begin with noticing. "What do you see? What changed? What's the same?" Naming and explaining come after careful looking — not before.

**Make it safe and hands-on at home.** Every topic includes a simple home observation or experiment a child can do with a parent using kitchen and garden materials. Safety is built in: an adult handles anything hot, sharp, or electrical-mains; never taste unknown substances; never look at the Sun; mains electricity is for adults only — children explore circuits with batteries (cells) and bulbs. The tutor should always state the safety note, not assume it.

**Vocabulary is taught, not assumed.** Words like *opaque*, *evaporation*, *vertebrate*, *circuit*, *friction* are precise tools. Introduce each with a clear, child-sized definition and a concrete example, then use it repeatedly so it sticks.

## Full scope & sequence — the real NC units, Year 1 → Year 6

Each year covers a small set of named units. Notice the spiral: **plants, animals & humans, materials, and physical processes** all recur and deepen.

### Key Stage 1

**Year 1 (ages 5–6)** — *first careful looking, naming the everyday world*
1. Plants (name common wild & garden plants; parts of a plant; deciduous vs evergreen trees)
2. Animals, including humans (name & group common animals; carnivore/herbivore/omnivore; the human body & senses)
3. Everyday materials (name materials; describe their properties)
4. Seasonal changes (the four seasons; day length; weather)
*(Working Scientifically: observe closely, identify and classify, ask simple questions.)*

**Year 2 (ages 6–7)** — *living things in their world; how things grow; what materials are for*
1. Living things and their habitats (living/dead/never-alive; habitats & microhabitats; simple food chains)
2. Plants (seeds and bulbs grow into plants; what plants need to grow)
3. Animals, including humans (animals have offspring that grow; basic needs of animals; humans: exercise, food, hygiene)
4. Uses of everyday materials (why a material is chosen for a job; materials can change shape)
*(Working Scientifically: simple comparative tests, gather and record data, observe growth over time.)*

### Key Stage 2

**Year 3 (ages 7–8)** — *systems begin; first physics of light and forces*
1. Plants (functions of roots, stem, leaves, flowers; what plants need; water transport; pollination & seed dispersal)
2. Animals, including humans (nutrition — animals can't make their own food; skeletons and muscles)
3. Rocks (group rocks by properties; how fossils form; soils)
4. Light (we need light to see; light reflects; shadows form when light is blocked; Sun safety)
5. Forces and magnets (pushes and pulls; movement on different surfaces; magnets attract/repel; magnetic materials)
*(Working Scientifically: set up simple fair tests, make systematic observations, use results to draw conclusions.)*

**Year 4 (ages 8–9)** — *classification, the digestive system, the physics of matter, sound & electricity*
1. Living things and their habitats (grouping with classification keys; environmental change & dangers to living things)
2. Animals, including humans (the digestive system; types of teeth; food chains — producer, predator, prey)
3. States of matter (solids, liquids, gases; melting/freezing/evaporation; the water cycle)
4. Sound (sound is made by vibrations; how sound travels; pitch and volume)
5. Electricity (simple series circuits; conductors and insulators; switches; mains vs battery safety)
*(Working Scientifically: take accurate measurements with standard units, record data in tables and bar charts.)*

**Year 5 (ages 9–10)** — *life cycles, growing up, changing materials, the Solar System, real forces*
1. Living things and their habitats (life cycles of mammal, amphibian, insect, bird; reproduction in plants and animals)
2. Animals, including humans (changes from birth to old age; human development to old age)
3. Properties and changes of materials (compare & group by property; dissolving, separating mixtures; reversible vs irreversible changes)
4. Earth and space (the Sun-centred Solar System; Earth's rotation = day/night; the Moon's orbit)
5. Forces (gravity; air resistance, water resistance, friction; mechanisms — levers, pulleys, gears)
*(Working Scientifically: plan different enquiry types, control variables, report findings with conclusions and explanations.)*

**Year 6 (ages 10–11)** — *classifying life, the circulatory system, evolution, deeper light & electricity*
1. Living things and their habitats (classification into broad groups; micro-organisms, plants, animals; how & why we classify)
2. Animals, including humans (the circulatory system — heart, blood, vessels; diet, exercise, drugs & lifestyle; how nutrients and water are transported)
3. Evolution and inheritance (offspring vary; adaptation to environment; fossils as evidence; natural selection — simply)
4. Light (light travels in straight lines; how we see — light enters the eye; reflection and shadows explained by straight-line travel)
5. Electricity (effect of voltage — number/voltage of cells; brightness and loudness; recognised circuit symbols)
*(Working Scientifically: plan and carry out controlled investigations, use test results to make predictions and set up further tests, report and present with scientific language and causal explanations.)*

## Files in this folder

- `year-1.md` … `year-6.md` — the teachable topics, each in the `_TEMPLATE.md` block (key concepts, prior knowledge, teach-it, hooks, misconceptions, checks, activity, resources).
- `misconceptions.md` — the consolidated classic science misconceptions with *why* + *fix*.
- `resources.md` — curated, real, free resources (BBC Bitesize, Oak National Academy, BBC Teach, Mystery Science, Generation Genius, Crash Course Kids) at platform + exact topic title.

> Teaching order within a year is flexible, but keep the prerequisite chains: teach "what plants need" before "how water travels in a plant"; teach "light reflects / shadows" (Y3) before "how we see" (Y6); teach "simple series circuits" (Y4) before "voltage and brightness" (Y6).
