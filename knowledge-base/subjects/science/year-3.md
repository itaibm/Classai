# Year 3 Science (ages 7–8, KS2)

Real NC units: **Plants · Animals, including humans · Rocks · Light · Forces and magnets.** Year 3 begins KS2: systems start (how plants and skeletons work), and the first physics of light and forces. Working Scientifically focus: set up simple **fair tests**, make systematic observations and measurements, use results to draw conclusions.

## 1. The jobs of a plant's parts

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – plants

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 — Naming plant parts; Year 2 — How seeds grow.

### Key concepts
- Each plant part has a **function (job)**: roots anchor and absorb water; stem supports and transports; leaves make food using light; flowers make seeds.
- Plants need **light, water, air, room to grow, and nutrients from the soil** — but they make their own *food* in their leaves.
- Water travels **up** the stem from the roots to the leaves.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can name roots, stem, leaves, flower; knows plants need water and light.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Now we learn *what each part does*. **Roots** anchor the plant so it doesn't blow away, and they **absorb water and minerals** from the soil. The **stem** holds the plant up toward the light and acts like a set of straws **carrying water up** to the leaves and flowers. The **leaves** are the plant's food factory: they catch sunlight and use it to **make food** (sugar) — this is the big idea that plants *make* their own food, they don't eat it. The **flower** is where new **seeds** are made. Plants need **light, water, air, space and soil nutrients** to grow well.

**Worked example.** Stand a stick of white celery (or a white flower) in water coloured with food dye. After a few hours the coloured water has travelled **up** the stem and you can see it in the leaves/petals — proving the stem carries water up from the "roots" end to the top.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A sunflower turning to face the Sun — leaves chasing light to make food.
- A houseplant that flops when unwatered — roots couldn't supply water.
- Cut flowers in a vase drinking up the water over days.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Plants get their food from the soil."
  **Why:** we feed plants by putting them in soil, so soil must be food.
  **Fix:** plants **make** food in their leaves using light; from soil they take **water and minerals**, not food. (See `misconceptions.md`.)
- **Wrong idea:** "Water goes down the stem to the roots."
  **Why:** water usually falls downward.
  **Fix:** roots drink it from below and the stem carries it **up** to the leaves — the celery dye test shows this.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is the job of a plant's roots? **A:** Anchor the plant and absorb water and minerals.
- **Q:** Where does a plant make its food? **A:** In its leaves, using light.
- **Q:** Which way does water travel in the stem — up or down? **A:** Up, from roots to leaves.

### Activity / practice
The **celery/white-flower dye experiment** (above) — observe the coloured water rise. Safe: just water, dye and a plant. Classai block: **matchPairs** plant part → its function.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *The parts of a plant and their functions*** (roots, stem, leaves, flower jobs).
- **Generation Genius — *Plant Parts* / *Photosynthesis (intro)*** (animated explainers, topic level).

## 2. Water, pollination and seed dispersal

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – plants

**Prerequisites:** The jobs of a plant's parts.

### Key concepts
- The **life cycle of a flowering plant**: seed → germinate → grow → flower → pollination → seeds → spread.
- **Pollination** is moving pollen from flower to flower, often by insects like bees.
- **Seed dispersal** spreads seeds away from the parent (by wind, water, animals, or popping).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows flowers make seeds and seeds grow into plants.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Flowering plants have a **life cycle**. A seed germinates and grows into a plant, which makes **flowers**. For seeds to form, **pollen** must travel from one flower to another — this is **pollination**, and it's often done by **bees and other insects** that brush pollen onto themselves while drinking nectar (wind pollinates some plants too). Once pollinated, the flower makes **seeds**. Then the plant must **spread (disperse)** its seeds so the new plants don't all crowd the parent: **wind** carries light fluffy seeds (dandelion), **animals** carry sticky burrs or eat fruit and drop the seeds, **water** floats some seeds, and some seed pods **pop** and fling seeds out.

**Worked example.** A dandelion: bees and the wind pollinate the yellow flower → it makes seeds → the flower turns into a fluffy "clock" → the **wind** blows the parachute seeds far away, where they land and grow into new dandelions. That's wind dispersal completing the life cycle.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Blowing a dandelion clock — that's literally seed dispersal.
- Sticky "cleaver" seeds (burrs) that catch on socks and dogs.
- Why bees matter for the fruit we eat (no pollination, no apples).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Bees make seeds."
  **Why:** bees are involved in pollination.
  **Fix:** bees *carry pollen* between flowers; the **plant** makes the seeds after pollination.
- **Wrong idea:** "Seeds dispersing is the same as germinating."
  **Why:** both happen to seeds.
  **Fix:** dispersal = the seed **travelling away**; germination = the seed **starting to grow**. Different stages.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does pollination mean? **A:** Moving pollen from one flower to another.
- **Q:** Name one way seeds are dispersed. **A:** Wind / animals / water / popping pods.
- **Q:** Put in order: flower, seed, germinate, grow. **A:** Germinate → grow → flower → seed.

### Activity / practice
**Seed hunt / sort:** collect seeds (or pictures) and sort them by *how they're dispersed* — light & fluffy (wind), sticky (animal), inside fruit (animal). Classai block: **ordering** the plant life cycle; **categorize** seeds by dispersal method.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Plant life cycles, pollination and seed dispersal*** (full cycle).
- **Mystery Science — *How do plants make seeds / spread seeds?*** (pollination & dispersal lessons).

## 3. Nutrition, skeletons and muscles

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Biology – animals including humans

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 — Animals' needs and staying healthy.

### Key concepts
- Animals (including humans) **cannot make their own food** — they must eat it (unlike plants).
- A **balanced diet** gives the right amounts of different food types for energy, growth and health.
- Humans and many animals have a **skeleton** (for support, protection and movement) and **muscles** that pull on bones to move them.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows we eat to live; has heard of bones and muscles.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Unlike plants, **animals can't make their own food** — they have to eat plants or other animals to get the nutrition they need. The right **balanced diet** has the correct amounts of different foods: carbohydrates and fats for **energy**, protein for **growth and repair**, plus fruit and vegetables for **vitamins and minerals**, and water. Humans (and other vertebrates) have a **skeleton** of bones inside the body that does three jobs: it **supports** us (holds us up), **protects** soft parts (the skull guards the brain, the ribs guard the heart and lungs), and helps us **move**. We move because **muscles** are attached to bones; a muscle can only **pull**, so muscles work in **pairs** — one pulls the bone one way, its partner pulls it back.

**Worked example.** Bend your arm. The **biceps** muscle on top shortens and **pulls** your forearm up. To straighten the arm again, the biceps relaxes and the **triceps** underneath pulls it back down. Muscles pull (never push), so they come in pairs — that's why you have a muscle on each side of your arm.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- An athlete's diet — why footballers eat carbs before a match (energy).
- Feel your own jaw or arm muscles working as you chew or lift.
- Animals with no bones (a worm, a jellyfish) vs animals with bones (you, a dog).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Humans make their own food like plants."
  **Why:** "making energy" sounds similar.
  **Fix:** only plants make food (in leaves, from light); animals must **eat** food.
- **Wrong idea:** "Muscles push the bone."
  **Why:** movement looks like pushing.
  **Fix:** muscles only **pull** (by getting shorter); that's why they work in opposing pairs.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Why can't a human make its own food the way a plant does? **A:** Animals can't use light to make food; we must eat it.
- **Q:** Give two jobs of the skeleton. **A:** Support, protection, movement (any two).
- **Q:** Why do muscles work in pairs? **A:** A muscle can only pull, so a partner is needed to pull the bone back.

### Activity / practice
**Feel the pair:** bend and straighten your arm and feel the biceps and triceps take turns. Or sort foods into a balanced-plate diagram. Classai block: **categorize** foods by type; **matchPairs** bone → what it protects.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Skeletons and muscles*** (support, protection, movement; muscle pairs).
- **Generation Genius — *Muscular and Skeletal Systems*** (animated, topic level).

## 4. Rocks, fossils and soils

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Geology / Earth science

**Prerequisites:** none (links to Year 1 materials).

### Key concepts
- Rocks can be **grouped by their properties** (hard/soft, does water soak in, grains or crystals).
- **Fossils** are the preserved remains or shapes of living things from long ago, found in rock.
- **Soil** is made from tiny bits of broken-down rock mixed with rotted plants and animals.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has handled stones and pebbles; knows what living things are.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Rocks** are natural solid materials, and they're not all the same — we **group** them by properties we can test: how **hard** they are, whether water **soaks in** (permeable) or runs off, and whether they're made of grains or crystals. Some rocks are soft and crumbly (chalk), some very hard (granite). **Fossils** form when a plant or animal dies and gets buried; over a very long time the soft parts rot away and the hard parts (like bones or shells) slowly turn to rock, or leave a rock-shaped imprint. Fossils are how we know about dinosaurs. **Soil** isn't a rock — it's made from **tiny broken-down bits of rock** mixed with **rotted plants and animals** (called organic matter), plus air and water; that's why plants grow in it.

**Worked example.** A scientist finds an ammonite fossil (a coiled shell shape) in a cliff. They explain: an ammonite (a sea animal) died, sank into mud, was buried by more mud over millions of years, and its shell slowly turned to rock — leaving the spiral shape we see today. The fossil tells us this creature lived long ago, and that this rock was once under the sea.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A pebble beach — sort stones by how smooth, heavy or hard they are.
- Dinosaur fossils in a museum — how they got there.
- Digging in the garden — what's soil actually made of?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "A fossil is the actual bone/animal."
  **Why:** fossils look like bones.
  **Fix:** usually the original has slowly turned **to rock** (or left a rock imprint) over millions of years.
- **Wrong idea:** "Soil is just dirt — it's not made of rock."
  **Why:** soil looks nothing like a rock.
  **Fix:** soil is mostly **tiny broken-up rock** plus rotted plants and animals.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name one property you could use to group rocks. **A:** Hardness (or: does water soak in, grains/crystals).
- **Q:** How does a fossil form? **A:** A buried living thing's hard parts slowly turn to rock over a very long time.
- **Q:** What is soil made from? **A:** Tiny bits of broken rock plus rotted plants and animals.

### Activity / practice
**Rock sort:** collect 4–5 stones and group them by hardness, colour and whether water soaks in (drip water on each). Classai block: **categorize** rocks by property; **ordering** the steps of fossil formation.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Rocks, fossils and soils*** (grouping rocks; how fossils form; soil).
- **Generation Genius — *Rock Cycle / Fossils*** (animated, topic level).

## 5. Light, dark and shadows

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Physics – light

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- We need **light to see**; there is **no light in total darkness**, so we can't see anything.
- Light comes from **sources** (the Sun, lamps, flames, screens). Dark is just the **absence** of light.
- A **shadow** forms when an **opaque** object **blocks** light; shadows change as the light or object moves.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has noticed shadows and that we can't see in the dark.

### Teach it (the explanation)
We can only see when there is **light**. In **complete darkness** there is no light at all, so we see nothing — your eyes work, but there's nothing for them to use. Light comes from **light sources**: the **Sun** (our biggest, never look straight at it), lamps, torches, candles and screens. **Dark is not a thing in itself** — it's simply where there's no light. A **shadow** appears when something **opaque** (you can't see through it) gets in the way of the light and **blocks** it, leaving a dark patch the same shape as the object behind it. Move the object or the light, and the shadow changes size and position.

**Worked example.** Shine a torch at a wall and hold your hand in between. Your hand is opaque, so it **blocks** the light and a hand-shaped **shadow** appears on the wall. Move your hand **closer to the torch** and the shadow gets **bigger**; move it closer to the wall and the shadow gets **smaller and sharper**. The shadow is where your hand stopped the light reaching the wall.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Hand-shadow puppets on a bedroom wall.
- Your own shadow outside — short at midday, long in the evening.
- Sun safety: a hat and shade block the Sun's strong light (never look at the Sun).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Dark is a thing that fills the room when the light goes off."
  **Why:** darkness feels like it "comes in."
  **Fix:** dark is just the **absence of light** — nothing comes in; light simply leaves.
- **Wrong idea:** "A shadow is a reflection of the object."
  **Why:** both relate to the object and light.
  **Fix:** a shadow is where light is **blocked** (a dark gap), not a bounced image.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Can you see in complete darkness? Why? **A:** No — there's no light for your eyes to use.
- **Q:** What makes a shadow form? **A:** An opaque object blocking the light.
- **Q:** You move your hand closer to the torch. Does the shadow get bigger or smaller? **A:** Bigger.

### Activity / practice
**Shadow puppet test:** with a torch and wall, change the distance and watch the shadow grow or shrink — a fair test (change distance, keep torch the same). Classai block: **multipleChoice** light-source vs not; **ordering** shadow size vs distance.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Light and shadows*** (sources, darkness, shadow formation).
- **Mystery Science — *How do we make a shadow bigger?* / Light lessons** (shadow investigations).

## 6. How light reflects (and Sun safety)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Physics – light

**Prerequisites:** Light, dark and shadows.

### Key concepts
- Light **bounces off** surfaces — this is **reflection**.
- Smooth, shiny surfaces (mirrors, water, polished metal) reflect light best.
- The **Sun's light is dangerous to look at**; protect eyes and skin.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows light comes from sources and travels to surfaces.

### Teach it (the explanation)
When light hits a surface, some of it **bounces off** — we call this **reflection**. Smooth, shiny surfaces like **mirrors, still water and polished metal** reflect light cleanly, which is why you can see your face in them. Dull, rough surfaces reflect light too, but scatter it in all directions, so you don't see a clear image. Reflection is useful: bike reflectors and high-vis jackets bounce car light back so drivers see you in the dark. **Important safety:** the **Sun** is so bright that you must **never look straight at it**, even with sunglasses — it can damage your eyes. Protect your skin and eyes in strong sun with a hat, sunglasses and shade.

**Worked example.** In a dark room, shine a torch onto a mirror angled toward the ceiling. The light **bounces off** the mirror and lights up a different spot — you've reflected the light around a corner. Swap the mirror for a piece of black card and the light is absorbed, not reflected: no bright spot. Shiny reflects; dull absorbs.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A cyclist's reflector or trainers that "light up" in car headlights.
- Catching sunlight with a mirror to make a spot dance on the wall.
- Why we wear sunglasses and a hat at the beach.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Only mirrors reflect light."
  **Why:** mirrors give the clearest reflection.
  **Fix:** *all* surfaces reflect some light — that's how we see them; mirrors just do it neatly.
- **Wrong idea:** "Sunglasses make it safe to look at the Sun."
  **Why:** sunglasses cut brightness.
  **Fix:** **never** look at the Sun, even with sunglasses — it can still harm your eyes.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is reflection? **A:** Light bouncing off a surface.
- **Q:** Which reflects light best — a mirror or a piece of cloth? **A:** A mirror (smooth and shiny).
- **Q:** Is it safe to look at the Sun with sunglasses? **A:** No — never look at the Sun.

### Activity / practice
**Reflection test:** in a dim room, shine a torch on different surfaces (mirror, foil, paper, black card) and rank how well each reflects. Classai block: **categorize** surfaces as good/poor reflectors.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Reflection of light*** (reflecting surfaces; Sun safety).
- **Crash Course Kids (YouTube channel) — *Light* episodes** (reflection for KS2 level).

## 7. Forces: pushes, pulls and friction

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Physics – forces

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- A **force** is a **push** or a **pull**; forces can start, stop, speed up, slow down or change the direction of movement.
- **Contact forces** need to touch the object; **friction** is a force between two surfaces that rub.
- The roughness of a surface changes how much friction there is.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has pushed and pulled things and noticed things stop on their own.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **force** is simply a **push** or a **pull**. Forces can **start** something moving, **stop** it, make it go **faster** or **slower**, or change its **direction**. Many forces are **contact forces** — they only act when things touch (you push a door, you pull a wagon). **Friction** is a special force: when two surfaces **rub against each other**, friction **slows things down** and acts in the opposite direction to the movement. **Rough** surfaces (carpet, grass) make **more** friction; **smooth** surfaces (ice, polished wood) make **less**. Friction is why a rolling ball eventually stops, and why we can grip the floor with our shoes.

**Worked example.** Roll a toy car down the same ramp onto two floors: a smooth tile floor and a rough carpet. On the **tile** it rolls far (little friction). On the **carpet** it stops quickly (lots of friction). The only thing you changed was the surface — a **fair test** — so the surface (its roughness) must be what changed the distance.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Why football boots have studs (more grip = more friction on grass).
- Sliding on ice vs walking on a path (low vs high friction).
- A bike's brakes — rubber rubbing the wheel uses friction to stop.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Things stop moving because they run out of force/energy."
  **Why:** moving things slow down by themselves.
  **Fix:** they stop because **friction** acts against them — on near-frictionless ice they keep sliding.
- **Wrong idea:** "Friction is always bad."
  **Why:** friction slows things and wears them out.
  **Fix:** friction is also useful — it lets us walk, grip and brake.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What are the two kinds of force in this topic? **A:** Pushes and pulls.
- **Q:** Does a rough or smooth surface make more friction? **A:** Rough.
- **Q:** Why does a rolling ball stop on grass? **A:** Friction between the ball and grass slows it down.

### Activity / practice
**Friction ramp test:** roll a car down a ramp onto different surfaces and measure how far it travels; keep the ramp the same (fair test). Classai block: **numberEntry** for distances; **ordering** surfaces from most to least friction.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Forces and friction*** (pushes, pulls, friction on surfaces).
- **Generation Genius — *Forces and Friction*** (animated, topic level).

## 8. Magnets: attract, repel and magnetic materials

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Physics – forces & magnets

**Prerequisites:** Forces: pushes, pulls and friction.

### Key concepts
- A magnet is a **non-contact force** — it can pull (attract) some materials **without touching** them.
- Only some materials are **magnetic** (iron, steel, nickel); most are not (plastic, wood, paper, aluminium, copper).
- Magnets have two **poles** (N and S): **like poles repel**, **opposite poles attract**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows what a force is (push/pull) and has seen a fridge magnet.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **magnet** pulls certain materials toward it, and it can do this **without touching** them — that makes it a **non-contact force** (the only one we meet in Year 3). Only some materials are **magnetic**: iron, steel and nickel are attracted to magnets. Most everyday materials are **not** magnetic — plastic, wood, paper, glass, rubber, and even some metals like aluminium and copper. Every magnet has two ends called **poles**, a **north (N)** and a **south (S)**. When you bring two magnets together: **opposite poles attract** (N pulls to S), but **like poles repel** (N pushes away from N, S pushes away from S) — you can feel an invisible push.

**Worked example.** Test objects with a magnet: a steel paperclip **jumps to it** (magnetic), a plastic spoon, a copper coin and a wooden peg **do nothing** (not magnetic). Then take two bar magnets: hold N to S and they snap together (**attract**); flip one so it's N to N and you feel them **push apart** (**repel**) before they even touch — that's the non-contact force.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Fridge magnets — why they stick to the (steel) fridge but not the (wooden) table.
- A magnet "fishing game" picking up metal paperclips.
- A compass needle (a magnet) pointing north.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "All metals are magnetic."
  **Why:** magnets stick to many metal things.
  **Fix:** only iron, steel and nickel are magnetic — copper coins and aluminium foil are **not** attracted. Test them.
- **Wrong idea:** "Magnets always pull together."
  **Why:** children mostly experience attraction.
  **Fix:** **like poles repel** — turn one magnet around and it pushes away.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name a material that is magnetic. **A:** Iron / steel / nickel.
- **Q:** Are all metals magnetic? **A:** No — e.g. copper and aluminium are not.
- **Q:** What happens when you bring two north poles together? **A:** They repel (push apart).

### Activity / practice
**Magnetic or not?** Test 6 household objects with a magnet and sort into magnetic / not magnetic; predict first (predict→check). Then explore attract/repel with two magnets. Classai block: **categorize** materials magnetic/not; **multipleChoice** attract vs repel.

- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Magnets and magnetic materials*** (poles, attract/repel, magnetic materials).
- **Generation Genius — *Magnets / Magnetic Force*** (animated, topic level).
