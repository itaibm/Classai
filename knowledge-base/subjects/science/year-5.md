## 1. Life cycles of animals

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Biology – living things & habitats

**Prerequisites:** Year 2 — offspring; Year 3 — plant life cycle.

### Key concepts
- Different animals have different **life cycles**: mammal, amphibian, insect and bird cycles differ.
- Some insects and amphibians go through **metamorphosis** — a dramatic body change as they grow.
- **Reproduction** can be **sexual** (two parents) or, in some plants/animals, **asexual** (one parent).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows animals have young that grow into adults; knows the plant life cycle.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Animals grow through a **life cycle** from birth to adult to having young of their own — but different animal groups do it differently. A **mammal** (like a dog or human) gives birth to live young that look like small adults and feed on milk. A **bird** lays eggs, which hatch into chicks that grow into adults. An **amphibian** like a frog goes through **metamorphosis**: egg (spawn) → tadpole (lives in water, has a tail, breathes with gills) → froglet → adult frog (lives on land, has legs, breathes air) — the body completely changes. An **insect** like a butterfly also undergoes metamorphosis: egg → larva (caterpillar) → pupa (chrysalis) → adult (butterfly). New living things are made by **reproduction**: usually **sexual** (needing two parents, so offspring vary), but some plants and simple animals can reproduce **asexually** from one parent (making copies).

**Worked example.** A frog's life cycle. Frogspawn (jelly + eggs) hatches into a **tadpole** with a tail and gills, swimming in the pond. Over weeks it grows back legs, then front legs, its tail shrinks, and its gills change to lungs — it becomes a **froglet**, then a land-living **adult frog** that returns to the pond to lay its own spawn. The animal changes shape so much that the baby and adult look like different creatures — that's **metamorphosis**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Raising tadpoles in a tank (or watching a pond) through spring.
- A butterfly garden or caterpillar kit.
- Comparing a human baby growing up to a frog's dramatic change.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "A caterpillar and a butterfly are different animals."
  **Why:** they look nothing alike.
  **Fix:** it's the **same animal** at different life-cycle stages — metamorphosis transforms it.
- **Wrong idea:** "All animals lay eggs" (or "all animals give live birth").
  **Why:** child generalises from one familiar animal.
  **Fix:** mammals usually give live birth; birds, amphibians and insects lay eggs — different cycles.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is metamorphosis? **A:** A big change in body shape as an animal grows (e.g. tadpole → frog).
- **Q:** Put in order: tadpole, frog, frogspawn, froglet. **A:** Frogspawn → tadpole → froglet → frog.
- **Q:** Name an animal that goes through metamorphosis. **A:** Frog / butterfly / many insects.

### Activity / practice
**Compare two cycles:** draw the life cycle of a butterfly and a human side by side and note what's the same and different. Classai block: **ordering** life-cycle stages; **matchPairs** animal → type of cycle.

- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Life cycles of animals*** (mammal, amphibian, insect, bird; metamorphosis).

## 2. Reproduction in plants

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – living things & habitats

**Prerequisites:** Life cycles of animals; Year 3 — pollination.

### Key concepts
- Plants reproduce **sexually** (pollination → seeds) and some reproduce **asexually** (runners, bulbs, cuttings).
- The flower's parts have reproductive jobs (stamen makes pollen; the stigma/ovary receive it and make seeds).
- After pollination and **fertilisation**, seeds form and are dispersed (link to Year 3).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows pollination moves pollen and seeds are dispersed.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Plants make new plants in two ways. **Sexual reproduction** uses the **flower**: the **stamen** makes **pollen**, which is carried (by insects or wind) to another flower's **stigma**; the pollen then fertilises an egg in the **ovary**, and a **seed** forms. Because two parents are involved, the new plants vary. Some plants also reproduce **asexually** — from just one parent, making copies: strawberry plants send out **runners** that root nearby, daffodils grow new **bulbs**, and gardeners take **cuttings** of a stem that grow into a whole new plant. Spider plants and potatoes do this too.

**Worked example.** A strawberry plant reproduces two ways. In summer its **flower** is pollinated by bees → seeds form in the strawberry fruit (**sexual**). The same plant also sends out **runners** — long stems that touch the soil and grow roots, making a new strawberry plant that's a copy of the parent (**asexual**). One plant, two routes to making more.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Growing a new plant from a cutting on a windowsill (e.g. mint, spider plant).
- Strawberry runners or a sprouting potato in the cupboard.
- Bees in a garden doing the pollinating.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Plants can only grow from seeds."
  **Why:** seeds are the familiar way.
  **Fix:** many plants also reproduce asexually — runners, bulbs and cuttings make new plants without seeds.
- **Wrong idea:** "Pollination and fertilisation are the same thing."
  **Why:** they happen together.
  **Fix:** pollination = pollen **arriving** at the stigma; fertilisation = pollen then **joining** the egg to start a seed.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name the flower part that makes pollen. **A:** The stamen.
- **Q:** Give one way a plant reproduces asexually. **A:** Runners / bulbs / cuttings.
- **Q:** What forms after a flower is pollinated and fertilised? **A:** A seed (in the fruit).

### Activity / practice
**Grow a cutting:** put a mint or spider-plant cutting in water and watch roots grow (asexual reproduction over time). Classai block: **categorize** examples sexual/asexual; **matchPairs** flower part → job.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Reproduction in plants*** (sexual via flowers; asexual via runners/bulbs).
- **Generation Genius — *Plant Reproduction*** (animated, topic level).

## 3. Growing up: humans from birth to old age

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Biology – animals including humans

**Prerequisites:** Life cycles of animals.

### Key concepts
- Humans grow and change through **life stages**: baby → child → teenager → adult → old age.
- We change in **size and ability** at each stage; growth is fastest in babyhood and the teenage years.
- A **gestation** period (development before birth) varies between animals.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows people grow from babies to adults; knows animal life cycles.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Humans, like all mammals, change through their lives in a set of **stages**. A **baby** is small and helpless, learning to sit, crawl and walk. A **child** grows taller, learns to talk, read and do lots of things. A **teenager (adolescent)** goes through fast growth and changes on the way to becoming an adult. An **adult** is fully grown and may have children of their own. In **old age** the body slows down. Growth isn't steady — it's fastest in the first couple of years and again in the teenage years. Different mammals develop for different lengths of time before birth (the **gestation** period): a mouse only a few weeks, a human about nine months, an elephant nearly two years.

**Worked example.** Order a human life: a newborn **baby** (can't walk or talk) → a **child** (running, reading) → a **teenager** (growing fast, becoming independent) → an **adult** (fully grown) → **old age**. Compare gestation: a human grows for about 9 months before birth, but an elephant grows for around 22 months — bigger animals often take longer.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A family photo timeline — baby, child, grown-up.
- How long different baby animals stay with their parents.
- Why a baby can't do what an older child can — and what changes.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "People grow at the same steady rate every year."
  **Why:** growth seems gradual.
  **Fix:** growth has **fast bursts** (babyhood and the teenage years) and slower times.
- **Wrong idea:** "All baby animals are ready before birth at the same time."
  **Why:** birth seems like one fixed event.
  **Fix:** gestation varies a lot — weeks for a mouse, ~9 months for a human, ~22 for an elephant.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Put in order: adult, baby, teenager, child. **A:** Baby → child → teenager → adult.
- **Q:** During which stages is human growth fastest? **A:** Babyhood and the teenage years.
- **Q:** What does "gestation" mean? **A:** The time a baby develops before being born.

### Activity / practice
**Life-stage timeline:** make a timeline of human stages with one thing a person can do at each. Classai block: **ordering** life stages; **matchPairs** animal → gestation length.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *The human life cycle / growing up*** (stages from birth to old age).
- **BBC Teach — *Growing and changing* (KS2)** (short clips on human development).

## 4. Properties of materials and separating mixtures

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Chemistry – materials

**Prerequisites:** Year 1–2 materials; Year 4 — states of matter.

### Key concepts
- Materials can be **compared and grouped** by properties: hardness, transparency, conductivity (electrical & thermal), magnetism, solubility.
- Some solids **dissolve** in water to form a **solution**; we can recover them and separate mixtures by **filtering, sieving and evaporating**.
- The right separation method depends on the properties of the mixture.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows about properties, conductors/insulators, and states of matter.

### Teach it (the explanation)
We can **compare and group materials** using many properties: how **hard** they are, whether they're **transparent**, whether they conduct **electricity** or **heat**, whether they're **magnetic**, and whether they **dissolve** in water (**solubility**). When a solid like salt or sugar **dissolves** in water, it seems to disappear but it's still there — it has made a **solution** (and you can get it back). To separate mixtures we use the property differences: **sieving** separates big bits from small (stones from sand); **filtering** separates an undissolved solid from a liquid (sand from water, using filter paper); **evaporating** the water recovers a dissolved solid (boil off the water and salt is left behind). The clever bit is choosing the method that matches the mixture.

**Worked example.** You have salty water and want the salt back. The salt has **dissolved**, so filtering won't catch it. Instead, **evaporate**: gently heat the salty water so the **water turns to vapour and leaves**, and the **salt is left behind** as crystals. (An adult handles any heating.) Now you have separated the mixture using the salt's property of being left behind when water evaporates.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Stirring sugar into a drink — it dissolves but it's still there (it tastes sweet).
- Sea salt made by evaporating seawater.
- Filtering muddy water through a coffee filter.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "When sugar dissolves, it disappears / is gone."
  **Why:** you can't see it any more.
  **Fix:** it's still there in the **solution** (you can taste it, and evaporating the water brings it back).
- **Wrong idea:** "You can filter salt out of salty water."
  **Why:** filtering separates solids from liquids.
  **Fix:** dissolved salt passes through the filter — you must **evaporate** the water to recover it.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is it called when a solid mixes into water and seems to vanish? **A:** It dissolves (making a solution).
- **Q:** How would you get dissolved salt back from salty water? **A:** Evaporate the water (the salt is left behind).
- **Q:** Which method separates sand from water? **A:** Filtering.

### Activity / practice
**Salt-recovery investigation:** dissolve a little salt in warm water, then leave it on a saucer in a warm spot (or an adult gently evaporates it) until salt crystals appear. Classai block: **matchPairs** mixture → separation method; **categorize** materials by property.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Dissolving, mixtures and separating*** (solutions; filtering, sieving, evaporating).
- **Generation Genius — *Mixtures and Solutions*** (animated, topic level).

## 5. Reversible and irreversible changes

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Chemistry – changes of materials

**Prerequisites:** Properties of materials and separating mixtures; Year 4 — states of matter.

### Key concepts
- A **reversible change** can be undone — you still have the same material (melting, freezing, dissolving, mixing).
- An **irreversible change** makes a **new material** and usually **can't be undone** (burning, cooking, rusting, mixing some chemicals).
- Some irreversible changes give off **gas, heat or light** — clues that a new substance has formed.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows changes of state and dissolving (all reversible).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Changes come in two kinds. A **reversible change** can be undone and you still have the **same material**: melting and freezing (ice ↔ water), dissolving and evaporating (salt ↔ salty water), and simply mixing things you could separate again. An **irreversible change** makes a **brand-new material** and usually **can't be reversed**: **burning** wood makes ash and smoke (you can't get the wood back), **cooking** an egg sets it solid for good, **rusting** turns iron into orange iron oxide, and mixing some chemicals (like vinegar and bicarbonate of soda) fizzes into something new. A useful clue that a new material has formed is **bubbles of gas, heat, light, or a colour change**.

**Worked example.** Mix **bicarbonate of soda** with **vinegar** in a cup. It **fizzes** and bubbles — a **gas** (carbon dioxide) is being made, which is the clue that a **new material** has formed. You **can't** turn it back into the original vinegar and powder, so this is an **irreversible** change. Compare: melt an ice cube and re-freeze it — same water, fully reversible.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- The classic fizzing "volcano" (vinegar + bicarb) — safe and dramatic.
- Cooking: a cake or fried egg can't be turned back into batter or raw egg.
- A rusty bike chain or gate.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "All changes can be undone if you try hard enough."
  **Why:** melting/freezing seem to undo easily, so children over-generalise.
  **Fix:** burning, cooking and rusting make **new materials** that **can't** be turned back.
- **Wrong idea:** "Mixing always makes a new material."
  **Why:** mixing changes how things look.
  **Fix:** mixing salt and water is **reversible** (no new material) — only some mixtures react to make something new.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Is melting ice a reversible or irreversible change? **A:** Reversible (it's still water).
- **Q:** Is burning paper reversible? Why? **A:** No — it makes new materials (ash, smoke) you can't turn back.
- **Q:** What clue tells you a new material may have formed? **A:** Gas/bubbles, heat, light, or a colour change.

### Activity / practice
**Reversible vs not:** with a parent, (1) melt then re-freeze an ice cube (reversible), (2) mix vinegar + bicarb and watch it fizz (irreversible). Sort each change. Safe: no flames needed. Classai block: **categorize** changes reversible/irreversible.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Reversible and irreversible changes*** (with examples and clues).
- **Generation Genius — *Reversible and Irreversible Changes / Chemical Reactions*** (animated, topic level).

## 6. Earth and Space

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Physics / Earth & space

**Prerequisites:** Year 1 — seasons; Year 3 — light & shadows.

### Key concepts
- The Earth and the other planets **orbit the Sun**; the Sun is a **star** at the centre of our Solar System.
- The Earth, Sun and Moon are approximately **spherical** (ball-shaped), not flat discs.
- **Day and night** are caused by the **Earth spinning (rotating)** once a day — the Sun doesn't move across the sky; *we* turn.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Sun rises and sets and that there's a Moon; knows about light and shadows.

### Teach it (the explanation)
We live in the **Solar System**: the **Sun** (a star) sits in the middle, and **eight planets** — including Earth — travel around it in paths called **orbits**. The Earth, Sun and Moon are all roughly **spherical** (ball-shaped), even though the ground looks flat to us. The **Moon orbits the Earth** (about once a month) while the Earth orbits the Sun (about once a year). The big idea is **day and night**: the Earth **spins** (rotates) on its axis once every **24 hours**, so the side facing the Sun has **day** and the side facing away has **night**. The Sun only *seems* to move across the sky — really it stays put and **we** are turning. That's why shadows swing round and shorten/lengthen through the day.

**Worked example.** Use a torch (the Sun) and a globe with a sticker on your town (you). Shine the torch on one side — that side has **day**, the dark side has **night**. Slowly spin the globe; your sticker moves from the lit side into the dark and back. Nothing about the torch moved — the **Earth's spin** made day turn to night. So "the Sun goes down" really means "my part of the Earth has turned away from the Sun."

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Time zones: it's daytime here but night-time on the other side of the world — same Sun, different side facing it.
- Watching a shadow stick swing and change length over a day.
- Why we have day and night even though the Sun never switches off.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The Sun moves around the Earth / travels across the sky."
  **Why:** it really looks like the Sun rises, crosses the sky and sets.
  **Fix:** the **Earth spins** while the Sun stays put — our turning makes the Sun *appear* to move. (See `misconceptions.md`.)
- **Wrong idea:** "Night happens because the Sun goes somewhere / switches off."
  **Why:** the Sun disappears at sunset.
  **Fix:** the Sun is always shining; your side of the Earth has just **rotated away** from it.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is at the centre of our Solar System? **A:** The Sun (a star).
- **Q:** What causes day and night? **A:** The Earth spinning (rotating) once every 24 hours.
- **Q:** Does the Sun really move across the sky? **A:** No — the Earth turns; the Sun only appears to move.

### Activity / practice
**Globe-and-torch day/night model:** spin a globe by a torch and show how your town goes from day to night. Or track a **shadow stick** outside through the day. Classai block: **ordering** Sun/Earth/Moon facts; **multipleChoice** explaining day and night.

- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Science — *Earth and space: day and night, the Solar System*** (orbits; rotation; day/night).
- **Crash Course Kids (YouTube channel) — *Earth, Sun and Moon* episodes** (Solar System & day/night).

## 7. Forces: gravity, resistance and mechanisms

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Physics – forces

**Prerequisites:** Year 3 — forces & friction.

### Key concepts
- **Gravity** is a force that pulls objects toward the Earth (it's why things fall and have weight).
- **Air resistance, water resistance and friction** are forces that act **against** motion (drag).
- **Mechanisms** — **levers, pulleys and gears** — let a smaller force do a bigger job.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows forces are pushes/pulls and that friction slows things (Year 3).

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Gravity** is the force that pulls everything toward the Earth — it's why a dropped ball falls, why we stay on the ground, and what we feel as an object's **weight**. As things move, other forces push **against** them: **air resistance** (drag from the air — what slows a parachute), **water resistance** (drag from water — why it's hard to run in a pool), and **friction** (between surfaces). These resistive forces all act in the **opposite direction to movement**. Humans use clever **mechanisms** to make a small force do a big job: a **lever** (like a see-saw or a crowbar) lets you lift a heavy load with less effort; a **pulley** lets you lift a load by pulling down; **gears** change how fast or how forcefully wheels turn (like the gears on a bike).

**Worked example.** Drop two identical sheets of paper, but **scrunch one into a tight ball**. The ball falls **fast and straight**; the flat sheet **floats down slowly**, drifting. Gravity pulls both down equally, but the flat sheet has much more **air resistance** (more surface catching the air), so it's slowed more. Same paper, same gravity — the only difference is the **air resistance**, which is what made one fall slower. (This also shows why "heavier falls faster" is wrong — it's about air resistance, not weight.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A parachute or sycamore "helicopter" seed using air resistance.
- A bike's gears making a hill easier; a see-saw lifting a heavier friend.
- Why a streamlined swimmer or boat cuts through water resistance.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Heavier objects always fall faster than light ones."
  **Why:** a stone clearly beats a feather to the ground.
  **Fix:** that's **air resistance**, not weight — without air, both fall together; gravity pulls equally. (See `misconceptions.md`.)
- **Wrong idea:** "Gravity needs air to work" / "there's no gravity in space."
  **Why:** astronauts float, so "no gravity."
  **Fix:** gravity works through empty space; astronauts float because they're **falling around** the Earth, not because gravity is gone.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What force pulls a dropped ball to the ground? **A:** Gravity.
- **Q:** Why does a flat sheet of paper fall more slowly than a scrunched one? **A:** It has more air resistance.
- **Q:** Name a mechanism that helps lift a heavy load with less force. **A:** A lever (or pulley, or gears).

### Activity / practice
**Parachute / paper-drop fair test:** drop a flat vs scrunched sheet, then make a parachute and time its fall, changing one thing (canopy size). Measure with a timer. Classai block: **numberEntry** for fall times; **matchPairs** force → effect.
