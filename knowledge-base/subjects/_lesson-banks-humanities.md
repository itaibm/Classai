# Lesson Banks — Humanities & Enrichment subjects

Ready-to-run **mini lesson outlines** for History, Geography, Spanish, Art, Music, Computing and Life Skills, each mapped to the Classai beat sequence **hook → explain → example → check → practice → recap**, plus a short **question bank** per subject (answer + one likely wrong answer, matching the app's `wrongAnswers` shape). These are scaffolds: the tutor personalises hooks to the learner's interests and adapts difficulty (gentle / standard / challenge).

Each lesson lists, per beat: the goal and the suggested Classai block. Display blocks (richText, slideshow, whiteboard, image, video, table, keyTerm, steps) are look-only; interactive blocks (multipleChoice, trueFalse, fillBlank, matchPairs, ordering, categorize, numberEntry, shortText, speak) are where the child *does* the thinking.

## HISTORY

### Lesson H1 — The Great Fire of London (1666)
- **Hook (slideshow):** "In 1666 a whole city burned for days — how could a single bakery start it?" 3 image-slides of Pudding Lane / flames / the river.
- **Explain (richText):** Houses were wooden, close together, and dry after a hot summer; fire began in Thomas Farriner's bakery and spread for ~4 days; ended by firebreaks and the wind dropping.
- **Example (whiteboard):** Map the spread street-by-street; mark where it started and how it jumped roof to roof.
- **Check (multipleChoice):** "Why did the fire spread so fast?" (wooden houses close together / it was raining / the river caught fire).
- **Practice (ordering):** Sequence the events: bakery catches → wind spreads it → houses pulled down for firebreaks → fire dies → city rebuilt in brick.
- **Recap (keyTerm):** *Firebreak* — a gap made to stop fire spreading. Link to today: smoke alarms, brick houses, the fire brigade.

### Lesson H2 — Sources: how do we know about the past?
- **Hook (richText):** "No cameras in Roman times — so how do we know anything?"
- **Explain (slideshow):** Historians use *sources* — objects, writing, buildings, pictures. *Primary* (made at the time) vs *secondary* (made later). Evidence can be incomplete or biased.
- **Example (image):** Examine a Roman coin/Samuel Pepys' diary entry — what does it tell us, what doesn't it?
- **Check (categorize):** Sort items into primary / secondary (a diary from 1666 vs a 2020 book about the Fire).
- **Practice (shortText):** "One thing this source tells us, and one thing it doesn't."
- **Recap:** Good historians ask *who made this, when, and why?*

### Lesson H3 — Ancient Egypt: why the Nile mattered
- **Hook (image):** Satellite photo — a green ribbon of life in a desert. "Why does everyone live along one river?"
- **Explain (richText):** The Nile flooded each year, leaving rich black soil for farming; it gave water, food, transport. "Egypt is the gift of the Nile."
- **Example (whiteboard):** Diagram flood → silt → crops → food surplus → time for building, writing, pyramids.
- **Check (trueFalse):** "The Nile's yearly flood helped farmers." (true).
- **Practice (multipleChoice):** "Why did Egyptian towns grow beside the Nile?" (water & fertile soil / it was cooler / gold in the water).
- **Recap:** Geography shapes history — rivers grow civilisations.

**History question bank**
1. Q: Where did the Great Fire of London start? A: A bakery in Pudding Lane. ✗ The Tower of London.
2. Q: Primary or secondary source — a diary written in 1666? A: Primary. ✗ Secondary.
3. Q: Why were 1666 London houses so flammable? A: Made of wood and packed close together. ✗ They were made of brick.
4. Q: Why was the Nile vital to Ancient Egypt? A: Flood left fertile soil for farming; gave water and transport. ✗ It was full of gold.
5. Q: What is a firebreak? A: A gap (e.g. pulling down houses) to stop fire spreading. ✗ A type of fire engine.
6. Q: What does a historian ask about a source? A: Who made it, when, and why. ✗ How much it costs today.
7. Q: Were the pyramids built before or after farming surplus freed up workers? A: After — surplus food gave time to build. ✗ Before farming existed.
8. Q: Give one limit of a single source. A: It may be incomplete or biased / one viewpoint. ✗ Sources are always completely true.

## GEOGRAPHY

### Lesson G1 — The water cycle
- **Hook (whiteboard, animate):** "The water you drank today might be older than the dinosaurs — where has it been?"
- **Explain (slideshow):** Evaporation (sun heats water → vapour) → condensation (cools → clouds) → precipitation (rain/snow) → collection (rivers/sea) → repeat.
- **Example (whiteboard):** Draw and label the cycle with arrows; show it never ends.
- **Check (fillBlank):** "Water turns to vapour by ____ and forms clouds by ____." (evaporation / condensation).
- **Practice (ordering):** Sequence the four stages from a puddle to rain.
- **Recap (keyTerm):** *Precipitation* — water falling as rain, snow, sleet or hail.

### Lesson G2 — Reading a map (grid references & compass)
- **Hook (image):** A treasure map. "How do we say *exactly* where the treasure is?"
- **Explain (richText):** Maps use a grid of numbered lines; a 4-figure reference gives a square (along the corridor, up the stairs — eastings then northings). A compass gives direction (N, E, S, W).
- **Example (whiteboard):** Find square (3,2) together; "go along 3, up 2."
- **Check (multipleChoice):** "To read a grid reference you go…" (along then up / up then along / diagonally).
- **Practice (shortText/numberEntry):** Give the grid reference of a marked symbol.
- **Recap:** "Along the corridor, then up the stairs" + the compass rose.

### Lesson G3 — Volcanoes: why mountains erupt
- **Hook (video or slideshow):** Footage/images of an eruption. "What's coming out, and from where?"
- **Explain (whiteboard):** Earth's crust sits on plates; where they meet, molten rock (*magma*) rises; when it erupts it's *lava*; cools into new rock. Volcanoes often line plate edges (Ring of Fire).
- **Example (image):** Cross-section of a volcano — magma chamber, vent, crater.
- **Check (trueFalse):** "Molten rock is called magma underground and lava once it erupts." (true).
- **Practice (matchPairs):** Match terms — magma / lava / crater / vent — to meanings.
- **Recap:** People still live near volcanoes for fertile soil — risk vs benefit.

**Geography question bank**
1. Q: Name the stage where water vapour cools into clouds. A: Condensation. ✗ Evaporation.
2. Q: In a 4-figure grid reference, which number first? A: Eastings (along) then northings (up). A2 ✗ Up then along.
3. Q: What's molten rock called once it erupts? A: Lava. ✗ Magma (that's underground).
4. Q: The 4 compass points clockwise from North? A: North, East, South, West. ✗ North, West, South, East.
5. Q: Why is the water cycle never-ending? A: Water is recycled — evaporates, condenses, falls, collects, repeats. ✗ New water is made each time.
6. Q: Why do many volcanoes occur in lines (e.g. Ring of Fire)? A: Along edges where tectonic plates meet. ✗ Randomly anywhere.
7. Q: What is precipitation? A: Water falling as rain/snow/sleet/hail. ✗ Water turning into vapour.
8. Q: One reason people live near volcanoes? A: Fertile soil / minerals / tourism. ✗ Volcanoes never erupt.

## SPANISH (speaking-focused — use the `speak` block heavily)

### Lesson S1 — Greetings & introducing yourself
- **Hook (speak):** Avatar: "¡Hola! Me llamo Nova. ¿Y tú?" Child copies "Hola".
- **Explain (keyTerm):** *Hola* (OH-lah) = hello; *Me llamo…* (meh YAH-moh) = my name is; *¿Cómo te llamas?* = what's your name?
- **Example (slideshow):** Model a 3-line dialogue: Hola → ¿Cómo te llamas? → Me llamo ___.
- **Check (speak):** Child answers "¿Cómo te llamas?" aloud.
- **Practice (matchPairs):** Match Spanish ↔ English greetings (hola, adiós, gracias, buenos días).
- **Recap (speak):** Whole greeting exchanged once more; recycle next session (spacing).

### Lesson S2 — Numbers 0–10
- **Hook (speak):** Count fingers in Spanish together: uno, dos, tres…
- **Explain (slideshow):** 0–10 with pronunciation hints (uno OO-noh, dos, tres, cuatro KWAH-troh, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez).
- **Example (whiteboard):** Point to numerals, say each; clap the count.
- **Check (multipleChoice):** "Which is *siete*?" (7 / 6 / 9).
- **Practice (speak):** Say your age — "Tengo ___ años."
- **Recap (matchPairs):** Numeral ↔ Spanish word.

### Lesson S3 — Colours & describing
- **Hook (image):** A rainbow. "Let's name these colours in Spanish."
- **Explain (keyTerm):** rojo (red), azul (blue), verde (green), amarillo (yellow), negro, blanco; note colour comes *after* the noun in Spanish (*la casa roja*).
- **Example (slideshow):** Objects + colour: "el coche rojo, el pez azul."
- **Check (fillBlank):** "el sol amarillo means the ____ sun." (yellow).
- **Practice (speak):** Describe an object: "El gato es negro."
- **Recap:** Colours follow the noun — *una flor roja*, not *roja flor*.

**Spanish question bank**
1. Q: How do you say "my name is Sam"? A: Me llamo Sam. ✗ Yo soy llamo Sam.
2. Q: What is "gracias"? A: Thank you. ✗ Goodbye.
3. Q: Which number is "cinco"? A: 5. ✗ 4.
4. Q: Say "blue" in Spanish. A: Azul. ✗ Verde (that's green).
5. Q: Where does the colour word go — "the red car"? A: After the noun: el coche rojo. ✗ Before: el rojo coche.
6. Q: "¿Cómo te llamas?" means…? A: What's your name? ✗ How are you?
7. Q: Say "I am 9 years old." A: Tengo nueve años. ✗ Soy nueve años.
8. Q: What is "adiós"? A: Goodbye. ✗ Hello.

## ART

### Lesson A1 — The colour wheel & mixing
- **Hook (image):** A colour wheel. "With just 3 colours you can make almost any other — which 3?"
- **Explain (richText):** Primary = red, yellow, blue (can't be mixed from others). Mix two primaries → secondary: red+yellow=orange, yellow+blue=green, blue+red=purple.
- **Example (whiteboard):** Show the mixes as overlapping circles.
- **Check (multipleChoice):** "Yellow + blue = ?" (green / orange / purple).
- **Practice (matchPairs):** Match each secondary to the two primaries that make it.
- **Recap (keyTerm):** *Primary colours* — the three you mix from. (Real practice: mix paints at home.)

### Lesson A2 — Observational drawing (drawing what you see)
- **Hook (image):** Two drawings of a hand — one "from memory" (symbol), one "from looking". "Why does the second look real?"
- **Explain (richText):** Artists draw what they *see*, not what they *think* — slow looking, follow the outline, notice shapes between things (negative space).
- **Example (whiteboard):** Model contour-drawing an apple slowly.
- **Check (trueFalse):** "Good observational drawing means drawing what you actually see." (true).
- **Practice (shortText/offline):** Child draws a real object for 3 minutes, eyes mostly on the object.
- **Recap:** Look more than you draw; sketchbooks are for trying, not perfection.

### Lesson A3 — Studying an artist: Kandinsky (shape, colour, feeling)
- **Hook (image):** Kandinsky's concentric circles. "How does this picture make you feel — and why?"
- **Explain (slideshow):** Kandinsky linked colours and shapes to feelings and even music; abstract art doesn't have to show real things.
- **Example (whiteboard):** Pick a feeling → choose colours/shapes for it.
- **Check (multipleChoice):** "Abstract art must show real objects." (false / true).
- **Practice (offline):** Make a "feelings" pattern of circles in chosen colours.
- **Recap:** Art can express feelings, not just copy the world.

**Art question bank**
1. Q: Name the three primary colours. A: Red, yellow, blue. ✗ Green, orange, purple.
2. Q: Blue + yellow = ? A: Green. ✗ Purple.
3. Q: What is observational drawing? A: Drawing what you actually see. ✗ Drawing from imagination only.
4. Q: Does abstract art have to show real objects? A: No. ✗ Yes, always.
5. Q: Red + blue = ? A: Purple. ✗ Orange.
6. Q: What is "negative space"? A: The shapes/space around and between objects. ✗ A drawing mistake.
7. Q: A sketchbook is for…? A: Trying ideas and practising (not just finished work). ✗ Only perfect drawings.
8. Q: Name a secondary colour. A: Orange, green or purple. ✗ Red.

## MUSIC

### Lesson M1 — Pulse vs rhythm
- **Hook (speak/clap):** "March on the spot — that steady beat is the *pulse*. Now clap the words of your name — that's *rhythm*."
- **Explain (richText):** Pulse = the steady heartbeat of music (unchanging). Rhythm = the pattern of long and short sounds *over* the pulse.
- **Example (whiteboard):** Show pulse as even dots; rhythm as words ("ap-ple, ba-na-na") above them.
- **Check (trueFalse):** "The pulse stays steady while rhythm changes." (true).
- **Practice (speak/clap):** Keep a foot-tap pulse while clapping a name rhythm.
- **Recap (keyTerm):** *Pulse* — the steady beat; *rhythm* — the pattern on top.

### Lesson M2 — Pitch (high and low)
- **Hook (speak):** Sing a high "wheee" and a low "boom". "Which animal sounds high? Which low?"
- **Explain (slideshow):** Pitch = how high or low a sound is. High ≠ loud (a mouse can squeak quietly-high; a drum booms loud-low).
- **Example (whiteboard):** Draw pitch going up/down like a line as you sing.
- **Check (multipleChoice):** "A tiny bird's tweet is usually…" (high / low / loud).
- **Practice (speak):** Copy 3 pitches (high, low, high) sung by the avatar.
- **Recap:** High/low is *pitch*; loud/quiet is *dynamics* — different things.

### Lesson M3 — Dynamics & tempo (loud/quiet, fast/slow)
- **Hook (speak):** Say "hello" loud then whisper; fast then slow. "Same word — different feeling."
- **Explain (richText):** Dynamics = volume (loud/quiet, *forte/piano*). Tempo = speed (fast/slow). Composers change them for effect.
- **Example (slideshow):** Match music moods — a scary scene (loud, fast) vs a lullaby (quiet, slow).
- **Check (categorize):** Sort word-cards into Dynamics vs Tempo (loud, fast, quiet, slow).
- **Practice (speak/clap):** Perform a phrase quiet-and-slow, then loud-and-fast.
- **Recap:** Loud≠fast — dynamics and tempo are independent (common mix-up).

**Music question bank**
1. Q: What is the pulse in music? A: The steady, unchanging beat. ✗ The pattern of long/short sounds.
2. Q: What is rhythm? A: The pattern of long and short sounds over the pulse. ✗ How loud the music is.
3. Q: Does "high pitch" mean "loud"? A: No — pitch is high/low, loudness is dynamics. ✗ Yes.
4. Q: What does dynamics mean? A: How loud or quiet. ✗ How fast or slow.
5. Q: What does tempo mean? A: How fast or slow. ✗ How high or low.
6. Q: Steady foot-tap while you clap a name — which is the foot-tap? A: The pulse. ✗ The rhythm.
7. Q: A lullaby is usually…? A: Quiet and slow. ✗ Loud and fast.
8. Q: Can a sound be high but quiet? A: Yes — pitch and loudness are separate. ✗ No.

## COMPUTING

### Lesson C1 — What is an algorithm? (unplugged)
- **Hook (richText):** "I'm a robot that only follows exact instructions. Tell me how to make a jam sandwich — carefully!"
- **Explain (slideshow):** An algorithm is a precise, ordered set of steps to do a task. Computers do *exactly* what they're told — order and precision matter.
- **Example (whiteboard):** Build the sandwich algorithm; deliberately follow a vague step literally to show why precision matters.
- **Check (ordering):** Put sandwich/teeth-brushing steps in the correct order.
- **Practice (shortText):** Write a 4-step algorithm for a simple task.
- **Recap (keyTerm):** *Algorithm* — precise steps in order; *bug* — a step that's wrong.

### Lesson C2 — Debugging
- **Hook (whiteboard):** Show a "robot dance" algorithm with one wrong step. "Why did it fall over?"
- **Explain (richText):** Bugs are normal; debugging = find and fix the broken step. Read carefully, test a bit at a time.
- **Example (ordering):** Spot the out-of-order step and fix it.
- **Check (multipleChoice):** "Your program does the wrong thing. First you should…" (find the broken step / delete everything / give up).
- **Practice (shortText):** Find the bug in a 4-step instruction.
- **Recap:** Every coder debugs — mistakes are part of it.

### Lesson C3 — Loops (repetition)
- **Hook (richText):** "To draw a square you turn and move 4 times — is there a shortcut?"
- **Explain (slideshow):** A loop repeats steps so you don't copy them. "Repeat 4: forward, turn."
- **Example (whiteboard):** Replace 8 repeated blocks with `repeat 4`.
- **Check (multipleChoice):** "What does `repeat 3` do to the steps inside?" (does them 3 times / once / never).
- **Practice (numberEntry):** "To draw a triangle, `repeat ?`" (3).
- **Recap:** Loops make programs shorter and clearer.

**Computing question bank**
1. Q: What is an algorithm? A: A precise, ordered set of steps. ✗ A type of computer.
2. Q: What is a bug? A: A mistake/error in the steps. ✗ A kind of loop.
3. Q: What does debugging mean? A: Finding and fixing the error. ✗ Adding more steps.
4. Q: Why use a loop? A: To repeat steps without copying them. ✗ To make code run slower.
5. Q: `repeat 5 [step]` does the step how many times? A: 5. ✗ Once.
6. Q: Why does precise order matter to a computer? A: It follows instructions exactly/literally. ✗ It guesses what you mean.
7. Q: First thing to do when a program misbehaves? A: Find the broken step (debug). ✗ Delete everything.
8. Q: To draw a square with a loop: repeat how many? A: 4. ✗ 2.

## LIFE SKILLS

### Lesson L1 — Needs vs wants (and saving)
- **Hook (categorize):** Show items (water, a toy, a coat, sweets). "Which do we *need* to live, and which do we just *want*?"
- **Explain (richText):** Needs = things to live safely (food, water, shelter, warmth). Wants = nice extras. Money is limited, so we choose; saving means keeping some for later.
- **Example (whiteboard):** £5 pocket money → cover a want now vs save toward a bigger goal.
- **Check (categorize):** Sort items into needs / wants.
- **Practice (numberEntry):** "Save £2 a week — how many weeks for a £10 toy?" (5).
- **Recap:** Spend on needs first; saving turns small amounts into big buys.

### Lesson L2 — Big feelings & calming down
- **Hook (richText):** "Everyone gets angry, worried or sad sometimes — that's normal. What helps?"
- **Explain (slideshow):** Feelings are signals, not bad things. When feelings get big, the thinking brain goes offline; calming tools (slow breaths, count to 5, name the feeling, ask for help) bring it back.
- **Example (whiteboard):** Model "name it to tame it": "I feel frustrated because…"
- **Check (multipleChoice):** "A good first step when you feel really angry?" (take slow breaths / shout at someone / bottle it up).
- **Practice (speak/shortText):** Practise one calming strategy and name a feeling.
- **Recap:** All feelings are OK; what we *do* with them matters. Trusted adults help.

### Lesson L3 — Growth mindset (the power of "yet")
- **Hook (richText):** "'I can't do this' — add one word to change everything: 'I can't do this *yet*.'"
- **Explain (slideshow):** The brain grows stronger with practice (like a muscle). Mistakes are how we learn; effort and good strategies improve us — being stuck is the start, not the end.
- **Example (whiteboard):** Reframe fixed thoughts → growth thoughts ("I'm bad at maths" → "I'm still learning these steps").
- **Check (matchPairs):** Match fixed statements to growth versions.
- **Practice (shortText):** Turn one "I can't…" into "I can't… yet, so I'll…".
- **Recap (keyTerm):** *Yet* — the word that keeps you learning.

**Life skills question bank**
1. Q: Is a coat in winter a need or a want? A: A need. ✗ A want.
2. Q: What does saving money mean? A: Keeping some now to use later. ✗ Spending it all quickly.
3. Q: Save £3 a week — weeks to reach £12? A: 4. ✗ 3.
4. Q: Are angry feelings bad/wrong? A: No — all feelings are OK; what we do matters. ✗ Yes, you shouldn't feel them.
5. Q: One good way to calm down? A: Slow breaths / count to 5 / name the feeling / ask for help. ✗ Shout at someone.
6. Q: What does adding "yet" do to "I can't do this"? A: Makes it about still learning — you can improve. ✗ Nothing.
7. Q: Are mistakes part of learning? A: Yes — they help the brain grow. ✗ No, they mean you failed.
8. Q: Spend on needs or wants first? A: Needs first. ✗ Wants first.
