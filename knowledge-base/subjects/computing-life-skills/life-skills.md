# Life Skills (PSHE & Practical Skills, KS1–KS2)

Twelve topics covering everyday competence for ages 5–11, drawn from the non-statutory PSHE/RSHE framework and practical life skills: financial literacy, critical thinking, health & wellbeing, social skills, and practical skills. These topics shape children's confidence, feelings and choices, so keep the tone **warm, supportive and never shaming.** Mistakes and big feelings are normal; the message is always that the child can learn, grow and ask for help.

---

## 1. What is money & where it comes from

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–3 (ages 5–8) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – financial literacy

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- **Money** is what we use to pay for things (coins, notes, and now cards/digital money too).
- We get money mostly by **earning** it — grown-ups do jobs/work and are paid.
- Money is **limited** — there's only so much, so we have to choose how to use it.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has seen coins, notes or a card used to buy something.
- Can count small numbers.

### Teach it (the explanation)
**Money** is a clever idea that lets us swap things easily. Long ago people *bartered* — traded a chicken for some bread. Tricky! So we invented money: coins and notes (and now cards and phones) that everyone agrees are worth something, so we can buy what we need.

Where does money come from? Mostly from **work**. Grown-ups do jobs — a nurse, a builder, a shopkeeper, a driver — and they're paid money for the work they do. Some people run their own business; some money comes as a gift (like birthday money) or pocket money for helping at home. The big idea: money usually has to be **earned**, and there's only so much of it, so we **choose** carefully how to spend it.

**Worked example.** Imagine you have £2 of pocket money. A toy costs £1.50 and a comic costs £1. You can't buy *both* (that's £2.50, more than you have). Money is limited, so you choose: the toy, the comic, or save the £2 for something bigger later. Every spending choice means saying "yes" to one thing and "no" to another.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Shops:** paying at a real shop with coins or a card.
- **Jobs:** what jobs do people in their family or community do?
- **Pocket money:** earning a little for helping at home.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Money comes from the cash machine / card, so it never runs out."
  **Why:** the machine seems to magic money out; cards hide the limit.
  **Fix:** explain the machine/card only gives money a grown-up already *earned and put in*; it can run out.
- **Wrong idea:** "You can just buy everything you want."
  **Why:** doesn't grasp that money is limited.
  **Fix:** use a fixed amount of play coins and show that buying one thing means not buying another.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Where does most money come from? **A:** From working/doing a job and being paid (also gifts/pocket money).
- **Q:** You have £2. A book is £1.50 and a pen is £1. Can you buy both? **A:** No — that's £2.50, more than £2. You have to choose.

### Activity / practice
**Number entry / role-play:** a "shop" game with play coins and a set budget — the child picks what they can afford and works out the change.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 — "What is money?" / "Coins and notes."**
- **BBC Bitesize / "Pounds and pence" — money topics for young children** (free).

## 2. Needs vs wants

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–4 (ages 5–9) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – financial literacy

**Prerequisites:** What is money & where it comes from.

### Key concepts
- A **need** is something we must have to live and be well (food, water, somewhere to live, warm clothes).
- A **want** is something nice to have but not essential (toys, sweets, the latest game).
- Spending wisely means covering **needs first**, then choosing **wants** carefully.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows money is limited and has to be chosen carefully.
- Can name things they own and things they wish for.

### Teach it (the explanation)
When money is limited, it helps to sort things into **needs** and **wants**.
- A **need** is something you really must have to live, stay healthy and safe: food, clean water, a home, warm clothes, medicine.
- A **want** is something you'd *like* but could live without: a new toy, sweets, a video game, a fancier pair of trainers.

Both are fine! The trick is **needs come first.** Grown-ups make sure there's money for needs (food, the home, bills) before spending on wants. And lots of things are a bit of both — you *need* shoes, but you might *want* the most expensive, glittery ones. Knowing the difference helps you make good choices with your own money too.

**Worked example.** Sort these: bread (need), a teddy (want), water (need), a comic (want), a coat (need), an ice cream (want). Now imagine you have £5 and you're hungry with no lunch — the wise first spend is the bread, because food is a *need*. Wants can wait or be saved for.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Shopping list:** which items are needs, which are treats?
- **Desert island:** what would you *need* to survive vs just *want*?
- **Birthday list:** wants — and that's okay, they're for special times.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "I *need* that toy / those sweets."
  **Why:** strong desire feels like need.
  **Fix:** test it — "could you stay healthy and safe without it?" If yes, it's a want (which is fine!).
- **Wrong idea:** needs and wants are totally separate, never overlapping.
  **Why:** simple sorting misses nuance.
  **Fix:** show the "need a coat, want a sparkly one" example — the basic thing is a need, the upgrade a want.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Is food a need or a want? Is a video game a need or a want? **A:** Food = need; video game = want.
- **Q:** Why should needs come before wants when spending money? **A:** Because needs keep us healthy and safe; wants are nice extras we can have after needs are covered.

### Activity / practice
**Categorize:** sort a set of items (water, sweets, a coat, a toy, medicine, a comic) into "need" and "want," then pick one want worth saving up for.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1/KS2 — "Needs and wants."**
- **Internet Matters / money-education sites — "Needs vs wants" activities** (free).

## 3. Saving, spending & simple budgeting

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – financial literacy

**Prerequisites:** What is money; Needs vs wants.

### Key concepts
- **Saving** means keeping some money now to use later, often for something bigger.
- A **budget** is a plan for your money: what comes in, and how you'll use it.
- A simple plan: **spend some, save some** (and some people give some).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows money is limited and the difference between needs and wants.
- Can add and subtract small amounts.

### Teach it (the explanation)
If you spend every penny the moment you get it, you can never afford anything bigger. **Saving** is the answer: keep a bit of your money safe now so it grows into enough for something you really want later — like a bike or a special game.

A **budget** is just a plan for your money. You look at what **comes in** (pocket money, a gift) and decide how to **use it**: a bit to spend now, a bit to save, maybe a bit to give. A popular kid-friendly plan is three jars or pots: **Spend, Save, Give.** Each time you get money, you share it between the pots.

To save for a goal, work out: *How much does it cost? How much can I save each week? How many weeks will that take?* That's real budgeting — and it makes the wait feel doable.

**Worked example.** You want a £12 game. You get £3 pocket money a week and decide to save £2 of it each week (spending £1). £12 ÷ £2 = **6 weeks** to reach your goal. After 3 weeks you'll have £6 — halfway! A budget turns "I'll never afford it" into a clear plan.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Goals:** saving up for a specific toy, game or trip they want.
- **Jars:** decorating Spend / Save / Give pots.
- **Charity:** giving a little to a cause they care about.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Saving means I lose the money."
  **Why:** putting money away feels like spending it.
  **Fix:** show the saving pot growing — the money is still theirs, just waiting to buy something bigger.
- **Wrong idea:** "I can spend and save the same money."
  **Why:** doesn't see money as finite once allocated.
  **Fix:** use real pots/coins — money put in "save" isn't in "spend" any more.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a budget? **A:** A plan for your money — what comes in and how you'll spend, save (and give) it.
- **Q:** You save £2 a week. How many weeks to save £10? **A:** 5 weeks (£10 ÷ £2).
- **Q:** Why might you save instead of spending it all now? **A:** To afford something bigger or more important later.

### Activity / practice
**Number entry / build:** set a savings goal (a priced item), pick a weekly amount to save, and work out how many weeks it'll take; optionally plan a Spend/Save/Give split of one week's money.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — "Saving and spending" / "Budgeting."**
- **BBC Bitesize / money-education — "Spend, save, give" activities** (free).

## 4. Asking good questions & simple logic (critical thinking)

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 2–6 (ages 6–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – critical thinking

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- A **good question** helps you find out or understand more (often starts who, what, when, where, why, how).
- **Logical thinking** means reasoning step by step from what you know.
- Curiosity is a strength — it's good to wonder, ask and check.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can ask simple questions and listen to answers.
- Understands "because" — that things have reasons.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Smart thinkers aren't the ones who *know* everything — they're the ones who **ask good questions** and **reason carefully**. A good question opens a door to find out more. The most useful question words are **who, what, when, where, why** and **how** — and the best of all is often "**Why?**" and "**How do you know?**"

**Logical thinking** means working things out step by step from clues you already have, instead of just guessing. If you know "all cats have whiskers" and "Tom is a cat," you can *reason* that "Tom has whiskers" — no need to check! Connecting facts like this is logic.

Being curious and asking questions is a real strength. It's how scientists, detectives and inventors figure things out. Never feel silly for asking — questions are how we learn.

**Worked example.** A detective puzzle: "The cake is gone. There are crumbs leading to the dog's basket, and the dog looks very pleased." Ask good questions — Who had the chance? What's the evidence? — and reason step by step: crumbs lead to the dog, the dog looks guilty, so the dog probably ate the cake. We used clues + logic to reach a sensible conclusion (and we'd still check before being *sure*).

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Detective/mystery:** solving a puzzle from clues.
- **Science:** asking "why?" and "what if?" about the world.
- **Games:** working out the next move logically in chess/strategy games.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Asking questions means you're not clever."
  **Why:** equates not-knowing with failure.
  **Fix:** praise questions — the cleverest people ask the most; questions are how we learn.
- **Wrong idea:** jumping to an answer without using the clues.
  **Why:** guessing feels faster than reasoning.
  **Fix:** model "what do we *know*? what does that tell us?" — reasoning step by step from evidence.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name three question words that help you find out more. **A:** Any three of who, what, when, where, why, how.
- **Q:** All birds have feathers. A robin is a bird. So what do we know about a robin? **A:** A robin has feathers (worked out by logic, not checking).

### Activity / practice
**Speak / matchPairs:** give the child a short mystery or a set of clues and ask them to pose good questions and reason out a logical answer, explaining "how do you know?"

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — "Asking questions" / problem-solving activities.**
- **BBC Bitesize / Newsround — "Thinking critically about what you see and hear"** (free).

## 5. Facts vs opinions

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – critical thinking

**Prerequisites:** Asking good questions & simple logic.

### Key concepts
- A **fact** is something that can be checked and is true for everyone (water is wet; London is in England).
- An **opinion** is what someone *thinks or feels* — it can differ from person to person (chocolate is the best flavour).
- Both are useful, but it's important to know **which is which**, especially online and in adverts.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Understands true/false.
- Can give their own view on something ("I like…").

### Teach it (the explanation)
Some statements are **facts** — things that are true and can be **checked**, the same for everyone. "A triangle has three sides." "Spiders have eight legs." You could look these up and prove them.

Other statements are **opinions** — what a person **thinks, likes or feels**. "Spiders are scary." "Pizza is the best food." These aren't right or wrong — they change from person to person. (Words like *best, worst, lovely, horrible, should* are clues that you're hearing an opinion.)

Why does it matter? Because adverts, posts and even friends often mix facts and opinions to persuade you. "This is the BEST cereal EVER and it makes you super strong!" — *best ever* is opinion; *makes you super strong* is a claim you should **check**. A good thinker asks: "Is that a fact I can check, or someone's opinion?"

**Worked example.** Sort these: "The Sun is a star" → **fact** (checkable). "Maths is boring" → **opinion** (depends who you ask). "Our school has 200 children" → **fact** (you can count). "Blue is the prettiest colour" → **opinion** (a preference). For each opinion, notice nobody is *wrong* — they just think differently.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Adverts:** spotting the opinion-words used to sell things.
- **Reviews:** a game review mixing facts (it has 10 levels) and opinions (it's amazing).
- **Debates:** respecting that friends can have different opinions.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "If lots of people think it, it's a fact."
  **Why:** popularity feels like proof.
  **Fix:** a shared opinion is still an opinion (everyone *liking* a film doesn't make 'it's the best' a fact).
- **Wrong idea:** "Opinions are just wrong / facts are the only thing that matters."
  **Why:** dismisses feelings/views.
  **Fix:** explain opinions are valuable and valid — we just shouldn't treat them as proven facts.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Is "a week has seven days" a fact or an opinion? Is "Mondays are the worst" a fact or an opinion? **A:** Fact; opinion.
- **Q:** What clue words often signal an opinion? **A:** Words like best, worst, lovely, horrible, should, prettiest — feeling/judgement words.

### Activity / practice
**Categorize:** sort a list of statements into "fact" and "opinion," and for the opinions, identify the clue word that gives it away.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — "Fact and opinion."**
- **BBC Newsround — "Spotting fact vs opinion in the news"** (free).

## 6. Healthy eating

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – health & wellbeing

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- A **balanced diet** has lots of different foods, with plenty of fruit and vegetables.
- Some foods (sugary, very fatty) are "sometimes" treats, not "every day" foods.
- Food is **fuel** — it gives our bodies and brains energy to grow, move and think.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can name foods they eat.
- Knows eating gives them energy.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Food is your body's **fuel** — like petrol for a car. The kind of fuel you put in matters. To grow, move, play and think well, your body needs a **balanced diet**: a good mix of different foods, not just one kind.

A simple picture (the **Eatwell** idea) is to fill your plate mostly with: lots of **fruit and vegetables**, some **starchy foods** for energy (bread, rice, pasta, potatoes), some **protein** to build your body (beans, eggs, fish, meat), and a little **dairy** (milk, cheese, yoghurt). Drink plenty of **water**.

Then there are "**sometimes** foods" — sweets, crisps, fizzy drinks, cake. They're tasty and fine *now and then*, but they're treats, not every-day fuel, because too much sugar and fat isn't great for our bodies or teeth. Healthy eating isn't about banning treats — it's about **balance**: mostly good fuel, sometimes a treat.

**Worked example.** Build a balanced plate: half the plate fruit & veg (carrots, apple slices, peas), a quarter starchy (pasta), a quarter protein (chicken or beans), a glass of water. Compare with a plate of just sweets and fizzy drink — fun once in a while, but not fuel to grow on. Notice the balanced plate has *variety* and *colour*.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Sport:** what athletes eat to have energy and grow strong.
- **Rainbow:** "eat a rainbow" of different-coloured fruit and veg.
- **Cooking:** helping make a balanced meal or snack.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Treats/sweets are bad and I should never have them."
  **Why:** all-or-nothing thinking creates guilt.
  **Fix:** frame it as *balance* — sometimes foods are fine in small amounts; the aim is mostly good fuel.
- **Wrong idea:** "One healthy or one unhealthy meal makes me healthy/unhealthy."
  **Why:** focuses on a single meal.
  **Fix:** explain it's the *overall pattern* over days and weeks that matters, not one plate.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which should fill most of your plate for a balanced meal? **A:** Fruit and vegetables.
- **Q:** Are sweets an "every day" food or a "sometimes" food? **A:** A sometimes food / treat — fine now and then, in small amounts.

### Activity / practice
**Categorize / build:** the child builds a balanced plate by sorting foods into the groups (fruit & veg, starchy, protein, dairy, sometimes-treats) and aims for a colourful, balanced plate.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1/KS2 — "Healthy eating" / "The Eatwell Guide."**
- **NHS / Change4Life — "Healthier families: food & recipes"** (free).

## 7. Sleep & exercise

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – health & wellbeing

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- **Sleep** lets your body and brain rest, grow and remember — children need a lot of it (around 9–11 hours).
- **Exercise** keeps your heart, muscles and mood healthy; aim to be active every day.
- Both help you feel good, learn well and have energy.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows they feel tired without sleep and energetic after rest.
- Has done some active play.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Two things power up your body and brain for free: **sleep** and **exercise**.

When you **sleep**, your body isn't doing nothing — it's busy growing, repairing, and tidying up your brain, which is how you remember what you learned that day. That's why children need lots of sleep — around **9 to 11 hours** a night. Too little, and everything feels harder: grumpy mood, fuzzy thinking, less energy. A calm bedtime routine and switching off screens early help you fall asleep.

**Exercise** is moving your body — running, jumping, dancing, cycling, swimming, playing football. It makes your heart and muscles strong, helps you sleep better, *and* lifts your mood (moving releases "feel-good" signals in your brain). Aim to be **active every day** — about an hour of moving and playing.

Sleep and exercise work as a team with healthy eating to keep you well, happy and ready to learn.

**Worked example.** Compare two days. Day A: late night, no playing outside, tired and cross at school. Day B: good night's sleep, a bike ride and a game of tag, energetic and cheerful, sleeps well again. Same child — the difference is sleep and movement. Notice how they feed each other: exercise helps you sleep, and good sleep gives you energy to move.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Sport:** how athletes train *and* rest to perform.
- **Superhero:** sleep as "recharging your batteries."
- **Pets:** how animals sleep and play a lot too.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Sleep is wasted time — I'd rather stay up."
  **Why:** sleep feels like missing out.
  **Fix:** explain the body grows and the brain saves memories during sleep — it's *doing a job*, not nothing.
- **Wrong idea:** "Exercise means boring, hard workouts."
  **Why:** narrow idea of exercise.
  **Fix:** show that playing, dancing and games all count — exercise can be fun.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Roughly how many hours of sleep does a child your age need? **A:** Around 9–11 hours a night.
- **Q:** Name two good things exercise does for you. **A:** Any two of: strong heart/muscles, better sleep, better mood, more energy.

### Activity / practice
**Speak / build:** the child plans a healthy day including a bedtime routine and an active activity they enjoy, and explains why each helps.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1/KS2 — "Sleep" / "Exercise and keeping active."**
- **NHS — "How much sleep do children need?" / "Physical activity for children"** (free).

## 8. Feelings & calming down (emotional regulation)

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – health & wellbeing (PSHE)

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- **All feelings are okay** — happy, sad, angry, scared, excited, worried. They're normal and they pass.
- **Naming** a feeling helps you handle it ("I feel frustrated").
- Simple **calming tools** (slow breathing, a break, counting, talking to someone) help when feelings are big.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can recognise basic feelings in themselves and others.
- Has felt strong emotions (excited, upset, cross).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Everyone has feelings — lots of them, every day — and **all feelings are okay.** Feeling happy, sad, scared, angry, jealous or worried doesn't make you good or bad; it makes you human. Feelings are like weather: even the stormy ones pass.

The clever first step is to **name** the feeling: "I feel frustrated," "I feel nervous." Naming it turns a big, blurry storm into something you can understand and handle — it's like turning the volume down a little.

When a feeling gets really big (especially anger or worry), you can use a **calming tool** before you act:
- **Slow breathing** — breathe in slowly, out even more slowly (try "smell the flower, blow out the candle," or breathe round the edges of your hand).
- **Take a break / a quiet moment** before you say or do something.
- **Count to ten** slowly.
- **Talk to someone** you trust about how you feel.

Big feelings don't mean you're "being bad" — they mean you need a tool and maybe a grown-up's help. That's true for grown-ups too.

**Worked example.** Your tower gets knocked over and you feel **furious** — fists clenched, want to shout. Step 1: name it ("I'm really angry"). Step 2: pick a calming tool — three slow breaths, smell-the-flower-blow-the-candle. Step 3: once calmer, choose what to do — ask for help rebuilding, or take a short break. The feeling was okay; the tool helped you handle it kindly.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Weather:** feelings as weather that comes and goes.
- **Colours/emojis:** matching feelings to colours or faces.
- **Sport:** staying calm after a mistake in a game.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Some feelings (like anger or sadness) are bad and I shouldn't have them."
  **Why:** children learn to hide "negative" feelings.
  **Fix:** reassure that *all* feelings are okay — it's what we *do* with them that we can choose.
- **Wrong idea:** "If I feel angry I can't help what I do."
  **Why:** feels swept away by the emotion.
  **Fix:** teach the pause — name it, breathe, *then* choose; the feeling doesn't get to decide your action.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Is it okay to feel angry or sad sometimes? **A:** Yes — all feelings are okay and normal; they pass.
- **Q:** Name one thing you can do to calm down when a feeling gets really big. **A:** e.g. slow breathing, take a break, count to ten, talk to a trusted adult.

### Activity / practice
**Speak / matchPairs:** the child names feelings shown in faces/situations and chooses a calming tool for a "big feeling" scenario, then practises slow breathing.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1/KS2 — "Feelings and emotions" / "Managing big feelings."**
- **Childline — "Calming activities & toolbox"; NHS — "Children's emotional wellbeing"** (free).

## 9. Growth mindset & resilience

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 2–6 (ages 6–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – health & wellbeing (PSHE)

**Prerequisites:** Feelings & calming down.

### Key concepts
- Your brain **grows** when you practise — you get better at things by trying, not by being "born clever."
- **Mistakes** help your brain learn; struggle means you're growing, not failing.
- The magic word is "**yet**" — "I can't do this *yet*."

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has learned a new skill through practice (riding a bike, reading).
- Knows the feeling of finding something hard.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Here's something amazing: your brain is a bit like a muscle — it gets **stronger and cleverer the more you use it.** Every time you practise something hard, tiny connections in your brain grow. So people aren't simply "good" or "bad" at things forever — they get better by **practising.** This is called a **growth mindset.**

The surprising part: **mistakes are good for learning.** When something's tricky and you have to think hard, *that's* when your brain grows the most. So struggling doesn't mean you're failing — it means you're learning. Getting it wrong, then trying again, is exactly how you improve.

The most powerful word is "**yet.**" Instead of "I can't do this," say "I can't do this **yet**." That tiny word turns a dead end into a path: you're not stuck, you're on the way. Effort, trying different strategies, and asking for help are what get you there.

**Worked example.** A child says "I'm rubbish at times tables." Reframe it together: "You can't recall them all *yet*." Then plan the *yet*: practise a few each day, use a song, try again tomorrow. Compare to learning to ride a bike — wobbles and falls at first, then suddenly you can. Times tables work the same way: practice grows the skill. Mistakes were part of getting there, not proof you couldn't.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Sport/music:** how anyone good at football or piano got there by practising.
- **Video games:** levelling up and retrying a hard level until you beat it.
- **Brain-as-muscle:** "exercising" your brain to make it stronger.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "I'm just not a maths/reading person — some people can, I can't."
  **Why:** fixed mindset — believes ability is set at birth.
  **Fix:** point to a skill they once couldn't do and now can; ability grows with practice.
- **Wrong idea:** "Making mistakes means I'm bad at it / should stop."
  **Why:** treats errors as failure.
  **Fix:** reframe mistakes as the brain learning; the question is "what can this mistake teach me?"

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How do you get better at something hard? **A:** By practising, trying again, and learning from mistakes — not by being "born clever."
- **Q:** Turn "I can't do this" into a growth-mindset sentence. **A:** "I can't do this **yet**."

### Activity / practice
**Speak / fillBlank:** the child takes "I can't…" statements and adds "yet," then names one strategy (practise, ask for help, try another way) to make progress.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — "Growth mindset" / "Resilience and not giving up."**
- **ClassDojo "Growth Mindset" series (with Mojo)** — free animated resilience videos.

## 10. Social skills — listening, sharing & resolving disagreements

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – social skills (PSHE)

**Prerequisites:** Feelings & calming down.

### Key concepts
- **Listening** well means looking, staying quiet, and really hearing the other person.
- **Sharing and taking turns** keep play fair and friendships happy.
- When friends **disagree**, you can solve it calmly: take turns to speak, say how you feel, and find a fair fix together.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Plays and talks with other children.
- Has had a disagreement and felt the upset of it.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Getting on with others is a skill you can learn and practise, just like reading.

**Listening** is more than being quiet — it's *looking* at the person, keeping your own thoughts on hold for a moment, and really trying to understand them. People feel valued when you truly listen, and you learn more too.

**Sharing and taking turns** keep things fair. Nobody likes a game where one person hogs everything. Taking turns ("you go, then me") and sharing make play happier and friendships stronger.

**Disagreements** happen to *everyone* — even best friends. They're normal. What matters is solving them kindly:
1. **Calm down first** (use a breathing tool if you're cross).
2. **Take turns to speak and listen** — each says what happened and how they feel ("I felt sad when…").
3. **Find a fair fix together** — a compromise, a turn-taking plan, or a sorry.
If you can't sort it out, **ask a trusted adult to help** — that's fine, not failing.

**Worked example.** Two children both want the same blue crayon. A poor fix: grabbing and shouting (someone ends up upset). A good fix: calm down, take turns to speak ("I need blue for the sky" / "I need it for the sea"), then find a fair solution — take turns with it, or one uses blue now and the other next. Both feel heard; the friendship survives. Listening + turn-taking solved it.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Games:** taking turns and being a good sport when you lose.
- **Friendship:** how listening makes friends feel cared about.
- **Teamwork:** sharing jobs to build or make something together.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "To win a disagreement I have to be loudest / get my way."
  **Why:** sees conflict as winning vs losing.
  **Fix:** model "fair fix" thinking — the goal is everyone feeling heard, not one winner.
- **Wrong idea:** "Disagreeing with a friend means we're not friends any more."
  **Why:** conflict feels like the end of friendship.
  **Fix:** reassure that all friends disagree sometimes; solving it kindly makes friendships stronger.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What are two things good listening involves? **A:** Any two of: looking at the person, staying quiet, not interrupting, really trying to understand.
- **Q:** Two friends both want the last turn on a swing. Suggest a fair way to solve it. **A:** Take turns (e.g. count to 20 each), share the time, or ask a grown-up to help decide fairly.

### Activity / practice
**Speak / role-play:** act out a small disagreement and practise the steps — calm down, take turns to speak using "I felt…," and agree a fair fix together.

## 11. Telling the time, days & the calendar

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–4 (ages 5–9) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – practical (links to maths – time)

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- A clock shows **hours** (short hand) and **minutes** (long hand); we read o'clock, half past, quarter past/to, then 5-minute steps.
- A day has **24 hours**; a week has **7 days**; a year has **12 months**.
- A **calendar** helps us know today's date and plan ahead (birthdays, events).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can count to 60 and in fives.
- Knows day and night, and some days of the week.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Telling the time helps you run your day — when to get up, when school starts, when it's time for tea. On a clock face, the **short hand points to the hour**, the **long hand points to the minutes.** Read it in steps:
- **O'clock** — long hand on 12 (e.g. 3 o'clock).
- **Half past** — long hand on 6 (half an hour gone).
- **Quarter past** — long hand on 3; **quarter to** — long hand on 9.
- Then count minutes in **fives** round the clock (each number = 5 minutes).

Time also stacks up into bigger chunks we plan with: **60 minutes = 1 hour**, **24 hours = 1 day**, **7 days = 1 week**, about **4 weeks = 1 month**, **12 months = 1 year**. The **days of the week** (Monday–Sunday) and **months** (January–December) always go in the same order — worth learning by heart (a song helps!).

A **calendar** lays the days out in a grid so you can find **today's date**, count how many sleeps until an event, and plan ahead.

**Worked example.** The short hand is between 2 and 3; the long hand is on 6. The hour is 2 (the one it's just past), and "long hand on 6" means **half past**, so it's **half past 2** (2:30). Now a calendar question: if today is Monday the 5th, what date is next Monday? Add 7 days → the **12th**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Routine:** what time do they get up, eat, go to bed?
- **Countdown:** how many sleeps until their birthday or a trip (use a calendar).
- **TV/games:** "your favourite show starts at half past 4."

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** reading "half past 3" as "half past 6" because the long hand is on the 6.
  **Why:** reads the minute hand's number as the minutes literally.
  **Fix:** teach that the long hand on 6 means *30 minutes / half past*, and the **hour** comes from the short hand.
- **Wrong idea:** thinking the next month/day after the last is a fresh "1" with no pattern.
  **Why:** doesn't see the fixed cycle.
  **Fix:** use a calendar grid and the days/months song so the repeating order sticks.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** The short hand is just past 7 and the long hand is on 12. What time is it? **A:** 7 o'clock.
- **Q:** How many days are in a week, and how many months in a year? **A:** 7 days; 12 months.
- **Q:** Today is the 10th. What's the date one week from today? **A:** The 17th.

### Activity / practice
**Number entry / matchPairs:** read several clock faces (o'clock, half past, quarter past/to) and use a calendar to answer "what day/date is…" questions.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1/KS2 — "Telling the time" / "Days, months and the calendar."**
- **Topmarks — "Telling the Time" interactive clock games** (free).

## 12. Staying safe — road safety & first-aid awareness

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Life skills – practical safety (PSHE)

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- Crossing roads safely: **Stop, Look, Listen, Think** — find a safe place, and cross with a grown-up.
- In an emergency, **stay calm, get an adult, and know how to call 999** (UK) for ambulance, police or fire.
- Simple first-aid awareness: don't move someone badly hurt, get help, and you *can* help by fetching an adult.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Walks near roads with a grown-up.
- Knows what "an emergency" roughly means.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Knowing how to stay safe — and what to do if someone's hurt — helps you and the people around you.

**Crossing roads.** The safe rule is **Stop, Look, Listen, Think**:
1. **Stop** at the kerb (not on the road).
2. **Look** all around for traffic — and keep looking.
3. **Listen** for cars you can't see yet.
4. **Think** — is it safe? If a car's coming, wait. Cross when there's a gap, and **keep looking and listening** as you go.
Pick a **safe place** to cross (a crossing, a lollipop person, a quiet spot with a clear view), and young children should cross **holding a grown-up's hand.**

**Emergencies and first aid.** If someone is badly hurt, the most important things are: **stay calm** and **get an adult straight away.** In a real emergency in the UK you call **999** and ask for ambulance, police or fire — knowing this number is a great thing to remember. You don't need to be a doctor: fetching a grown-up *is* helping. For a tiny scrape, an adult can clean it and add a plaster; for anything serious, get help fast. Never move someone who's badly hurt unless they're in danger — wait for help.

**Worked example.** You want to cross a road to the park. Walk through it: find a safe spot away from parked cars → **Stop** at the kerb → **Look** right, left, right again → **Listen** → **Think** "is it clear?" A car is coming, so **wait**. It passes, the road's clear → cross briskly, still looking and listening, holding the grown-up's hand. Then practise the emergency line: "If someone collapses and won't wake, I stay calm, tell a grown-up, and we can call **999**."

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Walks:** practising Stop-Look-Listen-Think on real walks (with a grown-up).
- **Heroes:** the 999 helpers — ambulance, fire, police.
- **Plasters:** helping look after a small scrape at home.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "If I can't see a car right now, it's safe to run across."
  **Why:** checks once, then dashes.
  **Fix:** teach *keep* looking and listening the whole way, and cross calmly, not by running blindly.
- **Wrong idea:** "I'm just a kid, I can't help in an emergency."
  **Why:** feels powerless.
  **Fix:** explain that staying calm, getting an adult and knowing 999 *is* powerful, real help.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What are the four steps for crossing a road safely? **A:** Stop, Look, Listen, Think (find a safe place; cross with a grown-up).
- **Q:** What number do you call in a UK emergency, and what can you ask for? **A:** 999 — ambulance, police or fire.
- **Q:** Your friend has fallen and is really hurt. What's the first thing to do? **A:** Stay calm and get an adult straight away (call 999 if it's serious).

### Activity / practice
**Ordering / speak:** put the road-crossing steps in order (Stop, Look, Listen, Think), and role-play an emergency: "stay calm → get an adult → call 999 and ask for the right help."

- **THINK! (UK road safety) — children's road safety; St John Ambulance — "First aid for kids"** (free).
