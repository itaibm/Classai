## How to teach computing

Computing for young children is **not** "learning to use a computer" and it is **not** mainly typing code. At its heart it is a way of thinking — **computational thinking** — that applies far beyond a screen. Four ideas run through everything:

- **Decomposition** — breaking a big problem into smaller, manageable parts. ("How do I make a sandwich?" → get bread, add filling, cut it.)
- **Pattern recognition** — spotting things that repeat or are similar. ("Every dance step repeats four times.")
- **Abstraction** — ignoring the unimportant detail and keeping what matters. (A map shows roads, not every brick.)
- **Algorithms** — a precise, step-by-step set of instructions to do something or solve a problem.

### Unplugged before screens

The best primary computing often happens **away from the computer**. Children give each other instructions to draw a picture, act as "robots" that follow commands, sort objects, or sequence the steps of brushing teeth. "Unplugged" activities teach the *thinking* without the distraction of a device, and they are free — no computer needed. Teach the concept unplugged first, then transfer it to Scratch Jr / Scratch.

### Precise instructions and the "literal robot"

The single most powerful teaching move is the **literal robot**: the tutor (or parent) follows the child's instructions *exactly*, including the silly results. Tell the robot "put the lid on the pen" without saying "pick up the lid first" and the robot tries to push a capless pen through a lid. Children laugh, then learn that computers do *exactly* what you say — no more, no less. This is why instructions must be precise and ordered.

### Debugging is normal, not failure

Programs almost never work first time, and **that is completely normal**. A "bug" is just a mistake in the instructions; **debugging** is finding and fixing it. Frame every error as interesting, not shameful: "Great, we found a bug! Let's hunt down where it went wrong." Teach a simple method: *predict* what should happen, *run* it, *spot* where it went wrong, *fix*, *run again*. This builds resilience and is exactly how real programmers work.

### Programming progression (Scratch Jr → Scratch)

Code is built from a few core ideas, introduced roughly in this order:

1. **Sequence** — instructions run in order, top to bottom.
2. **Events** — something *starts* the code (when the green flag is clicked, when this sprite is tapped).
3. **Repetition / loops** — "repeat 4 times" instead of writing the same step four times.
4. **Selection / conditionals** — "if … then …": the program chooses based on a condition.
5. **Variables** — a named box that stores a value that can change (a score, a name, a count).

Scratch Jr (free app, ages 5–7) covers sequence, events and simple loops with picture blocks. Scratch (free at **scratch.mit.edu**, ages 7+) adds loops, variables, conditionals and much more, with coloured drag-and-drop blocks — no typos possible, so children focus on logic, not syntax.

## How to teach life skills & PSHE

These topics touch children's feelings, families, money and safety, so the tone matters as much as the content:

- **Warm and supportive.** Never shaming. A child who feels worried, made a mistake online, or finds feelings hard should always feel safe to talk.
- **Concrete and practical.** Money is learned by handling coins and "shopping"; time by reading a real clock; feelings by naming them.
- **Trusted adults at the centre.** The recurring safety message is simple and constant: *if anything ever worries, upsets or confuses you, tell a trusted grown-up.* It is never the child's fault, and telling is always the brave, right thing.
- **Age-appropriate.** Keep online-safety content focused on kindness, privacy and asking for help — not frightening scenarios.

## KS1 → KS2 scope & sequence

| Strand | KS1 (Years 1–2, ages 5–7) | KS2 (Years 3–6, ages 7–11) |
|--------|----------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Algorithms & logic** | What an algorithm is; following & writing precise step-by-step instructions; predicting what simple programs do | Decomposition, patterns & abstraction; designing, writing and debugging programs to achieve goals; logical reasoning to predict behaviour |
| **Programming** | Create & debug simple programs (Scratch Jr / Bee-Bot): sequence, events, simple repeats | Sequence, **selection** (if/then) and **repetition** (loops); use **variables**; control physical or on-screen systems (Scratch) |
| **Networks & the internet** | — (introduced through "where do videos come from?") | How networks/the internet work simply; how search engines find & rank results; evaluating whether content is reliable |
| **Data & information** | Sort and group objects; simple pictograms & tally charts | Collect, organise and present data (pictograms, bar charts, branching databases, spreadsheets); analyse it |
| **Creating digital content** | Use technology purposefully to create, store and retrieve digital content (paint, simple text, photos) | Combine software/services to design and create content (text, images, sound, presentations) for a real audience |
| **Online safety & digital literacy** | Use technology safely & respectfully; keep personal information private; tell a trusted adult if worried | Use technology responsibly; recognise acceptable/unacceptable behaviour; identify reliable vs unreliable info; report concerns |
| **Life skills (PSHE)** | Needs vs wants; where money comes from; feelings & kindness; healthy basics; telling the time (o'clock/half past); road safety basics | Saving & simple budgeting; facts vs opinions & spotting unreliable claims; sleep/exercise/healthy eating; emotional regulation & growth mindset; resolving disagreements; calendar, first-aid awareness, road safety |

The curriculum is a **spiral**: the same ideas (a precise instruction, a loop, "tell a trusted adult", needs vs wants) come back each year with more depth and harder examples. Always teach the concept unplugged or concretely first, then move to the screen.
