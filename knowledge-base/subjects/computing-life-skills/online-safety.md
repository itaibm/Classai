# Online Safety & Digital Literacy (KS1–KS2)

Six topics covering staying safe, kind and wise online, mapped to the digital-literacy strand of the Computing curriculum and the online-safety part of PSHE/RSHE. These topics touch children's feelings and worries, so the tone is **warm, calm and reassuring** throughout. The recurring message is simple and never frightening: *the internet is brilliant, and a trusted adult is always there to help.* Never imply a child is to blame, and keep examples gentle and age-appropriate.

---

## 1. Personal information & privacy

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Digital literacy – privacy

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- **Personal information** is private detail about you: your full name, address, school, phone number, photos, passwords.
- We keep personal information **private** online — we don't share it with people we don't know in real life.
- A grown-up should always help before you share anything about yourself online.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows their own name and roughly what an "address" or "school" is.
- Has seen a game, app or website that asks questions.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Some information about you is **private** — it's like the key to your front door, and you only give it to people you really trust. This **personal information** includes your full name, where you live, your school, your phone number, your password and photos of you.

Online, you sometimes "meet" people you've never met in real life — in a game chat or a message. Even if they seem friendly, **we don't share personal information with people we don't know offline.** And whenever an app, game or website asks for details about you, the rule is: **check with a trusted adult first.**

Passwords are extra-special private. A good password is a secret — not your name or "1234" — and you only ever share it with a parent or carer, never with a friend or anyone online.

**Worked example.** A game chat says: "Hi! What school do you go to and where do you live?" What do we do? We **don't answer** the personal questions, and we **tell a trusted adult**. We could talk about our favourite colour in the game — that's not private — but never our school, address or full name.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Games:** chat boxes in online games asking questions.
- **Keys/treasure:** personal info is like a secret key you keep safe.
- **Family:** who counts as a "trusted adult" in their life.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "If someone online is nice, it's fine to tell them where I live."
  **Why:** children judge trust by friendliness.
  **Fix:** explain that online you can't be sure who someone really is, so private info stays private and a grown-up helps.
- **Wrong idea:** "Sharing my password with a best friend is okay."
  **Why:** sharing secrets feels like friendship.
  **Fix:** passwords are only for you and a parent/carer — true friends won't mind keeping yours private.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name two pieces of personal information you should keep private online. **A:** Any two of: full name, address, school, phone number, password, photos.
- **Q:** A new online "friend" in a game asks where you live. What do you do? **A:** Don't tell them, and tell a trusted adult.

### Activity / practice
**Categorize:** sort facts into "okay to share in a game" (favourite colour, that you like dogs) vs "keep private" (home address, school, phone number, password).

### Resources
- **ThinkUKnow (CEOP) — Jessie & Friends / Play Like Share** (privacy stories for ages 4–8).
- **Internet Matters — "Privacy & identity"** advice and activities (free).

## 2. Being kind online

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Digital literacy / PSHE – kind behaviour

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- The same kindness rules apply online as in real life: be friendly, fair and respectful.
- Unkind words online (teasing, leaving people out, mean messages) hurt real feelings.
- If you see or receive something unkind, **tell a trusted adult** — and it's never your fault.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Understands the difference between kind and unkind behaviour offline.
- Knows what it feels like to be left out or upset.

### Teach it (the explanation)
There's a person on the other side of every screen — with feelings just like yours. So **the kindness rules are the same online as in the playground:** be friendly, share, take turns, and treat others the way you'd like to be treated.

Sometimes online it's easy to forget there's a real person there, and people type things they'd never say to your face — a mean comment, leaving someone out of a game, or a horrible message. That's **unkind behaviour**, and it can really hurt, even through a screen.

A good test before you send something: **"Would I be happy if this was said to me, out loud, in front of my family?"** If not, don't send it. And if someone is unkind *to* you, or you see them being unkind to someone else, you don't have to deal with it alone — **tell a trusted adult.** It isn't telling tales; it's the kind, brave thing to do, and it is never your fault.

**Worked example.** In a group chat, some friends start sending laughing-at messages about one child who isn't there. What can you do? (1) Don't join in. (2) You could send something kind instead. (3) Tell a trusted adult, because mean messages hurt and a grown-up can help stop them. Being a kind bystander matters.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Games:** good sportsmanship online — saying "well played," not gloating.
- **Messages:** thinking before you send, like thinking before you speak.
- **Friendship:** how it feels to be left out, and how to include people.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "It doesn't count / doesn't really hurt because it's just online."
  **Why:** the screen hides the other person's feelings.
  **Fix:** remind them a real person with real feelings reads every message.
- **Wrong idea:** "Telling an adult about a mean message is tattling."
  **Why:** fear of being seen as a tell-tale.
  **Fix:** distinguish "getting someone into trouble for fun" from "getting help to keep someone safe" — the second is always right.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What's a good question to ask yourself before sending a message? **A:** "Would I be happy if someone sent this to me?" / "Is it kind?"
- **Q:** Someone sends you a mean message. What should you do? **A:** Don't reply angrily; tell a trusted adult (and remember it's not your fault).

### Activity / practice
**Multiple choice / speak:** read short messages and decide "kind" or "unkind," then suggest a kinder way to say the unkind ones.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — "Being kind online" / "Cyberbullying."**
- **ThinkUKnow / Childline — "Online friendships & being kind"** (free, child-friendly).

## 3. What to do if something worries you

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Digital literacy / PSHE – getting help

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- If anything online ever worries, scares, confuses or upsets you, **tell a trusted adult straight away.**
- It is **never your fault**, and you will **never be in trouble** for telling.
- A simple plan: **stop, don't reply, keep it (don't delete), tell a trusted adult.**

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can name a trusted adult (parent, carer, teacher, relative).
- Understands feelings like worried, scared, upset.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The internet is mostly fun and friendly — but sometimes you might bump into something that makes you feel **worried, scared, confused or upset**: a horrible message, a video that frightens you, or someone asking you to do something that feels wrong. When that happens, here's the most important thing to know: **you should tell a trusted adult, and you will never be in trouble for it.** It is *never* your fault.

Have a simple plan ready, like a fire drill for online worries:
1. **Stop** — you don't have to keep looking.
2. **Don't reply** — especially to a stranger or a mean message.
3. **Keep it** — don't delete it; a grown-up may need to see it to help.
4. **Tell a trusted adult** — straight away.

Who's a trusted adult? Someone who looks after you and you feel safe with — a parent, carer, grandparent, teacher. It helps to name two or three so there's always someone to go to. (Older children can also know about Childline — a free, private helpline for any worry.)

**Worked example.** A message pops up from someone you don't know saying "don't tell your mum and dad about this." That secret-keeping is a big warning sign. The plan: **stop**, **don't reply**, **keep the message**, and **tell a trusted adult right away.** A grown-up will be glad you told them and will help.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Safety drill:** like knowing what to do in a fire — practise so it's automatic.
- **Helpers:** who are the helpers in their life they can always go to?
- **Bravery:** telling is the brave, strong thing to do.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "I'll get into trouble / lose my tablet if I tell."
  **Why:** fear of consequences keeps worries secret.
  **Fix:** reassure clearly and repeatedly — telling is always right and never gets *them* into trouble.
- **Wrong idea:** "If someone says 'keep it a secret,' I should."
  **Why:** children are taught to keep promises.
  **Fix:** explain that a grown-up online asking you to keep a secret *from your family* is a warning sign — always tell.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Something online makes you feel scared. What's the first thing to do? **A:** Tell a trusted adult (after stopping and not replying).
- **Q:** Will you get in trouble for telling a grown-up about something online that upset you? **A:** No — it's never your fault and telling is always the right thing.
- **Q:** Who are two trusted adults you could tell? **A:** Child names two (e.g. mum, teacher, grandma, carer).

### Activity / practice
**Speak / ordering:** the child names their trusted adults and puts the "worry plan" in order — stop, don't reply, keep it, tell a trusted adult.

### Resources
- **ThinkUKnow (CEOP) — "Tell a trusted adult"** resources; CEOP report tool for older children.
- **Childline (childline.org.uk) — free, confidential support** for children (mention to KS2).

## 4. Reliable vs unreliable information

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 3–6 (ages 7–11) · **Est. minutes:** 25 · **Subject strand:** Digital literacy – evaluating information

**Prerequisites:** none (links to Computing – search engines).

### Key concepts
- **Not everything online is true** — anyone can put anything on the internet.
- Reliable information comes from sources likely to **know** and to **check** their facts.
- To judge a source, ask: **Who made it? Why? Is it up to date? Do other good sources agree?**

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can read short web text.
- Understands the idea of true vs made-up.

### Teach it (the explanation)
On the internet, **anyone can write anything** — there's no rule that says everything online must be true. Some pages are made by experts who check their facts; others are jokes, adverts, mistakes or even deliberately false ("fake news"). So a smart reader doesn't believe something just because it's on a screen — they **check**.

Four detective questions help you judge a source:
- **Who** made it? An encyclopaedia, museum or news site is more likely to be right than a random post or an advert.
- **Why** was it made? To inform you, or to sell you something or make you laugh?
- **When** was it made? Old information can be out of date.
- Do **other good sources agree**? If two or three trustworthy places say the same, it's probably reliable.

**Worked example.** Two pages say different things about how long a goldfish lives. Page A is a vet/aquarium charity, explains its facts, and is dated this year. Page B is an advert for fish food with no author and a wild claim. Which is more reliable? **Page A** — a knowledgeable source, made to inform, recent. We'd still check a second trustworthy source agrees before being sure.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Animals/space:** checking an amazing "fact" about a creature or planet.
- **Detective:** being a fact-detective who looks for clues about a source.
- **Adverts:** spotting when a page is really just trying to sell something.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "If it's written down / online, it's true."
  **Why:** print and screens carry authority for young children.
  **Fix:** show two pages that disagree — they can't both be right, so we must check.
- **Wrong idea:** "The most exciting or shocking claim is the real one."
  **Why:** dramatic content grabs attention.
  **Fix:** teach that surprising claims need *more* checking, not less; look for who said it and whether others agree.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Is everything you read on the internet true? **A:** No — anyone can put anything online, so we have to check.
- **Q:** Give two questions to check if a web page is reliable. **A:** Any two of: Who made it? Why? When? Do other good sources agree?

### Activity / practice
**Categorize / multiple choice:** given a topic and a few "sources" (a museum page, a joke site, an advert, a news site), the child ranks them most- to least-reliable and explains using the detective questions.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — "Fake news" / "Is it true? Reliable information online."**
- **Internet Matters — "Critical thinking & fake news"** primary resources (free).

## 5. Screen-time balance

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 1–6 (ages 5–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** PSHE / digital literacy – healthy habits

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- Screens are fun and useful, **and** so are other things — balance is key.
- Too much screen time can crowd out sleep, play, exercise and time with people.
- Healthy habits: take breaks, stop well before bed, and have screen-free activities you love.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Uses screens for games, videos or learning.
- Knows some non-screen activities they enjoy.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Screens are great — for games, videos, learning and chatting. But they're a bit like sweets: lovely in the right amount, not so good if it's *all* you have. A healthy day has **balance**: some screen time *and* plenty of other good things — running about, reading, building, drawing, playing with friends, and sleep.

Why balance? Lots of screen time can quietly eat up the time you'd spend moving, sleeping or being with people, and screens close to bedtime can make it harder to fall asleep (the bright light tells your brain it's still daytime). So good habits help:
- **Take breaks** — every so often, look away and move (rest your eyes and body).
- **Screen-free before bed** — switch off a while before sleep.
- **Have favourites that aren't screens** — so it's easy to choose something else.

Apps and games are *designed* to keep you tapping, so it's normal to find it hard to stop — that's not your fault, it just means we plan ahead.

**Worked example.** Plan a balanced Saturday: morning bike ride (move), some screen time for a favourite game (fun), lunch, building or drawing (create), reading before a screen-free bedtime (rest). Notice screens are *part* of the day, not the whole day — that's balance.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Sport/outdoors:** the active, screen-free things they love.
- **Sweets analogy:** screens are a treat best enjoyed in balance.
- **Sleep:** how a good night's sleep helps everything the next day.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "More screen time is always more fun / better."
  **Why:** the immediate reward feels great.
  **Fix:** notice how they feel *after* lots of screen time vs after a mix; balance feels better.
- **Wrong idea:** "I should be able to just stop whenever — if I can't, something's wrong with me."
  **Why:** self-blame about a normal struggle.
  **Fix:** explain apps are built to be hard to put down; that's why we set break times and plans in advance.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Why is balance between screens and other activities important? **A:** So we still get sleep, exercise, play and time with people — and feel good.
- **Q:** Name one healthy screen habit. **A:** e.g. take breaks, stop a while before bed, have screen-free activities you enjoy.

### Activity / practice
**Categorize / build:** the child plans a balanced day, sorting activities into "screen" and "screen-free," and names one favourite screen-free thing to do.

### Resources
- **Internet Matters — "Screen time"** advice and family tips (free).
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — "Healthy habits / balancing screen time."**

## 6. Age-appropriate choices online

**Year / Key Stage:** Years 2–6 (ages 6–11) · **Est. minutes:** 20 · **Subject strand:** Digital literacy / PSHE – suitable content

**Prerequisites:** What to do if something worries you.

### Key concepts
- Some games, videos and apps are made for **older** people, not for you yet.
- **Age ratings** (like PEGI numbers) are helpful labels that say who content is suitable for.
- If something feels too grown-up, scary or "not for me," **stop and check with a trusted adult.**

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has seen games or videos with numbers/age labels.
- Knows the feeling of something being "too scary" or "too grown-up."

### Teach it (the explanation)
Not everything online is made for children — just like some films at the cinema are for grown-ups. **Age ratings** are friendly labels that tell you who something is suitable for. On games you might see **PEGI** numbers like PEGI 3, 7, 12, 16 or 18 — the number is the *youngest* age it's made for. A PEGI 18 game is for adults, not for a 9-year-old, even if friends are playing it. Videos and apps often have age guidance too.

These labels exist to look after you — they warn that content might be too scary, too violent or too grown-up. So a good habit is: **check the age rating, and if something is older than you, or just feels wrong, scary or too grown-up, stop and ask a trusted adult.** Choosing things made for your age isn't being treated like a baby — it's being sensible, and it keeps the internet fun.

**Worked example.** Friends are talking about a PEGI 18 game. You're 9. What's the wise choice? The "18" means it's made for adults, so it's **not for you yet** — even if friends play it. Tell a trusted adult, and choose a game that's right for your age. There are loads of brilliant games made just for you.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- **Films:** cinema age ratings they may already know (U, PG, 12).
- **Games:** the PEGI number on a favourite game's box or store page.
- **Growing up:** some things are to look forward to when older.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "If my friends play it, it must be okay for me."
  **Why:** peer behaviour feels like permission.
  **Fix:** the rating is about *age*, not popularity; checking with a trusted adult is the sensible move.
- **Wrong idea:** "Age ratings are just to spoil my fun."
  **Why:** sees the label as a barrier.
  **Fix:** explain ratings protect you from content that could upset you, and there's plenty made just for your age.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does a PEGI number on a game tell you? **A:** The youngest age the game is made/suitable for.
- **Q:** A game is rated for ages older than you. What's the wise thing to do? **A:** Don't play it; check with a trusted adult and pick something right for your age.

### Activity / practice
**Multiple choice / speak:** given a few games/videos with age labels and a child's age, decide which are suitable and explain why; practise saying "I'll check with a trusted adult."

### Resources
- **Internet Matters — "Age ratings & PEGI"** parent/child guidance (free).
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 — "Age ratings / what's suitable online."**
