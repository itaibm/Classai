# Geography — Key Stage 1 (Years 1–2, ages 5–7)

KS1 geography moves from **local → national → world**, building an internal map and globe and the first skills: simple maps, the four compass points, hot/cold places, and the weather. Keep a **globe and a UK map** in view at all times; every fact gets pinned to one of them.

---

## 1. The United Kingdom — countries, capitals & surrounding seas

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–2 (ages 5–7) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Geography – locational knowledge (UK)

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- The **United Kingdom (UK)** is made of **four countries**: **England, Scotland, Wales** and **Northern Ireland**.
- Each has a **capital city**: London (England), Edinburgh (Scotland), Cardiff (Wales), Belfast (Northern Ireland).
- The UK is **surrounded by sea** (it's a group of islands): the North Sea, the Irish Sea, the English Channel and the Atlantic Ocean.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the name of their own town and roughly that they live in the UK.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Where do you live? In a town, in a **country**, in the **United Kingdom** — the **UK** for short. The UK isn't one country; it's **four countries joined together**: **England, Scotland, Wales** and **Northern Ireland**. Point to each on the map: England (the biggest, in the south and middle), Scotland (to the north), Wales (to the west), and Northern Ireland (across the Irish Sea, on the island of Ireland).

Each country has a **capital city** — its most important city, where its leaders work. London is the capital of England (and the biggest city in the UK), Edinburgh is Scotland's, Cardiff is Wales's, and Belfast is Northern Ireland's.

Now look at the edges of the map — all blue! The UK is a group of **islands**, so it's **surrounded by sea**. The **North Sea** is to the east, the **Irish Sea** sits between Britain and Ireland, the **English Channel** is the narrow sea to the south (between England and France), and the wide **Atlantic Ocean** is to the west.

**Worked example.** Match each country to its capital: England → London; Scotland → Edinburgh; Wales → Cardiff; Northern Ireland → Belfast. (A memory trick: the capital is usually where that country's flag and government live.)

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Find where the learner lives on a UK map — which of the four countries is it in?
- Flags: each country has its own flag, and together they make the Union Jack.
- A football/rugby fan: the four countries have separate national teams.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "England, Britain and the UK all mean the same thing."
  **Why:** the words get used loosely.
  **Fix:** **England** is one country; **Great Britain** is the big island (England + Scotland + Wales); the **UK** is all four countries including Northern Ireland. Show each on the map.
- **Wrong idea:** "London is the capital of the whole UK and the only capital."
  **Why:** London is the biggest and most famous.
  **Fix:** London is England's capital (and largest UK city), but Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own capitals too.
- **Wrong idea:** the UK is joined to mainland Europe by land.
  **Why:** it looks close on the map.
  **Fix:** it's **islands**, surrounded by sea — you cross water (or the Channel Tunnel) to reach France.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name the four countries of the UK. **A:** England, Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland.
- **Q:** What is the capital city of Scotland? **A:** Edinburgh.
- **Q:** Is the UK surrounded by land or sea? Name one sea around it. **A:** Sea (it's islands); e.g. the North Sea, Irish Sea, English Channel, or Atlantic Ocean.
- **Q:** Are "England" and "the UK" the same thing? **A:** No — England is one of the four countries in the UK.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs block.* Match each country to its capital, then *labelling*: drag the four country names onto a blank UK map. Optionally name one surrounding sea.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Geography — "The United Kingdom"** (four countries, capitals, surrounding seas).
- **National Geographic Kids — "United Kingdom country profile"** (facts and a map).

## 2. Continents & oceans of the world

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–2 (ages 5–7) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Geography – locational knowledge (world)

**Prerequisites:** The United Kingdom — countries, capitals & surrounding seas.

### Key concepts
- The world's land is grouped into **seven continents**: Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Europe, North America, South America, Oceania (Australasia).
- The world's water is grouped into **five oceans**: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern (Antarctic) and Arctic.
- A **globe** is the truest map of the Earth (a sphere); a **world map** is the same thing flattened out.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can find the UK on a map; understands "land" vs "sea."

### Teach it (the explanation)
Zoom out from the UK to the whole **Earth**, which is a giant ball (a **sphere**) — that's why a **globe** shows it best. Spin it and you'll see the land sits in big chunks called **continents**, and the water in huge stretches called **oceans**.

There are **seven continents**. The biggest is **Asia**; then **Africa**, **North America**, **South America**, **Antarctica** (the frozen one at the bottom), **Europe** (where the UK is — quite a small continent), and **Oceania/Australasia** (Australia and nearby islands). A rhyme can help, or just keep finding them on the globe.

There are **five oceans**. The **Pacific** is the largest (it's huge), then the **Atlantic** (the one to the west of the UK), the **Indian** Ocean, the **Southern** Ocean (around Antarctica) and the **Arctic** Ocean (the icy one at the top). Notice the UK is a tiny set of islands in **Europe**, beside the **Atlantic**.

**Worked example.** Find the UK on the globe: it's in the continent of **Europe**, with the **Atlantic Ocean** to its west. Now find the biggest ocean (**Pacific**) and the coldest, iciest continent (**Antarctica**). Pointing things out on the globe is the skill here.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "Where do your favourite animals live?" — penguins (Antarctica), lions (Africa), kangaroos (Oceania).
- Spin a globe and stop it with a finger: "what continent did you land on?"
- A favourite holiday or family abroad: which continent is it in?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "A country and a continent are the same."
  **Why:** both are big areas on the map.
  **Fix:** a **continent** is a giant area of land holding **many countries** (e.g. Africa contains dozens of countries); the UK is a country *in* the continent of Europe.
- **Wrong idea:** Australia/Antarctica are countries or aren't continents.
  **Why:** Australia is a country *and* the main land of a continent.
  **Fix:** clarify Oceania/Australasia and Antarctica are continents; Antarctica has no countries, just ice and research bases.
- **Wrong idea:** the flat world map shows true sizes/shapes perfectly.
  **Why:** maps look exact.
  **Fix:** flattening a ball stretches the edges (e.g. it makes Antarctica/Greenland look huge); a **globe** is more truthful.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How many continents are there, and which one is the UK in? **A:** Seven; the UK is in Europe.
- **Q:** Name two oceans. **A:** Any two of: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern, Arctic.
- **Q:** Which continent is the coldest and covered in ice? **A:** Antarctica.
- **Q:** What's the difference between a country and a continent? **A:** A continent is a huge area of land holding many countries.

### Activity / practice
*Labelling / matchPairs.* Drag the seven continent names and five ocean names onto a blank world map. Then match animals to continents (penguin→Antarctica, kangaroo→Oceania, lion→Africa).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Geography — "Continents and oceans"** (the seven continents and five oceans).
- **National Geographic Kids — "Continents"** and **"Oceans"** (maps, facts, animals).

## 3. Hot & cold places — the Equator and the Poles

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–2 (ages 5–7) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Geography – hot & cold areas of the world

**Prerequisites:** Continents & oceans of the world.

### Key concepts
- Places near the **Equator** (an imaginary line around the middle of the Earth) are **hot**; places near the **North and South Poles** (top and bottom) are **cold**.
- This is because of how **sunlight hits the Earth**: directly and strongly at the middle, slanted and weakly at the poles.
- Different climates mean different **plants, animals and ways of living** (rainforest near the Equator; ice and penguins/polar bears at the poles).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can find continents and the poles on a globe; knows what hot and cold feel like.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Look at the globe again. Round the **middle** runs an imaginary line called the **Equator**. The places along it — like parts of Africa and South America — are the **hottest** on Earth, with steamy **rainforests**. At the very **top** (the **North Pole**, in the Arctic) and the very **bottom** (the **South Pole**, in Antarctica) it's **freezing cold**, covered in ice and snow all year.

Why? Because of the sunlight. The Earth is a **ball**, so near the middle the sun shines **straight down**, strong and direct — very hot. Near the top and bottom the same sunlight arrives at a **slant**, spread thin and weak — so it's cold. (You can show this with a torch shone straight on, then at an angle, on a ball.)

Hot and cold places suit different life. Near the Equator: thick **rainforests**, monkeys, parrots, lots of rain. At the poles: **ice**, with **polar bears** in the Arctic (north) and **penguins** in Antarctica (south) — and people there wear thick clothes and live very differently from people at the Equator.

**Worked example.** "Why is it hot at the Equator and cold at the poles?" Because the Earth is round: at the **middle**, sunlight hits **straight on** (strong → hot); at the **poles**, sunlight hits at a **slant** (weak, spread out → cold). Same sun, different angle.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Polar animals (penguins, polar bears) vs rainforest animals (monkeys, parrots) — "where would each live?"
- The torch-and-ball demo: feel how straight light is stronger than slanted light.
- Clothes: what would you pack for the Equator vs the North Pole?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "It's hot at the Equator because it's closer to the Sun there."
  **Why:** sounds logical — closer = hotter.
  **Fix:** the whole Earth is basically the same distance from the Sun; it's the **angle** of the sunlight (straight vs slanted) that makes the middle hot and the poles cold.
- **Wrong idea:** "Polar bears and penguins live together."
  **Why:** both are "ice animals."
  **Fix:** polar bears live in the **Arctic (North)**, penguins in **Antarctica (South)** — opposite ends of the Earth; they never meet in the wild.
- **Wrong idea:** "Everywhere is either hot or cold."
  **Why:** the two extremes are the focus.
  **Fix:** in between (like the UK) it's **mild** — not very hot or very cold, with four seasons.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Where on Earth is it hottest, and what is that line called? **A:** Around the middle, on the Equator.
- **Q:** Why are the poles so cold? **A:** Sunlight hits them at a slant (weak and spread out), unlike the strong straight sunlight at the Equator.
- **Q:** Where do polar bears live — the North Pole or the South Pole? **A:** The North (the Arctic); penguins live in the South (Antarctica).
- **Q:** Is the UK a very hot or very cold place? **A:** Neither — it's mild/in between, with four seasons.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize block.* Sort pictures/animals into "Hot place (near the Equator)" or "Cold place (near the Poles)" — e.g. monkey, polar bear, rainforest, igloo, penguin, parrot. Then *multipleChoice* on why the poles are cold.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Geography — "Hot and cold places"** (Equator, poles, why it's hot/cold).
- **National Geographic Kids — "The Arctic" / "Antarctica"** (polar life and animals).

## 4. My local area — simple maps, aerial views & the compass

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–2 (ages 5–7) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** Geography – maps, fieldwork & compass directions

**Prerequisites:** none (best taught early; revisited).

### Key concepts
- A **map** is a drawing of a place seen from **above** (a bird's-eye view), shrunk down and simplified.
- Maps use **symbols** and a **key** to show real things (a tree symbol = trees; blue = water).
- The **four main compass directions** are **North, South, East, West** — they help us say where things are and which way to go.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows their own classroom/home and can talk about left/right, near/far.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Imagine a bird flying high above your school, looking straight down. What it sees — the roofs, the playground, the paths — is what a **map** shows: a **bird's-eye view** from **above**. A map is also **shrunk** (a whole town fits on one page) and **simplified** (it leaves out the small details and uses little pictures).

Those little pictures are **symbols**, and a **key** tells you what each one means (a blue line = a river; a green patch = a park; a small tree = woods). So you read a map by checking the key.

To say **where** things are, geographers use the four **compass directions**: **North, South, East and West**. North is usually the **top** of the map. A helpful order going clockwise is **N–E–S–W**: "**N**ever **E**at **S**oggy **W**eetabix." A **compass** always points North, so you can line up your map with the real world.

**Worked example.** Make a simple map of your bedroom or classroom from above: draw a rectangle for the room, then mark the door, the bed/desk and the window as little symbols, and add a key. Put an "N" arrow at the top. You've made a map: bird's-eye, shrunk, simplified, with a key and a direction.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Draw a treasure map of the garden/home with a key and an X.
- Use a real or phone compass to find which way North is from the front door.
- Look at the home/school on a satellite map app — "that's the bird's-eye view!"

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** a map should look like a normal photo from the side (you can see the front of houses).
  **Why:** children draw what they see at eye level.
  **Fix:** a map is from **straight above** — you see **roofs and tops**, not fronts; show an aerial photo to prove it.
- **Wrong idea:** the symbols/colours on a map are the real thing ("the blue line *is* a river you could fall in").
  **Why:** confusing the symbol with reality.
  **Fix:** the map is a **drawing that stands for** real things — the blue line *represents* the river; the key explains the code.
- **Wrong idea:** "North" means "up / the way I'm facing."
  **Why:** "up the map" feels like "up."
  **Fix:** North is a fixed direction (a compass always points to it), not "up" or "in front of me"; turning your body doesn't move North.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does a map show — the view from the side or from above? **A:** From above (a bird's-eye view).
- **Q:** What is a map *key* for? **A:** It explains what the symbols/colours on the map mean.
- **Q:** Name the four main compass directions. **A:** North, South, East, West.
- **Q:** Which direction is usually at the top of a map? **A:** North.

### Activity / practice
*Drawing + labelling.* Child draws a simple bird's-eye map of one room or the playground with at least three symbols and a key, then adds a North arrow. Or a *matchPairs*: symbol → meaning (tree symbol→woods, blue→water, green→park).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Geography — "Maps and the compass"** (bird's-eye view, symbols, keys, N/E/S/W).
- **Ordnance Survey — "MapZone" / OS Mapzone games** (kid-friendly intro to map symbols, free).

## 5. Weather & seasons in the UK

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1–2 (ages 5–7) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** Geography – weather and seasons

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- **Weather** is what the sky and air are doing **today** (sunny, rainy, windy, snowy) — and it changes day to day.
- The UK has **four seasons** that come in order: **spring, summer, autumn, winter**, each with typical weather.
- We can **observe and record** the weather (with symbols and simple measuring), which is what weather forecasters do.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Notices whether it's sunny, rainy, hot or cold today; knows the days of the week.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Look out of the window. Is it **sunny, cloudy, rainy, windy** or **snowy**? That's the **weather** — what the air and sky are doing **right now**, and it can change from morning to afternoon and day to day. In the UK the weather is famously changeable!

Over a year the weather follows a pattern of **four seasons**, always in the same order: **spring** (warmer, plants grow, lambs born), **summer** (warmest, longest days, sunny), **autumn** (cooler, leaves turn brown and fall, windy), and **winter** (coldest, shortest days, sometimes frost and snow), then back to spring. The seasons happen because of how the Earth tilts as it travels round the Sun (just the idea at KS1).

Grown-ups who study weather are **forecasters** — they **observe and record** it. You can be one too: each day, draw a weather **symbol** (sun, cloud, raindrops), see if it's hot or cold, and watch which way the wind blows. Over a week you build a weather chart, just like a real forecast.

**Worked example.** Keep a weather chart for one week: each day, pick the symbol that matches (☀ sunny, ☁ cloudy, 🌧 rainy) and note hot/cold. At the end, count: "We had 3 rainy days, 2 sunny, 2 cloudy." That's observing and recording weather like a forecaster.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A daily "weather reporter" job: present today's weather with symbols.
- Seasonal favourites: building snowmen (winter), conkers (autumn), the beach (summer).
- Make a windsock or watch a flag to see wind direction.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "weather" and "climate/seasons" are the same.
  **Why:** both about hot/cold/rain.
  **Fix:** **weather** is *today* and changes fast; the **seasons** are the year-long pattern. One rainy summer day doesn't stop it being summer.
- **Wrong idea:** "It's summer everywhere in the world at the same time."
  **Why:** assuming the whole Earth shares one season.
  **Fix:** when it's summer in the UK (north), it's **winter** in places like Australia (south) — opposite seasons.
- **Wrong idea:** the seasons always come in different orders / can skip.
  **Why:** muddled sequence.
  **Fix:** the order is fixed — spring → summer → autumn → winter → spring, every year.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is the difference between weather and the seasons? **A:** Weather is what the sky is doing today (and changes fast); seasons are the year-long pattern.
- **Q:** Name the four seasons in order. **A:** Spring, summer, autumn, winter.
- **Q:** In which season do leaves fall off the trees in the UK? **A:** Autumn.
- **Q:** Name two types of weather. **A:** Any two of: sunny, rainy, cloudy, windy, snowy, foggy.

### Activity / practice
*Matching + ordering.* Order the four seasons; match each season to a typical picture (lambs→spring, beach→summer, falling leaves→autumn, snow→winter). Start a one-week weather chart with daily symbols.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 Geography — "Weather and seasons"** (types of weather, the four seasons, recording weather).
- **Met Office — Weather for Kids / education pages** (weather symbols, how forecasting works, free).
