# History — Key Stage 2 (Years 3–6, ages 7–11)

KS2 history is a roughly **chronological journey** through British and world history, taught through the disciplines: chronology, cause & consequence, similarity & difference, sources & evidence, significance. Children now use **BC/AD**, rough dates and centuries, and weigh sources more carefully. Keep returning to a long timeline so the periods stay in order (they are easy to muddle).

A quick AD/BC reminder for the tutor: **BC** = Before Christ (years count *down* toward 0); **AD** = Anno Domini, the years counting up from then. "BCE/CE" mean the same as BC/AD. So 3000 BC is older than 500 BC, which is older than AD 43.

## 1. Stone Age to Iron Age Britain

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3 (ages 7–8) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – British prehistory

**Prerequisites:** none (start of the KS2 chronology).

### Key concepts
- **Prehistory** = before writing, so we know it only from objects, bones and monuments — not books. Three long ages named after the best tool material: **Stone Age → Bronze Age → Iron Age**.
- The biggest change: people went from **hunter-gatherers** (following animals, gathering plants) to **farmers** who settled, grew crops and kept animals — the start of villages.
- Better materials (stone → bronze → iron) meant better tools and weapons, more farming and more trade.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Understands "a long time ago," that we learn about the past from sources, and can place events in order.

### Teach it (the explanation)
This is the deepest past — thousands of years, and **no writing at all**. So how do we know anything? From **archaeology**: digging up tools, bones, pots and monuments. That makes it detective work.

The **Stone Age** is the longest stretch. Early on, people were **hunter-gatherers**: they had no farms, so they moved around hunting animals and gathering berries, nuts and roots, using sharp **stone** tools and fire. Then came the huge change — the **Neolithic** ("new stone") revolution: people learned to **farm**. Now they could stay in one place, grow grain and keep animals. Staying put meant building homes, storing food, and making big monuments — like **Stonehenge**, raised around 4,500 years ago (we still aren't sure exactly how or why — another mystery from sources).

Next, people learned to mix metals to make **bronze** (the **Bronze Age**) — better tools, weapons and jewellery, and more trading. Later still they mastered **iron**, which is harder (the **Iron Age**). Iron Age people built **hillforts** and lived in round houses; tribes farmed and fought. This is roughly where Britain was when the Romans arrived.

**Worked example.** Why did farming change everything? Trace the chain: people learn to grow crops and keep animals → they don't need to follow the animals → they **settle** in one place → they build homes and store food → villages and bigger monuments appear. So: farming → settling down → the first permanent communities.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "Survival" angle: how would *you* get food with no shops — what would you hunt or gather?
- Look at a photo of Stonehenge; treat it as a 4,500-year-old puzzle ("how did they move stones that heavy?").
- Tools: handle (or picture) a flint hand-axe vs an iron knife — why iron wins.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Cavemen lived with dinosaurs."
  **Why:** films and toys mix the two.
  **Fix:** dinosaurs died out about **66 million** years ago; the first humans came **millions** of years later — they never met. Put both on a (very stretched) timeline.
- **Wrong idea:** Stone Age people were stupid grunting brutes.
  **Why:** "primitive" is read as "unintelligent."
  **Fix:** they invented fire-use, tools, farming, art and Stonehenge — extremely clever with the materials they had.
- **Wrong idea:** the three ages had sharp start/end dates everywhere at once.
  **Why:** neat labels suggest neat switches.
  **Fix:** explain the ages overlapped and arrived at different times in different places; the names just mark the *best material* people used.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Why is it called *pre*history, and how do we learn about it? **A:** It's before writing, so we use archaeology — tools, bones, pots and monuments, not books.
- **Q:** Order these: Iron Age, Stone Age, Bronze Age. **A:** Stone Age, Bronze Age, Iron Age (oldest first).
- **Q:** What was the biggest change in the Stone Age and why did it matter? **A:** People learned to farm, so they could settle in one place — the start of villages.
- **Q:** Did Stone Age people live with dinosaurs? Explain. **A:** No — dinosaurs died out millions of years before humans existed.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + categorize.* First order the three ages oldest→newest; then sort items (flint axe, bronze sword, iron knife, hunting wild deer, growing wheat) by which age/way of life they belong to.

## 2. Ancient Egypt

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–4 (ages 7–9) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – early civilisations

**Prerequisites:** Stone Age to Iron Age Britain (the idea of long-ago civilisations and sources).

### Key concepts
- One of the world's first great **civilisations** (~5,000 years ago), built along the **River Nile**, whose yearly flood made the land fertile for farming.
- Ruled by a **pharaoh** (king, seen as part-god); famous for **pyramids** (royal tombs), **hieroglyphics** (picture-writing), and **mummies** (preserving bodies for the afterlife).
- We know so much because Egyptians **wrote things down** and buried objects in dry tombs — superbly preserved sources, like Tutankhamun's tomb.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows that early people farmed and settled, and that we use sources/archaeology to learn about the past.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Run your finger down a map of north-east Africa and find the long blue line of the **River Nile**. Egypt is mostly desert, so why did a great civilisation grow there? Because once a year the Nile **flooded**, then dropped, leaving rich black mud on the banks — perfect for **farming**. Reliable food meant a big, organised society could grow.

Egypt was ruled by a **pharaoh**, a king the people believed was chosen by the gods. The Egyptians worshipped **many gods** (like Ra the sun god) and believed strongly in an **afterlife** — life after death. To prepare a body for it, they made **mummies**: drying and wrapping the body so it lasted. The richest, like pharaohs, were buried with treasures inside giant stone **pyramids** — among the most astonishing buildings ever made, by hand, thousands of years ago.

They invented a picture-writing called **hieroglyphics**, carved on tombs and written on **papyrus** (paper from a reed). For ages nobody could read it — until the **Rosetta Stone** gave the key. Because Egyptians wrote so much and their dry tombs preserved everything, we have brilliant sources, like the tomb of the boy-king **Tutankhamun**, found nearly untouched in 1922.

**Worked example.** "Why did Egypt grow up beside the Nile?" Chain it: desert all around → little water → but the Nile floods yearly → leaves fertile mud → good farming → plenty of food → a large, organised civilisation can live there. So the river is the reason the whole thing exists.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Mummies and tombs — the "spooky but real" hook; how and why bodies were preserved.
- Crack a code: write the child's name in hieroglyphics.
- Pyramids as an engineering puzzle ("how, with no machines?").

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The pyramids were built by aliens / it's impossible by hand."
  **Why:** the scale seems unbelievable.
  **Fix:** thousands of skilled, organised workers over years, with ramps, levers and huge planning — humans, not aliens.
- **Wrong idea:** mummies were made because Egyptians were "creepy."
  **Why:** judging by modern feelings.
  **Fix:** it came from a serious religious belief in the afterlife — they preserved the body so the person could live on.
- **Wrong idea:** Ancient Egypt and the Romans/Greeks were all "the same time, long ago."
  **Why:** all of antiquity blurs together.
  **Fix:** place Egypt on the timeline — it's *much* older; the pyramids were already ancient when the Romans came.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Why was the River Nile so important to the Egyptians? **A:** Its yearly flood left fertile mud for farming, so people could grow food in the desert.
- **Q:** What were the pyramids for? **A:** Giant tombs for pharaohs, with treasures for the afterlife.
- **Q:** Why do we know so much about Ancient Egypt? **A:** They wrote (hieroglyphics) and dry tombs preserved objects and bodies — rich sources like Tutankhamun's tomb.
- **Q:** What is a mummy and why did Egyptians make them? **A:** A preserved, wrapped body — made because they believed in an afterlife.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs / categorize.* Match terms to meanings: pharaoh → god-king ruler; pyramid → royal tomb; hieroglyphics → picture-writing; mummy → preserved body; Nile → river that flooded to feed farming.

## 3. Ancient Greece

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–5 (ages 8–10) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – Ancient Greece and its legacy

**Prerequisites:** Ancient Egypt (idea of an early civilisation; BC dates).

### Key concepts
- Ancient Greece (~2,500 years ago) was not one country but many **city-states** (like **Athens** and **Sparta**) that shared a language, gods and the **Olympic Games**.
- Greek ideas still shape our world: **democracy** (rule by the people, born in Athens), early **science, maths and philosophy**, theatre, and famous **myths**.
- **Athens vs Sparta** is a great similarity/difference study: Athens prized learning, art and democracy; Sparta prized war and toughness.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Comfortable with BC dating and the idea that civilisations leave a *legacy* we still use.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Around **2,500 years ago**, the land we call Greece wasn't one kingdom — it was lots of separate **city-states**, each its own little country with its own rulers and army, often quarrelling. What they shared was a language, the same **gods** (Zeus, Athena, Poseidon and more, who star in Greek **myths**), and the **Olympic Games**, a sports festival every four years — yes, the ancestor of today's Olympics.

Two city-states are worth comparing. **Athens** loved **learning, art, theatre and ideas** — and invented **democracy**, where (some) citizens voted on decisions instead of obeying a single king. **Sparta** was the opposite: a tough warrior state where boys trained as soldiers from childhood and toughness mattered most.

The Greeks' biggest gift is their **legacy** — ideas we still use. **Democracy** (we still vote), early **philosophy** (thinkers like Socrates asking "how should we live?"), **maths and science** (Pythagoras, Archimedes), and the **theatre** with its comedies and tragedies. We even still tell their myths.

**Worked example.** "How is Ancient Greece still part of our lives?" List the legacy: we hold the **Olympics**; many countries use **democracy** and voting; we study Greek **maths** (Pythagoras) and **myths**; words like *democracy* and *politics* are Greek. So a civilisation from 2,500 years ago still shapes today.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- The Olympics — connect to a sporty learner; then-and-now of the Games.
- Greek myths (Heracles, the Minotaur, the Trojan Horse) for a story-lover.
- "Vote on it" — run a tiny class vote to feel what democracy means.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Ancient Greece was one big country with one king."
  **Why:** modern Greece is one nation, so the past is assumed the same.
  **Fix:** it was many independent **city-states** that often fought each other and only shared culture.
- **Wrong idea:** Athenian democracy meant *everyone* could vote.
  **Why:** "democracy" is read as today's version.
  **Fix:** only free adult **male citizens** voted; women, enslaved people and foreigners could not — an important honest limit.
- **Wrong idea:** the Greek gods/myths were just "fairy tales they didn't take seriously."
  **Why:** we treat myths as stories.
  **Fix:** these were their **real religion** — temples, festivals and worship, central to daily life.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a *city-state*, and name two famous Greek ones. **A:** An independent city that ran like its own country; Athens and Sparta.
- **Q:** What does *democracy* mean, and which city-state began it? **A:** Rule by the people (citizens vote on decisions); it began in Athens.
- **Q:** Give two things from Ancient Greece we still have today. **A:** Any two of: the Olympic Games, democracy/voting, maths and science ideas, theatre, myths.
- **Q:** Name one big difference between Athens and Sparta. **A:** Athens valued learning, art and democracy; Sparta valued war and toughness.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize block.* Sort statements into "Athens" or "Sparta" (loved learning and art / trained boys as soldiers / invented democracy / valued toughness above all). Then a *multipleChoice*: "Which modern thing came from Ancient Greece?" (the Olympics / democracy).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 History — "Ancient Greece"** (city-states, gods, Athens vs Sparta, legacy).
- **National Geographic Kids — "Ancient Greece facts"** (gods, Olympics, daily life).
- **BBC Teach — "Ancient Greece"** clips (democracy, the Olympics, myths).

## 4. The Roman Empire and its impact on Britain

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4 (ages 8–9) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – Romans in Britain

**Prerequisites:** Stone Age to Iron Age Britain (what Britain was like before); Ancient Greece (empires & legacy).

### Key concepts
- Rome grew into a huge **empire** ruling much of Europe; its powerful, organised **army** **invaded Britain in AD 43** and ruled most of it for ~400 years.
- The Romans changed Britain hugely: **roads**, **towns** (like London), **baths**, central heating, coins, writing and the first cities — much of which lasted.
- **Resistance & two sides:** not everyone welcomed them — **Queen Boudica** led a famous British revolt; **Hadrian's Wall** was built to keep northern tribes out.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows Iron Age Britain had tribes and hillforts; understands AD dating and what an "empire" is.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Before the Romans, Britain was a land of **Iron Age tribes** in roundhouses and hillforts. Then, in **AD 43**, the mighty **Roman Empire** — already ruling lands all round the Mediterranean — **invaded**. Their secret was a superbly trained, organised **army** (legions) with armour, tactics and discipline. They conquered most of Britain and stayed for about **400 years**.

What did they change? Almost everything. They built **straight roads** (some still under our roads today) to move armies and trade fast — hence the saying "all roads lead to Rome." They built the first proper **towns and cities**, including **Londinium** (London), with stone buildings, **public baths**, **central heating** (hypocausts), markets, coins and writing. They brought new foods and ways of living.

But it wasn't all welcome. Some Britons fought back. **Queen Boudica** of the Iceni tribe led a fierce revolt around AD 60, burning Roman towns, before being defeated. And to stop raids from northern tribes (in what's now Scotland), the Romans built **Hadrian's Wall** right across the north of Britain — you can still walk along it. Eventually Rome itself weakened and the army left around AD 410, leaving its roads, towns and ruins behind.

**Worked example.** "What did the Romans leave behind in Britain?" List the lasting impact: **roads** (still beneath modern ones), **towns/cities** (London began as Londinium), **bathhouses and heating**, **coins and writing**, even Hadrian's Wall. So even after the army left, Roman things shaped Britain for centuries.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Roman soldiers/army — armour, shields, the "tortoise" formation for a battle-minded learner.
- Roman baths and underfloor heating — "they had heated floors 2,000 years ago!"
- Boudica as a fierce warrior-queen story; Hadrian's Wall on a map.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The Romans came from Britain / Rome is in Britain."
  **Why:** "Roman Britain" makes Rome sound British.
  **Fix:** Rome is in **Italy**; the Romans **came from abroad** and conquered Britain as part of a huge empire.
- **Wrong idea:** "Everyone in Britain was happy the Romans came."
  **Why:** the "they brought roads and baths" story sounds purely good.
  **Fix:** it was an **invasion**; many fought back, like **Boudica**, and Hadrian's Wall shows ongoing conflict.
- **Wrong idea:** the Romans built nothing that lasted.
  **Why:** it was "ages ago."
  **Fix:** roads, town foundations, baths and walls survive — London itself began as a Roman town.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** In what year did the Romans invade Britain, and where did they come from? **A:** AD 43; they came from Rome/Italy as part of the Roman Empire.
- **Q:** Give two things the Romans built or brought to Britain that lasted. **A:** Any two of: roads, towns/cities (e.g. London), baths/central heating, coins, writing, Hadrian's Wall.
- **Q:** Who was Boudica? **A:** A British queen (of the Iceni) who led a revolt against the Romans.
- **Q:** Why did the Romans build Hadrian's Wall? **A:** To defend Roman Britain from tribes to the north (in what's now Scotland).

### Activity / practice
*Categorize / matchPairs.* Sort "before the Romans" vs "after the Romans came" (hillforts & roundhouses vs straight roads, towns, baths, coins). Match: Boudica → British revolt; Hadrian's Wall → northern defence; Londinium → London; AD 43 → the invasion.

- **National Geographic Kids — "Roman Britain facts"** (army, daily life, towns).

## 5. Anglo-Saxons and Scots

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – settlement of Britain after Rome

**Prerequisites:** The Roman Empire and its impact on Britain.

### Key concepts
- After the Romans left (~AD 410), tribes from across the North Sea — **Angles, Saxons and Jutes** — settled in Britain; their name gives us "**England**" (Angle-land) and much of English.
- Britain split into several **Anglo-Saxon kingdoms** (like Wessex, Mercia, Northumbria); the **Scots** were peoples in the north (Scotland).
- This period gives us many **place names**, the spread of **Christianity**, and stunning sources like the ship-burial at **Sutton Hoo**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Romans ruled Britain then left; understands "kingdom" and how sources reveal the past.

### Teach it (the explanation)
When the Roman army left Britain around **AD 410**, it left a gap. Across the **North Sea**, peoples from what's now Germany, Denmark and the Netherlands — the **Angles, Saxons and Jutes** — sailed over and **settled**. We call them the **Anglo-Saxons**. Their towns weren't grand Roman cities; they lived mostly in **wooden villages** and farmed. Their language grew into **Old English**, the root of the English we speak, and the **Angles** gave their name to "**Engla-land**" — England.

Britain wasn't one country yet. It split into several **kingdoms** that fought and allied — Wessex, Mercia, Northumbria, Kent and others — each with its own king. In the north were the **Scots** and **Picts**, the peoples of what became Scotland.

At first the Anglo-Saxons worshipped many gods (some of our weekday names come from them — *Wednesday* from Woden, *Thursday* from Thunor/Thor). Over time, **Christianity** spread, with monasteries becoming centres of writing and learning. How do we know all this? From objects and burials — most famously **Sutton Hoo**, a king buried inside an entire **ship** with a magnificent helmet and treasure, found in 1939: a spectacular source about Anglo-Saxon power and beliefs.

**Worked example.** "Where does the name *England* come from?" Trace it: the **Angles** were one of the tribes who settled → their land became "**Angle-land**" / **Engla-land** → today's **England**. Many of our **place names** and the English language itself come from these settlers.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- The Sutton Hoo helmet — show the image; a real king buried in a ship.
- Weekday names: Woden→Wednesday, Thunor→Thursday — "you say their gods' names every week."
- Village life vs Roman cities — what would your day be like as an Anglo-Saxon child?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "England had always been called England / been one country."
  **Why:** today's map feels timeless.
  **Fix:** the name comes from the **Angles**, and Britain was several rival **kingdoms** for a long time before it united.
- **Wrong idea:** the Anglo-Saxons were the same as the Vikings.
  **Why:** both "old invaders from over the sea."
  **Fix:** the Anglo-Saxons came **earlier** (after the Romans); the **Vikings came later** and often fought the Anglo-Saxons.
- **Wrong idea:** the "Dark Ages" means nothing happened / nobody was clever.
  **Why:** the old nickname.
  **Fix:** there was art, fine metalwork (Sutton Hoo), poetry (*Beowulf*) and learning in monasteries — far from "dark."

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Who settled in Britain after the Romans left, and where did they come from? **A:** The Angles, Saxons and Jutes, from across the North Sea (now Germany/Denmark/Netherlands).
- **Q:** Where does the name "England" come from? **A:** From the Angles — "Angle-land."
- **Q:** Was Anglo-Saxon Britain one country? **A:** No — it was several rival kingdoms (e.g. Wessex, Mercia, Northumbria), with Scots and Picts in the north.
- **Q:** What is Sutton Hoo and why does it matter? **A:** A ship-burial of an Anglo-Saxon king with treasure and a helmet — a rich source about their power and beliefs.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs / fillBlank.* Match weekday → god (Wednesday→Woden, Thursday→Thunor/Thor). Fill the blank: "The ___ gave their name to England." (Angles). Order on the timeline: Romans → Anglo-Saxons → Vikings.

## 6. The Vikings and the struggle for England

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – Vikings and Anglo-Saxons to 1066

**Prerequisites:** Anglo-Saxons and Scots.

### Key concepts
- From around **AD 793**, **Vikings** from **Scandinavia** (Norway, Denmark, Sweden) **raided** then **settled** in Britain, fighting the Anglo-Saxons for control.
- Vikings were not *only* raiders: they were skilled **sailors, traders, explorers and craftspeople** who built remarkable **longships** and sailed as far as Iceland, Greenland and North America.
- **King Alfred the Great** of Wessex resisted them; over time Anglo-Saxons and Vikings settled together, and England was finally unified — until **1066**, the end of this period.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Anglo-Saxons settled Britain into kingdoms; understands raiding/settling and reading sources.

### Teach it (the explanation)
In **AD 793**, raiders in fast **longships** struck the monastery at **Lindisfarne** off the north-east coast — the first big **Viking** raid. The Vikings came from **Scandinavia** (Norway, Denmark, Sweden). Their longships were brilliant: shallow enough to sail right up rivers and onto beaches, so they could strike fast and vanish. They raided rich, undefended places like monasteries for treasure.

But here's the fuller picture: Vikings weren't *just* violent raiders. They were superb **sailors, traders and explorers**, fine **craftspeople**, and farmers. They set up trading towns (like Jorvik — **York**), reached **Iceland, Greenland and even North America** (about 500 years before Columbus), and many came to Britain to **settle and farm**, not only to raid.

For years they fought the Anglo-Saxons for England. They conquered so much that part of England became the **Danelaw**, under Viking rule. The Anglo-Saxon king who held them back was **Alfred the Great** of Wessex — he defeated the Vikings in battle, made a treaty, and is famous for defending and uniting English resistance. Gradually Saxons and Vikings lived side by side, blending languages and customs, and England became one kingdom. This whole Anglo-Saxon-and-Viking age ends at the year **1066**.

**Worked example.** "Were the Vikings just raiders?" Weigh the evidence: yes, they raided (Lindisfarne, monasteries) — *but* they also **traded** (York/Jorvik), **explored** (Iceland, Greenland, North America), **farmed and settled**, and made fine craft. So a fair answer: fierce raiders **and** skilled sailors, traders and settlers — both are true.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Longships — design and sail one; why the shape made them so deadly and far-reaching.
- Vikings reached North America before Columbus — a surprising "first."
- Words we still use from Old Norse (sky, egg, knife, *they*) — "you speak a bit of Viking."

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Vikings wore horned helmets."
  **Why:** the popular cartoon image.
  **Fix:** there's **no evidence** of horned battle helmets — that's a Victorian myth; real helmets were plain and practical.
- **Wrong idea:** "Vikings were only mindless raiders."
  **Why:** the raiding stories are the famous ones.
  **Fix:** balance it — they were also expert sailors, long-distance **traders, explorers and settlers** who built towns like York.
- **Wrong idea:** Vikings and Anglo-Saxons are the same / came at the same time.
  **Why:** both are "old sea invaders."
  **Fix:** Anglo-Saxons came **first** (after the Romans); **Vikings came later** and fought them — put both on the timeline (Romans → Saxons → Vikings → 1066).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Where did the Vikings come from, and roughly when did they start raiding Britain? **A:** Scandinavia (Norway/Denmark/Sweden); from around AD 793 (Lindisfarne).
- **Q:** Name two things Vikings did *besides* raiding. **A:** Any two of: traded, explored (Iceland/Greenland/North America), farmed and settled, made fine crafts, built towns like York.
- **Q:** Did Vikings wear horned helmets? **A:** No — that's a myth with no evidence; real helmets were plain.
- **Q:** Who was Alfred the Great and what is he known for? **A:** King of Wessex who resisted the Vikings and helped defend/unite English resistance.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize block.* Sort facts into "Raider", "Explorer/Trader", or "Settler" to break the one-sided image. Then *multipleChoice*: "Which is a myth about Vikings?" (horned helmets).

- **National Geographic Kids — "Viking facts"** (longships, daily life, exploration).

## 7. A non-European society — the Maya

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – a non-European society contrasting with British history

**Prerequisites:** Ancient Egypt or Ancient Greece (the idea of a sophisticated early civilisation).

### Key concepts
- The **Maya** built an advanced civilisation in the rainforests of **Central America** (e.g. Mexico, Guatemala), flourishing around AD 250–900 — at the *same time* as the Anglo-Saxons in Britain.
- They were remarkable **astronomers and mathematicians**: a working concept of **zero**, an accurate **calendar**, picture-writing (**glyphs**), and great stone **cities** and **pyramid-temples**.
- Comparing the Maya with Anglo-Saxon Britain shows different peoples reaching high achievements in their own ways — and challenges the idea that "advanced" only means Europe.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has studied at least one early civilisation and Anglo-Saxon Britain (for the time comparison); can use a world map.

### Teach it (the explanation)
While the Anglo-Saxons were farming villages in Britain, on the other side of the world — in the rainforests of **Central America** (today's Mexico, Guatemala, Belize) — the **Maya** were building a brilliant civilisation. Their cities, like **Tikal** and **Chichén Itzá**, had huge stone **pyramid-temples**, palaces and ball-courts, carved out of the jungle.

The Maya were extraordinary thinkers. They were superb **astronomers**, tracking the sun, moon and planets, and built one of the most accurate **calendars** of the ancient world. In **maths** they used a place-value system and understood **zero** — something Europe didn't have for centuries. They wrote in detailed **glyphs** (picture-symbols) carved on temples and painted in bark-paper books. They grew **maize** (corn) as their staple food and traded goods like cacao (chocolate!) across the region.

The Maya worshipped many **gods** linked to nature and the heavens, and their religion shaped their calendar and buildings. Around **AD 900** many great Maya cities were mysteriously **abandoned** — historians think a mix of drought, war and overuse of the land — but, importantly, **the Maya people did not vanish**: millions of their descendants still live in Central America today, speaking Maya languages.

**Worked example.** "Were the Maya 'advanced'?" Test it against real achievements: they used **zero** and place-value maths, built an accurate **calendar**, had **writing** (glyphs), and constructed huge stone **cities and pyramids** — all without metal tools or the wheel for transport. So: yes, an advanced civilisation that, in maths and astronomy, was *ahead* of Europe at the time.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Chocolate! Cacao was precious to the Maya — even used as money.
- Crack the code: Maya number symbols (dots and bars) and the idea of zero.
- Pyramids in the jungle — compare with Egypt's pyramids (both pyramids, very different purposes and places).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The Maya all died out / disappeared."
  **Why:** the abandoned cities suggest extinction.
  **Fix:** the *cities* were abandoned around AD 900, but **millions of Maya descendants live today** and still speak Maya languages.
- **Wrong idea:** "Faraway old peoples were primitive compared to Europe."
  **Why:** a Europe-first assumption.
  **Fix:** the Maya had **zero**, advanced astronomy, writing and great cities *before or beyond* what Europe had then — "advanced" isn't only European.
- **Wrong idea:** the Maya, Aztecs and Incas are all the same.
  **Why:** all "ancient American" peoples.
  **Fix:** they were **different** civilisations in different places and times (Maya in the rainforests, earlier; Aztecs and Incas later and elsewhere).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Where and roughly when did the Maya civilisation flourish? **A:** In Central America (Mexico/Guatemala etc.), around AD 250–900 — at the same time as the Anglo-Saxons.
- **Q:** Give two impressive achievements of the Maya. **A:** Any two of: the concept of zero / advanced maths, an accurate calendar, writing (glyphs), great stone cities and pyramids, astronomy.
- **Q:** Did the Maya people all disappear? **A:** No — their big cities were abandoned ~AD 900, but millions of Maya descendants live today.
- **Q:** Name one thing the Maya had that Europe didn't have for a long time afterwards. **A:** The concept of zero (and a very accurate calendar).

### Activity / practice
*Categorize / matchPairs.* Match Maya achievements to descriptions (zero → a number Europe lacked; glyphs → picture-writing; cacao → used as money; calendar → very accurate). Compare-and-contrast block: same/different between Maya and Anglo-Saxons (same era; very different lives).

- **BBC Bitesize KS2 History — "The Maya civilisation"** (cities, maths, calendar, writing, daily life).
- **National Geographic Kids — "Maya facts"** (pyramids, gods, achievements).
- **BBC Teach — "The Maya"** clips (Tikal, astronomy and maths, religion).

## 8. A local history study

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – local history in depth

**Prerequisites:** Any one period above (so the local study can be placed in national context).

### Key concepts
- An **in-depth study of the child's own area** over time — connecting national history to "right where I live."
- Local change has **causes and consequences**: industry, transport, war or population growth reshaped towns; we can find the evidence locally.
- Historians use **local sources**: old maps, census records, photographs, buildings, war memorials, museum collections and people's memories.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the broad chronology (Romans → Saxons → Vikings → … → modern) to place local events; can interrogate sources.

### Teach it (the explanation)
*(Tutor: personalise to the learner's actual town or city — ask the parent for the place name and one notable local feature. The method below applies anywhere.)*

Now turn the historian's tools onto **your own area**. Big national stories left marks locally. Did your town grow because of a **factory, mine, mill, port or railway** in the 1800s? Was there a **castle, abbey or Roman site**? Is there a **war memorial** listing local people who died in the World Wars? Each is a thread connecting your street to the national timeline.

Use **local sources** like a detective: compare an **old map** of your town with a modern one (which streets are new? where did fields become houses?); look at **old photographs** of the high street; read names on a **war memorial**; visit the **local museum**; and ask **older residents** what changed in their lifetime. Then ask the historian's questions: **what changed, when, why (cause), and what followed (consequence)?**

Example pattern: a town near a coalfield. In 1750 it's a village; a **coal mine** opens; people move there for work; the population **booms**; a **railway** is built to carry coal; rows of miners' houses spring up; later the mine **closes** and the town has to find new work. That arc — growth then change — is written into the streets, and old maps and photos prove it.

**Worked example.** Take one local feature — say a railway station or a mill. Ask: *When* was it built (date on the building, old maps)? *Why* (to move coal/cotton/people)? *What changed because of it* (the town grew, new houses, new jobs)? *How do we know* (maps, photos, census, memories)? That's a complete local-history enquiry from a single source.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A "then and now" walk (real or via digital maps/Street View) past the oldest local building.
- Your own surname or a local street name as a clue to the area's past trades.
- A family connection — where did relatives work locally, and what was that industry?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Important history only happens in capital cities / abroad."
  **Why:** big events feel distant.
  **Fix:** national stories (industry, war, migration) all left **local evidence** — find one concrete example nearby.
- **Wrong idea:** the local area "has always looked like this."
  **Why:** no sense of change over time.
  **Fix:** compare an old and a new map/photo of the same spot to reveal what changed and why.
- **Wrong idea:** local sources are "just old stuff," not real evidence.
  **Why:** maps/photos feel less serious than books.
  **Fix:** show how a single old map or census reveals dates, jobs and growth — primary evidence historians rely on.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name two local sources a historian could use to study your town's past. **A:** Any two of: old maps, photographs, census records, war memorials, buildings, museum collections, older residents' memories.
- **Q:** Why might a town have grown quickly in the 1800s? **A:** A new industry/transport — e.g. a factory, mine, mill, port or railway brought jobs, so people moved there.
- **Q:** How could you find out what your street looked like 150 years ago? **A:** Compare old and modern maps, look at old photographs, read the census, and ask older residents.

### Activity / practice
*Compare + speak block.* Show an old vs modern map (or photo) of the learner's area; child lists two things that changed and one that stayed the same, then explains a likely *cause*. If no local material is to hand, use any town's old/new map pair as a model.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 History — "Local history study"** (how to investigate your area through time).
- **National Library of Scotland — "Maps" (georeferenced historic OS maps)** and **Ordnance Survey** modern maps (compare old vs new; parent-guided).
- **Your local archive / museum website and the local war memorial** (primary local sources).

## 9. A theme beyond 1066 — the history of flight

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – a study extending knowledge beyond 1066 (change over time)

**Prerequisites:** Local history study (or any earlier period) — for the long timeline.

### Key concepts
- A **theme across time** shows **change and continuity** over a long span: in about **120 years** humans went from never flying, to the first powered plane, to the Moon.
- Key milestones: the **Wright brothers'** first powered flight (**1903**), passenger and jet airliners, and the **Moon landing** (**1969**) — each built on the last.
- New technology has huge **consequences** — flight shrank the world (travel, trade, holidays) but also changed warfare and the environment.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Comfortable with timelines and dates; understands cause/consequence and "change over time."

### Teach it (the explanation)
Here's a "theme through time" study — how one thing, **flight**, transformed in living memory of your great-grandparents.

For all of history, humans couldn't fly. People tried gliders and hot-air **balloons** (which lifted people from the 1780s) but couldn't steer powered flight. Then in **1903**, two American brothers, **Orville and Wilbur Wright**, flew the first **powered, controlled aeroplane** — just 12 seconds, lower than a house, but it changed everything.

After that, change came astonishingly fast — a great example of **building on what came before**. Planes got bigger and faster; in the World Wars they were used in battle; then came **passenger airliners**, and **jet engines** that let ordinary people fly across the world for holidays and business. Rockets — flight pushed to the extreme — carried humans into **space**, and in **1969** **Neil Armstrong** walked on the **Moon**. From "humans cannot fly" to "humans on the Moon" took only about **66 years**.

Think about the **consequences**. Flight **shrank the world**: journeys that took weeks by ship take hours by plane; we can trade, travel and visit family across oceans. But there are downsides too: aircraft were used in **war and bombing**, and today flying adds to **pollution and climate change**. Good history asks about *both* sides of a change.

**Worked example.** Order the milestones and see the acceleration: hot-air **balloon** (1780s) → first **powered flight**, the Wright brothers (1903) → **jet airliners** carry millions (mid-1900s) → **Moon landing** (1969). Each step **built on the last** — and the gaps between them got shorter, showing change speeding up.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A learner who's been on a plane: "your great-grandparents may never have flown at all."
- Paper aeroplanes / how wings work, linked to the Wright brothers' problem.
- Space link — the same story of flight ends with people on the Moon.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "People have always had planes."
  **Why:** flight feels normal and timeless today.
  **Fix:** powered flight is only ~120 years old — within a few generations; place 1903 on the timeline.
- **Wrong idea:** new inventions are "all good" (or "all bad").
  **Why:** simple judgement of technology.
  **Fix:** weigh both **consequences** — flight connects the world *and* causes pollution and was used in war.
- **Wrong idea:** the Wright brothers' plane was a big modern airliner.
  **Why:** imagining today's planes.
  **Fix:** it was tiny and flew for 12 seconds just metres off the ground — the point is everything that grew *from* it.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Who made the first powered, controlled flight, and in what year? **A:** The Wright brothers (Orville and Wilbur), in 1903.
- **Q:** Put these in order: jet airliners, Moon landing, first powered flight, hot-air balloon. **A:** hot-air balloon → first powered flight (1903) → jet airliners → Moon landing (1969).
- **Q:** Give one good and one bad consequence of the invention of flight. **A:** Good — fast travel/trade, connecting the world; bad — used in war/bombing, causes pollution.
- **Q:** Roughly how long was it from the first powered flight to the Moon landing? **A:** About 66 years (1903 → 1969) — very fast change.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + categorize.* Order the milestones on a timeline; then sort consequences of flight into "helped the world" vs "caused problems." A *speak* prompt: "Tell me how flight changed people's lives in the last 120 years."

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 History — "Significant turning points" / history of flight & transport** (change over time).
- **National Geographic Kids — "The Wright brothers / history of flight"** (milestones and images).
- **Royal Air Force Museum (online) — history of flight** timelines and exhibits (parent-guided).
