# History & Geography (KS1–KS2)

Classai `SubjectKey`: **`history`** (this folder covers both history and geography — the two "humanities" of the UK primary curriculum. They share a folder because they share habits of mind: reading the world for change, place, cause and evidence.)

This subject teaches children to think about **time** (history) and **space** (geography): what happened before they were born and why it matters, and what the world is like beyond their own street. The aim at primary level is not to memorise a thousand dates and capitals, but to build a secure mental "map" — a timeline they can hang events on, and a globe they can place countries on — and to learn the *disciplines*: weighing evidence, comparing then and now, reading a real map.

---

## How to teach primary history

History is a **way of thinking**, not just a story. Five disciplinary ideas run through every topic. Teach the content *through* these, not around them.

**1. Chronology & timelines.** Young children have almost no sense of historical time — "a long time ago" can mean last year or 4,000 years ago. Build a physical or drawn **timeline** and keep returning to it. Always place a new topic on it ("the Romans came *after* the Stone Age but *before* the Vikings"). Use the child's own life as the near end ("you → your parents → your grandparents → before photographs → before writing"). Order and sequence matter more than exact dates at KS1; rough dates and "centuries"/"BC and AD" come in at KS2.

**2. Cause & consequence.** Things happen *for reasons* and *lead to* other things. Always ask "why did that happen?" and "what changed because of it?" (The Great Fire spread *because* houses were wooden and close together; *as a result* London was rebuilt in brick and stone.)

**3. Similarity & difference (change & continuity).** Compare life *then* and *now*, and compare two periods. What changed? What stayed the same? (A Roman child and you: both went to lessons and played games; but no electricity, different gods, slaves in the household.) Guard against "the olden days were all the same" — the Stone Age and the Victorians are as far apart from each other as you are from the Romans.

**4. Sources & evidence.** We know about the past from **sources** — things left behind: objects (artefacts), buildings, bones, paintings, and (later) writing. Always ask "**how do we know?**" Distinguish what a source tells us from what we guess. Note that sources can be incomplete or biased (the Romans wrote about the "barbarians"; the losers rarely wrote anything).

**5. Significance.** Why do we still talk about *this* person or event? Significance is about lasting impact, not just fame. (Florence Nightingale is significant because she changed how hospitals are run, not just because she was brave.)

**Sequence of a good history lesson:** hook with an object/picture/mystery → place it on the timeline → tell the narrative → ask "how do we know / why / what changed?" → compare then-and-now → a "you do" (sort, sequence, or interpret a source).

## How to teach primary geography

Geography is **place knowledge plus the skill of reading the world**. Four strands:

**1. Locational knowledge.** Where things *are*: the four countries of the UK and their capitals, the seven continents and five oceans, key features (rivers, mountains, capitals). Build this on a **map and a globe** kept always in view, zooming from the local (my town) → national (UK) → world. Children need an internal map to hang everything else on.

**2. Place knowledge.** What a *particular* place is like, and how two places compare (e.g. their own town vs. a contrasting place — a hot, dry village or a city in another continent). Place knowledge is about *similarity and difference between real places*, not stereotypes.

**3. Human & physical geography.** Split the world into **physical** features (made by nature: rivers, mountains, weather, coasts, volcanoes) and **human** features (made by people: cities, roads, farms, ports). At KS2 this deepens into processes — the water cycle, climate zones and biomes, settlements and land use.

**4. Geographical skills & fieldwork.** *Doing* geography: using maps, atlases, globes and digital maps; the **four (then eight) compass points**; **simple maps and a key**; at KS2, **grid references, symbols, contours**; and **fieldwork** — going outside to observe, measure and record their own local area.

**Map literacy is the through-line.** Start with picture maps and aerial photos of the classroom/playground (KS1), move to OS-style symbol maps with keys and four-figure grid references (KS2). A map is a *flat, shrunk, simplified, bird's-eye* drawing of real space — every one of those four words is a concept to teach.

## Scope & sequence (KS1 → KS2)

The UK National Curriculum doesn't pin most history/geography topics to a single year — it sets them across the key stage. Below is a sensible, well-sequenced ordering. **History** is roughly chronological once you reach KS2; **geography** spirals from local → national → global, revisiting skills at greater depth.

### Key Stage 1 (Years 1–2, ages 5–7)

**History (`history-ks1.md`)** — within and beyond living memory, told as stories, anchored on a simple timeline:
1. Changes within living memory (toys, homes, school — then and now)
2. The Great Fire of London (an event beyond living memory)
3. Significant explorers — Christopher Columbus & Neil Armstrong (journeys then and now)
4. Significant nurses — Florence Nightingale & Mary Seacole
5. Local history — a significant place or person near home

**Geography (`geography-ks1.md`)** — local → UK → world, with first map and weather skills:
1. The United Kingdom — four countries, capitals & surrounding seas
2. Continents & oceans of the world
3. Hot & cold places (the Equator and the Poles)
4. My local area — simple maps, aerial views & the compass
5. Weather & seasons in the UK

### Key Stage 2 (Years 3–6, ages 7–11)

**History (`history-ks2.md`)** — a chronological journey through British and world history:
1. Stone Age to Iron Age Britain (prehistory)
2. Ancient Egypt (an early civilisation)
3. Ancient Greece (life, achievements, legacy)
4. The Roman Empire & its impact on Britain
5. Anglo-Saxons & Scots (settlement after the Romans)
6. Vikings & the struggle for England
7. A non-European society — the Maya
8. A local history study (an extended look at the child's own area through time)
9. A theme beyond 1066 (e.g. the history of flight / transport — change over time)

**Geography (`geography-ks2.md`)** — global systems, processes and real map skills:
1. Latitude, longitude, hemispheres & time zones
2. Climate zones & biomes
3. Rivers & the water cycle
4. Mountains, volcanoes & earthquakes
5. Settlements & land use
6. Map skills — grid references, symbols & contours
7. A region study — comparing a UK region, a European region & a region of the Americas

## Notes for the tutor

- **Be accurate and honest.** Don't smooth history into a fairy tale. Where a topic touches hard things (fire deaths, war, slavery, colonisation), tell the truth simply and without gratuitous detail. The Maya and Benin are *advanced civilisations* worth studying in their own right, not "exotic" footnotes; explorers' voyages also caused harm to peoples already living in those lands — say so plainly at an age-appropriate level.
- **Hang everything on the two big organisers:** the timeline (history) and the map/globe (geography). If a child can place a new fact on one of them, it sticks.
- **"How do we know?" and "Where is it?"** are the two questions to keep asking.
- See `misconceptions.md` for the wrong ideas that recur across topics, and `resources.md` for vetted free material.
