# History & Geography — Extension Topics (KS1–KS2)

Ten **additional** topics that deepen the core history and geography files without repeating them. They fall into three groups: **deeper historical skills** (working with sources, chronology across periods, interpretations, significance), **more periods & societies** (Ancient Sumer & the Indus Valley, the Tudors, the WWII home front, the transatlantic context handled sensitively), and **deeper geography** (OS map skills in depth, economic geography & trade, climate change at a child's level, fieldwork methods, biomes in depth). Same per-topic contract as `_TEMPLATE.md`. Keep hanging everything on the **timeline** and the **map/globe**, and keep asking *"how do we know?"* and *"where is it?"*. Sensitive topics (war, enslavement) are told truthfully, simply, and without gratuitous detail.

## 1. Working with sources — primary, secondary & "how reliable?"

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – disciplinary skill (sources & evidence)

**Prerequisites:** any KS2 period (e.g. Romans, Vikings) so there are real sources to examine.

### Key concepts
- A **primary source** was made *at the time* (a diary, coin, photo, tool, painting); a **secondary source** was made *later by someone studying it* (a textbook, a museum label, this lesson).
- Sources can be **incomplete, biased or one-sided** — we ask who made it, when, and why, before we trust it.
- History is built by **combining** several sources, not believing one on its own.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows we learn about the past from "sources" and has met named sources (Pepys's diary, Sutton Hoo, the Rosetta Stone).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Historians are detectives, and **sources** are their clues. There are two kinds. A **primary source** is a clue made *right then* — Samuel Pepys's diary written during the Great Fire, a Roman coin, a photo from 1900, a Viking sword. A **secondary source** is made *afterwards by someone who studied the primary ones* — a history book, a museum sign, a documentary, or me telling you this now.

Here's the catch: not every source tells the whole truth. A source can be **incomplete** (the bits that survived are random — wood rots, gold lasts), **biased** (the Romans wrote about "barbarians" — that's *their* side), or made to **persuade** (a poster wants you to think something). So before trusting a source, ask three questions: **Who** made it? **When**? **Why** — to record, to boast, to sell, to persuade? That's why historians never rely on one clue: they **cross-check** several sources and see where they agree.

**Worked example.** Two accounts of a battle: one written by the winning king's own scribe ("a glorious victory"), one a list of the dead in a village record. Which is more reliable about *how many died*? The village list — it had no reason to exaggerate, while the scribe wanted to make the king look great. Best of all: use *both*, and note the scribe's source is **biased** toward the king.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- "What would survive from *your* day for a future historian — your shoe, a photo, a screenshot?" (and what would rot away?)
- A team's account of a match vs the other team's — same event, two stories.
- Spot the bias in an old advert or a propaganda poster.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "If it's a primary source / written down, it must be true."
  **Why:** old and original feels trustworthy.
  **Fix:** primary sources can still be biased or wrong — a diary can lie or be mistaken. Reliability is separate from age.
- **Wrong idea:** "A textbook is the best source because it's a book."
  **Why:** books feel authoritative.
  **Fix:** a textbook is *secondary* — useful, but it depends on the primary sources behind it; check those.
- **Wrong idea:** "One good source is enough."
  **Why:** wanting a single right answer.
  **Fix:** historians combine sources; agreement between several is what builds confidence.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is the difference between a primary and a secondary source? **A:** Primary = made at the time (diary, coin, photo); secondary = made later by someone studying it (textbook, documentary).
- **Q:** Name two questions you'd ask to judge if a source is reliable. **A:** Any two of: Who made it? When? Why (to record/boast/persuade)? Does another source agree?
- **Q:** Why do historians use more than one source? **A:** Because any single source can be incomplete or biased; cross-checking gives a fuller, fairer picture.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize + speak.* Sort a mixed set into "primary" vs "secondary" (a Roman coin, a school textbook, a 1940 photo, a museum label, a knight's helmet, a documentary). Then pick one source and say aloud who made it, when, and one reason it might be biased. (`categorize` then `speak`.)

## 2. Chronology across the whole timeline — putting the periods in order

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – disciplinary skill (chronology)

**Prerequisites:** at least three KS2 periods (e.g. Stone Age, Egypt, Romans, Vikings).

### Key concepts
- Every period studied lives in **one order on a single long timeline** — they are easy to muddle, so we keep placing them.
- **BC counts down toward 0; AD counts up** — so 3000 BC is older than 500 BC, which is older than AD 1066.
- The gaps are huge and uneven: the Stone Age is far longer than everything after it put together.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has met several periods and the idea of BC/AD; can order events first→last.

### Teach it (the explanation)
You've now visited many times — Stone Age, Egypt, Greece, Romans, Anglo-Saxons, Vikings, the Maya. The big skill of **chronology** is placing them all on **one long line** in the right order, because it's easy to jumble "a long time ago" into one blur. Picture a giant ruler. On the far left: the **Stone Age** (thousands and thousands of years — longer than everything else combined). Then **Ancient Egypt** and **Ancient Sumer** (around 5,000 years ago), **Ancient Greece** (~2,500 years ago), the **Romans** (in Britain from AD 43), then **Anglo-Saxons**, **Vikings**, up to **1066** — and the **Maya** flourishing at the same time as the Anglo-Saxons, just on the other side of the world.

Remember the dating rule: **BC** years count *down* toward 0 (so 3000 BC is **older** than 500 BC), and **AD** years count *up* from there. A common trap is thinking a bigger BC number is more recent — it's the opposite. Two facts can be true at once in different places: while Anglo-Saxons farmed villages in Britain, the Maya built stone cities — same time, far apart in **space**.

**Worked example.** Order these oldest→newest: Vikings, Stone Age, Ancient Egypt, Romans in Britain. Use the rule: Stone Age (deep prehistory) → Ancient Egypt (~3000 BC) → Romans in Britain (AD 43) → Vikings (from AD 793). Now check a BC trap: which is older, 2000 BC or 500 BC? **2000 BC** — bigger BC number = further back.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Make a "washing-line timeline" across the room and peg each period on in order.
- "Who could have met whom?" — could a Viking meet a Roman? (No — centuries apart.)
- Stretch a piece of string: most of it is the Stone Age; everything famous is squashed at one end.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "3000 BC is more recent than 500 BC because 3000 is bigger."
  **Why:** treating BC like ordinary counting.
  **Fix:** BC counts *down* toward 0 — bigger BC number = older. Put both on the line to see.
- **Wrong idea:** "All the ancient periods happened around the same time."
  **Why:** "ancient" collapses together.
  **Fix:** the gaps are huge — Egypt's pyramids were already ancient when the Romans arrived.
- **Wrong idea:** "If two peoples were alive at once they must have known each other."
  **Why:** ignoring space.
  **Fix:** the Maya and Anglo-Saxons overlapped in *time* but were oceans apart in *space* and never met.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Order oldest→newest: Romans in Britain, Stone Age, Vikings, Ancient Greece. **A:** Stone Age → Ancient Greece → Romans in Britain → Vikings.
- **Q:** Which is older, 1500 BC or 200 BC? **A:** 1500 BC (bigger BC number = further back).
- **Q:** The Maya and the Anglo-Saxons lived at the same time — did they meet? **A:** No — they were on opposite sides of the world (same time, different place).

### Activity / practice
*Ordering.* Place 6–8 studied periods on a single timeline oldest→newest, then answer two "which is older?" BC/AD questions. (`ordering` block; optional `speak`: explain one huge gap, e.g. Stone Age vs Romans.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 History — "Chronology / timelines / BC and AD"**.
- **BBC Teach — primary history timeline clips** (sequencing the periods).

## 3. Interpretations & significance — why we remember some people, and why stories change

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – disciplinary skill (interpretations & significance)

**Prerequisites:** Working with sources (Topic 1); several KS2 individuals (Boudica, Nightingale, Alfred…).

### Key concepts
- An **interpretation** is *someone's telling* of the past — different people tell the same event differently, and tellings change over time.
- **Significance** isn't just fame: a person or event is *significant* if it caused lasting, wide change that still matters.
- New evidence and new questions make us **re-tell** history (e.g. remembering Mary Seacole alongside Florence Nightingale).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows sources can be biased; has met several "significant individuals."

### Teach it (the explanation)
Two ideas make a real historian. The first is **interpretation**: the same event can be *told* in different ways. A statue, a film, a textbook and a song about the same king are all **interpretations** — shaped by who made them, when, and what they wanted to show. That's why the story of the past sometimes **changes**: not because the past changed, but because we find new evidence or ask new questions. For years schools taught lots about Florence Nightingale and little about **Mary Seacole**; now we remember both — the past didn't change, our *interpretation* did.

The second idea is **significance**. Being *famous* isn't the same as being *significant*. Someone is significant when what they did caused **big, lasting change that still affects us**. Nightingale is significant because hospitals everywhere still follow her ideas about cleanliness and trained nurses — not just because she was brave. We weigh significance by asking: How many people were affected? How deeply? How long did the change last? Is it still important today?

**Worked example.** "Why is the invention of the printing press significant?" Test it: it affected **millions** (books became cheap), **deeply** (ordinary people could learn to read), and the change **lasted centuries** and still matters (all printed and even digital text descends from it). Lots of people, deep change, long-lasting, still important → highly significant. Compare with someone briefly famous whose impact faded — famous, but not very significant.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Two films or two books about the same event — spot how each *tells it* differently.
- "Who from today will history remember in 200 years, and why?" — weigh significance.
- A statue debate: why a town might add, move or re-label a statue as interpretations change.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The most famous person is the most significant."
  **Why:** fame is easy to see.
  **Fix:** significance = lasting, wide impact, not fame; some quiet people changed the world more than celebrities.
- **Wrong idea:** "If two history books disagree, one is just wrong / lying."
  **Why:** expecting one fixed story.
  **Fix:** they may be different *interpretations* built on different evidence and questions; both can be honest.
- **Wrong idea:** "History changing means the facts changed."
  **Why:** confusing facts with tellings.
  **Fix:** the events are fixed; what changes is our *interpretation* as we learn more or ask new questions.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a historical *interpretation*? **A:** Someone's telling of the past (a film, statue, textbook) — shaped by who made it and why; different tellings can differ.
- **Q:** Does *famous* mean the same as *significant*? Explain. **A:** No — significant means causing big, lasting change that still matters; you can be famous without being very significant.
- **Q:** Why do stories about the past sometimes change? **A:** New evidence or new questions give us new interpretations — the past stays the same, our telling of it changes.

### Activity / practice
*Speak + categorize.* Given two short "tellings" of one event, the child says how they differ and why (who made each, why). Then rank three people/events by significance using the test (how many / how deep / how long / still matters today). (`speak` then `ordering`.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 History — "Significant people and events" / "Interpretations"**.
- **Historical Association (primary) — "significance" guidance** (parent-guided).

## 4. Ancient Sumer & the Indus Valley — the first cities

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–4 (ages 7–9) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – early civilisations (world)

**Prerequisites:** Ancient Egypt (the idea of a river civilisation and sources).

### Key concepts
- Some of the world's **first cities and writing** grew up in two great river valleys: **Sumer** (Mesopotamia, between the Tigris and Euphrates, in today's Iraq) and the **Indus Valley** (in today's Pakistan/India).
- Like Egypt, these civilisations grew because **rivers** made farming reliable; surplus food let cities, jobs and **writing** appear.
- Sumer gave us **cuneiform** (the first writing) and the **wheel**; the Indus cities (e.g. **Mohenjo-daro**) had astonishing **planned streets and drains**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows Egypt grew along the Nile and that we use sources/archaeology for the deep past.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Egypt wasn't the only early civilisation — around the **same time, ~5,000 years ago**, two more grew up beside great rivers. In **Mesopotamia** ("the land between the rivers," the **Tigris** and **Euphrates**, in today's Iraq) lived the people of **Sumer**, who built some of the world's very first **cities** like **Ur**. Far to the east, along the **Indus** river (in today's Pakistan and north-west India), grew the **Indus Valley civilisation**, with cities like **Mohenjo-daro** and **Harappa**.

Why there? Same reason as Egypt: the rivers gave **water and rich soil** for farming, so people grew more food than they needed (a **surplus**). Surplus food meant some people could stop farming and become potters, priests, traders or builders — and that's how a **civilisation** with cities and jobs begins. The Sumerians invented **cuneiform**, the first writing (wedge marks pressed into clay), partly to **count and record goods**, and they used the **wheel**. The Indus cities amaze archaeologists for their **planning**: straight streets in a grid, brick houses, and some of the world's earliest **covered drains and toilets** — clever public health, thousands of years ago.

**Worked example.** "Why did the first cities grow beside rivers?" Chain it: river → water + fertile soil → reliable farming → **surplus** food → not everyone needs to farm → people become traders, builders, writers → towns grow into **cities** with writing to keep records. The river makes the whole thing possible — just like the Nile in Egypt.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Press wedge shapes into play-dough to "write" cuneiform like a Sumerian.
- Mohenjo-daro had drains and toilets 4,500 years ago — compare with your town's streets.
- Find the Tigris, Euphrates and Indus on a world map; notice all three early civilisations sit on rivers.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Egypt was the only ancient civilisation / the first one."
  **Why:** Egypt is the famous one.
  **Fix:** Sumer and the Indus Valley were just as early (Sumer's writing is among the oldest of all); civilisation rose in several places.
- **Wrong idea:** "Ancient peoples couldn't plan proper towns."
  **Why:** "old" read as "primitive."
  **Fix:** Mohenjo-daro had grid streets and covered drains — better planned than many later towns.
- **Wrong idea:** "Writing was invented for stories first."
  **Why:** we use writing for stories today.
  **Fix:** the earliest cuneiform was mostly for **counting goods and records** — accounts before tales.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Where did the Sumerians live, and what is one thing they invented? **A:** Mesopotamia (between the Tigris and Euphrates, today's Iraq); they invented cuneiform writing (and used the wheel).
- **Q:** Why did early cities grow next to rivers? **A:** Rivers gave water and fertile soil for reliable farming, creating surplus food so cities and new jobs could grow.
- **Q:** Name one impressive feature of Indus Valley cities like Mohenjo-daro. **A:** Planned grid streets / brick houses / covered drains and early toilets.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs + map.* Match civilisation → river → famous city (Egypt→Nile→…, Sumer→Tigris/Euphrates→Ur, Indus→Indus→Mohenjo-daro). Then a *speak* prompt: explain in one chain why all three grew beside rivers. (`matchPairs` then `speak`.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 History — "Early civilisations" / "The Indus Valley"**.
- **British Museum (online) — "Mesopotamia / Ancient Sumer" and "Indus Valley"** learning pages (parent-guided).

## 5. The Tudors — a turning point beyond 1066

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – a study of British history beyond 1066

**Prerequisites:** Chronology across the timeline (Topic 2) — to place the Tudors after 1066.

### Key concepts
- The **Tudors** ruled England ~1485–1603 (about 500 years ago), well **after 1066** — a famous, well-recorded period.
- **Henry VIII** had six wives and broke England away from the Catholic Church to make himself head of a new Church of England; his daughter **Elizabeth I** ruled a long, celebrated reign.
- Tudor England saw **exploration and the wider world** (sailors crossing oceans), a flowering of **theatre** (Shakespeare), but also great **inequality** between rich and poor.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can place periods on a timeline (knows "after 1066"); understands kings/queens and "significance."

### Teach it (the explanation)
Long after the Vikings and 1066, England was ruled by the **Tudor** family, from **1485 to 1603** — about 500 years ago. We know a *huge* amount about them because it's recent enough that letters, portraits, buildings and records survive (great for source work!).

The most famous Tudor is **Henry VIII** — remembered for his **six wives** and for a giant decision: when the Pope wouldn't let him divorce his first wife, Henry **broke away from the Catholic Church** and made himself the head of a brand-new **Church of England**. This was a massive, lasting change to religion in England. His daughter **Elizabeth I** then ruled for 45 years in a period people later called a "golden age."

Tudor England looked outward. English sailors crossed oceans to **explore and trade** (sometimes meeting peoples already living in distant lands — exploration brought contact *and* harm, as in the earlier explorers topic). At home, **theatre** blossomed — this is the age of **William Shakespeare** and the first great London playhouses. But it wasn't golden for everyone: there was sharp **inequality** — rich nobles in grand houses, and many poor people with hard, short lives. Good history holds both the glamour and the hardship together.

**Worked example.** "Was Henry VIII's break from the Church significant?" Apply the significance test: it affected **everyone in England** (the country's religion changed), it was a **deep** change (new Church, monasteries closed), and it **lasted** — the Church of England still exists today. Wide, deep, long-lasting, still here → highly significant, not just a king's personal drama.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Look at a Tudor portrait of Henry VIII or Elizabeth I and "read" it as a source (what does the costume *want* you to think?).
- Shakespeare and the Globe theatre — act a line; theatre as Tudor entertainment.
- Tudor explorers' ships on a world map — link back to the explorers topic.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The Tudors were 'the olden days' with knights and castles like the Normans."
  **Why:** all old periods blur.
  **Fix:** place them ~1485–1603, *after* 1066 and the Middle Ages — closer to us than to the Vikings.
- **Wrong idea:** "Henry changed the Church just to get divorced — it didn't really matter."
  **Why:** focusing on the gossip.
  **Fix:** the reason was personal, but the *consequence* was a deep, lasting change to England's religion that still shapes the country.
- **Wrong idea:** "Tudor times were a golden age for everybody."
  **Why:** the "golden age" label.
  **Fix:** it was glittering for the rich and for the arts, but hard and poor for many — both are true.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Roughly when did the Tudors rule, and is that before or after 1066? **A:** About 1485–1603 (around 500 years ago) — well *after* 1066.
- **Q:** What big change to religion did Henry VIII make, and why does it matter? **A:** He broke from the Catholic Church and made himself head of a new Church of England — a deep, lasting change still here today.
- **Q:** Name one bright thing and one hard thing about Tudor England. **A:** Bright: exploration / Shakespeare and theatre / Elizabeth's long reign. Hard: big inequality, poverty for many.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + speak.* Place the Tudors correctly on a timeline among earlier periods (after Vikings/1066), then a *speak* task: explain one way Henry VIII's reign still affects England today. (`ordering` then `speak`; optional `matchPairs`: Tudor monarch → one fact.)

## 6. The Second World War home front — a child's-eye view

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – a study beyond 1066 (within living memory of great-grandparents)

**Prerequisites:** Chronology (Topic 2); local history (for "how it touched our area").

### Key concepts
- The **Second World War** (1939–1945) reached ordinary people at home — the **"home front"** — even those far from any battle.
- Children's lives changed: **evacuation** (city children sent to the countryside for safety), **rationing** (sharing out scarce food fairly with ration books), **air-raid shelters** and the **blackout**.
- People **pulled together** ("make do and mend," "Dig for Victory") — a story of everyday courage, told gently and truthfully.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can place the 1940s on the timeline (great-grandparents' time); knows war means serious hardship. Keep the tone calm; no graphic detail.

### Teach it (the explanation)
*(Tutor: keep this gentle and reassuring — focus on daily life and community, not on frightening detail. Acknowledge that war is sad, then concentrate on how ordinary people coped.)*

The **Second World War** (**1939–1945**) was a huge war involving many countries. You don't have to be a soldier for a war to change your life — it reached right into homes and schools. We call that ordinary, everyday side the **"home front."** Your **great-grandparents** may have lived it.

For children, three big things changed. **Evacuation:** to keep them safe from bombing in big cities, many children were sent to live with families in the **countryside**, carrying a little case and a label — exciting and frightening at once, and often the first time away from home. **Rationing:** with ships unable to bring in much food, everyone got a fair, limited share using a **ration book** — small amounts of butter, sugar, meat and sweets. People grew their own vegetables ("**Dig for Victory**") and mended old clothes ("**make do and mend**"). And at night came the **blackout** (no lights showing, so bombers couldn't find towns) and **air-raid shelters** to hide in when the sirens sounded.

The strongest theme is people **pulling together** — neighbours sharing, children helping, everyone "doing their bit." It was hard and sometimes sad, but it's also a story of community and everyday courage.

**Worked example.** "Why were children evacuated?" Reason it out: cities (like London) were being **bombed** → cities were dangerous → the **countryside** was safer → so children were sent there to stay safe, even though it meant leaving home. Cause (danger in cities) → action (move children to safety) → consequence (children living with new families, missing home). That's cause-and-consequence in a real, human story.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Pack a tiny "evacuee's suitcase" — what one or two things would *you* take?
- Try a wartime recipe or look at a real ration book — how little sweets people got.
- Local link: was your town bombed, or did it take in evacuees? (Local history detective work.)

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Only soldiers were affected by the war."
  **Why:** war is pictured as just battles.
  **Fix:** the **home front** changed everyone — children, food, lights, work — far from any battlefield.
- **Wrong idea:** "Rationing meant there was no food at all / it was a punishment."
  **Why:** "ration" sounds like "nothing."
  **Fix:** rationing was a **fair way to share** scarce food so everyone got some — careful, not cruel.
- **Wrong idea:** "The Second World War was ages ago, like the Tudors."
  **Why:** all "history" feels equally distant.
  **Fix:** it's within living memory of great-grandparents — some people who remember it are still alive; place 1939–45 near the recent end of the timeline.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does "the home front" mean? **A:** The way the war affected ordinary people at home — not the fighting, but daily life (evacuation, rationing, shelters).
- **Q:** Why were many children evacuated to the countryside? **A:** To keep them safe from the bombing of cities.
- **Q:** What was a ration book for? **A:** To share scarce food fairly, so everyone got a limited, equal amount.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize + speak.* Sort home-front features (evacuation, rationing, blackout, Dig for Victory, air-raid shelter) and say what each was for. Then a gentle *speak* prompt: "Imagine you're an evacuee writing one line home — what would you say?" (`categorize` then `speak`.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 History — "World War 2 / The home front / Evacuation / Rationing"** (child-level).
- **Imperial War Museums (online) — "Second World War: the home front" family resources** (parent-guided).

## 7. The wider world & the transatlantic trade — a sensitive, honest introduction

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** History – a non-European / global theme (handled sensitively)

**Prerequisites:** Tudors / explorers (the wider world); Working with sources; Interpretations & significance. **Tutor: handle with care, age-appropriately and without graphic detail; centre the humanity and resilience of enslaved people.**

### Key concepts
- From the 1500s–1800s, European countries (including Britain) built ships and trade routes across the **Atlantic Ocean** linking Europe, Africa and the Americas.
- Part of this trade was a terrible wrong: **millions of African people were captured and enslaved**, forced across the Atlantic and made to work without freedom — a grave injustice we tell truthfully but gently.
- People **resisted** and worked to end it: enslaved people rebelled and survived, and **abolitionists** (like Olaudah Equiano and many others) campaigned until the slave trade and slavery were made illegal in the British Empire (1807; 1833).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Has met the earlier honest point that explorers' voyages harmed peoples already living in distant lands; understands "significance" and weighing sources.

### Teach it (the explanation)
*(Tutor: this topic must be handled with sensitivity, without graphic detail, and with the dignity, humanity and resilience of African and enslaved people at the centre. Be truthful, calm and brief; check in with the learner's feelings.)*

When we said earlier that explorers crossing oceans brought *contact and harm*, this is part of what that means. From about the **1500s to the 1800s**, European countries — Britain among them — sailed across the **Atlantic Ocean**, trading goods between Europe, Africa and the Americas.

But one part of that trade was deeply wrong. **Millions of people from Africa were captured, taken from their families, and enslaved** — carried across the Atlantic against their will and forced to work, with no freedom and no pay, on plantations in the Americas. This is one of the saddest, most serious wrongs in history, and we don't hide it or pretend it didn't happen. We also remember that these were **people** — with names, families, skills and cultures — not just a sad statistic.

Importantly, this is also a story of **resistance and change**. Enslaved people **resisted** in many ways and kept their cultures alive. Over time, brave campaigners called **abolitionists** — including formerly enslaved people like **Olaudah Equiano**, who wrote his own life story, alongside many others — worked for years to end it. Their campaigning helped make the slave **trade** illegal in the British Empire in **1807**, and slavery itself illegal in **1833**. Telling this history honestly is how we respect the people who lived it.

**Worked example.** Source work, sensitively. We have *Olaudah Equiano's own written account* (a **primary source**, by someone who lived through enslavement) and a *trader's business records* (also primary, but written by people **profiting** from the trade). Which gives the human truth of the experience? **Equiano's** — it is the voice of someone who was there, while the trader's records hide the suffering behind numbers. Combining honest sources, and centring the voices of the people who suffered, is how historians tell this truthfully.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A world map: trace the Atlantic routes between Europe, Africa and the Americas — geography and history together.
- Olaudah Equiano told *his own* story in a book — why a first-hand voice matters so much.
- Connect to "significance and change": ordinary campaigners, over many years, changed the law.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "It was a long time ago and didn't really matter / affect today."
  **Why:** distance makes it feel unimportant.
  **Fix:** it was a vast injustice affecting millions, and its effects on families, communities and societies are still felt today — which is exactly why we learn about it honestly.
- **Wrong idea:** "Enslaved people did nothing / just accepted it."
  **Why:** old tellings erased their agency.
  **Fix:** people **resisted**, survived, kept their cultures, and many — like Equiano — actively campaigned for freedom.
- **Wrong idea:** "Britain only ever ended slavery, so it was the hero."
  **Why:** a one-sided telling.
  **Fix:** Britain both **profited from** the trade for a long time **and** later abolished it after years of campaigning — an honest history holds both facts.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Which three continents did the Atlantic trade routes link? **A:** Europe, Africa and the Americas.
- **Q:** What grave wrong was part of this trade? **A:** Millions of African people were captured and enslaved, forced across the Atlantic to work with no freedom.
- **Q:** Name one way this is also a story of resistance and change. **A:** Enslaved people resisted and kept their cultures alive; abolitionists (e.g. Olaudah Equiano) campaigned until the trade (1807) and slavery (1833) were abolished in the British Empire.

### Activity / practice
*Speak + reflect.* A gentle, discussion-led close: the child explains in their own words why telling this history truthfully matters, and why a first-hand voice (Equiano) is so valuable. Keep it conversational; let the learner ask questions. (`speak` block; no quiz-style gamification on this topic.)

## 8. Ordnance Survey map skills in depth — six-figure references, scale & symbols

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – geographical skills & fieldwork (OS maps, advanced)

**Prerequisites:** Map skills — grid references, symbols & contours (four-figure references, KS2).

### Key concepts
- A **six-figure grid reference** pinpoints a spot to within ~100 m by splitting each grid square into tenths — still "**along the corridor, then up the stairs**," with an extra digit each way.
- **Scale** tells you how much the real world is shrunk (e.g. **1:25,000** means 1 cm on the map = 25,000 cm = **250 m** on the ground), and a **scale bar** lets you measure real distances.
- Fluent map reading combines **symbols (the key), grid references, scale and contours** at once.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can give a four-figure grid reference ("along then up"), read a key, and knows contours show height.

### Teach it (the explanation)
You can already give a **four-figure** grid reference to find a *square*. To pinpoint an exact *spot*, use a **six-figure** reference: imagine each grid square split into **ten** strips across and ten up. You still read **along the corridor first, then up the stairs**, but you add one extra digit each time for how far across (in tenths) and up (in tenths) inside the square. So a four-figure square "42 17" becomes something like "**425 173**" — across to 42 and **5 tenths** more, up to 17 and **3 tenths** more. Six figures gets you to within about **100 metres**.

Next, **scale**. A map is the real world shrunk by a fixed amount, written as a ratio. A common walker's map is **1:25,000** — meaning **1 cm on the map equals 25,000 cm in real life**, which is **250 metres**. (On a 1:50,000 map, 1 cm = 500 m — more land fits on the page but with less detail.) Every OS map has a **scale bar** so you can measure a distance on the map and read off the real kilometres.

Fluent map reading puts it all together: use the **key** to decode **symbols**, give a **six-figure reference** to pinpoint, use the **scale** to measure distance, and read **contours** to judge how steep and high the land is.

**Worked example.** Find how far it is between two symbols on a 1:25,000 map. Lay a ruler: they're **6 cm** apart on the map. Scale says 1 cm = 250 m, so 6 × 250 = **1,500 m = 1.5 km** in real life. Then pinpoint the church: along to 41 and 8 tenths → **418**, up to 23 and 2 tenths → **232**, giving the six-figure reference **418 232**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Plan a real walk on an OS map: pinpoint the start and end with six-figure references and measure how far it is.
- "Treasure within 100 m" — a six-figure reference treasure hunt outdoors.
- Compare a 1:25,000 and a 1:50,000 map of the same place — same land, different detail.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** reading a six-figure reference up-then-along, or splitting the square the wrong way.
  **Why:** habit / dropping the rule.
  **Fix:** always **along the corridor first, then up the stairs**; the 3rd and 6th digits are the *tenths* inside the square.
- **Wrong idea:** "Bigger scale number (1:50,000) means more detail."
  **Why:** bigger number feels like "more."
  **Fix:** a *larger* ratio number means *more* shrinking → *less* detail; 1:25,000 shows more detail than 1:50,000.
- **Wrong idea:** "1 cm on the map is 1 cm in real life."
  **Why:** forgetting the map is shrunk.
  **Fix:** use the scale — on 1:25,000, 1 cm = 250 m; measure with the scale bar.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** How is a six-figure grid reference different from a four-figure one? **A:** Each square is split into tenths, adding one extra digit across and up — pinpointing to ~100 m instead of a whole square.
- **Q:** On a 1:25,000 map, how far in real life is 1 cm? **A:** 250 m (25,000 cm).
- **Q:** Two places are 4 cm apart on a 1:25,000 map. How far is that in real life? **A:** 4 × 250 m = 1,000 m = 1 km.

### Activity / practice
*Number/grid entry.* Give two six-figure references for marked symbols on a sample OS extract (drill "along then up, plus tenths"), then measure a map distance with the scale and convert to real metres/kilometres. (`numberEntry` + `matchPairs` for symbols.)

- **Ordnance Survey — "Map reading: six-figure grid references / understanding scale"** (free, OS MapZone & guides).
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "Six-figure grid references / map scale"**.

## 9. Economic geography — trade, jobs & where our things come from

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – human geography (economic activity, trade & resources)

**Prerequisites:** Settlements & land use; continents & oceans; region study.

### Key concepts
- **Trade** is countries and people **buying and selling** goods and services; **imports** come *in* to a country, **exports** go *out*.
- Places specialise based on their **resources and climate** (bananas grow in hot countries; some places mine, manufacture or offer services), so goods travel around the world to reach us.
- Jobs split into **primary** (getting raw materials — farming, fishing, mining), **secondary** (making things — factories), and **tertiary** (services — shops, teaching, transport).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows about land use, that climate differs by place, and can use a world map.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Look at the label on a banana, a T-shirt or a phone: it often says it came from another country. That's **economic geography** — the study of how people make a living and how goods move around the world.

The key idea is **trade**: countries **buy and sell** to each other because no country can make everything well. Things a country **buys in** from abroad are **imports**; things it **sells out** to other countries are **exports**. Why trade at all? Because places **specialise** based on what they have. Hot, wet countries can grow **bananas and cocoa**; some countries have lots of a mineral to **mine**; others have big **factories** to manufacture goods; others sell **services**. The UK imports bananas (we can't grow them) and exports things like cars and services. So your breakfast and your gadgets are the end of a long **global journey**.

Jobs come in three families. **Primary** jobs take raw materials from the earth and sea — **farming, fishing, mining, forestry**. **Secondary** jobs **make** things from those materials — factory and building work. **Tertiary** jobs are **services** that help people — shops, teachers, doctors, drivers, programmers. Rich, modern economies (like the UK's) have mostly tertiary jobs now; the raw materials are often grown or dug elsewhere and traded to us.

**Worked example.** Trace a chocolate bar around the world. **Primary:** cocoa beans are **grown** in a hot country near the Equator (e.g. in West Africa) — that's a primary job and, for the UK, an **import**. **Secondary:** the beans are shipped to a **factory** and **made** into chocolate. **Tertiary:** a **shop** sells it to you, and **lorries** brought it there. One bar touches primary, secondary and tertiary jobs and crosses oceans through **trade**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A "label hunt" at home: where were your clothes, fruit and toys made? Plot them on a world map.
- Why can't we grow bananas or cocoa outdoors in the UK? (Climate — link to biomes.)
- Pretend trade game: two "countries" with different resources swap to get what each lacks.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Everything we buy is made in our own country."
  **Why:** goods just appear in shops.
  **Fix:** check real labels — most things travel from other countries through **trade** (imports).
- **Wrong idea:** mixing up imports and exports.
  **Why:** the words sound similar.
  **Fix:** im**port** = comes **in**(to port); ex**port** = goes **ex**(out). Bananas are an *import* to the UK.
- **Wrong idea:** "Farming/mining and factory work and shop work are all the same kind of job."
  **Why:** no framework.
  **Fix:** sort them — primary (raw materials), secondary (making), tertiary (services).

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is the difference between an import and an export? **A:** An import comes *into* a country from abroad; an export is sold *out* to another country.
- **Q:** Why does the UK import bananas instead of growing them? **A:** Bananas need a hot, wet climate the UK doesn't have, so we trade for them.
- **Q:** Sort these jobs into primary/secondary/tertiary: a farmer, a factory worker, a teacher. **A:** Farmer = primary; factory worker = secondary; teacher = tertiary.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize + map.* Sort jobs into primary/secondary/tertiary, and sort products as "UK can make/grow" vs "must import." Then trace one product's journey (primary→secondary→tertiary) and mark its start country on a world map. (`categorize` then `speak`.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "Trade / economic activity / where food comes from"**.
- **National Geographic Kids — country profiles** (for what different countries grow, mine and make).

## 10. Climate change at a child's level — and how geographers study a changing world

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – physical & human geography (environment, sustainability)

**Prerequisites:** Climate zones & biomes; rivers & the water cycle; weather & seasons. **Tutor: hopeful and factual, not frightening — emphasise that people are acting and can help.**

### Key concepts
- **Climate** is the usual long-term pattern of weather; **climate change** means that pattern is **slowly shifting** — on average, the Earth is **warming**.
- The main cause is extra **greenhouse gases** (especially **carbon dioxide**) from burning **fossil fuels** (coal, oil, gas), which trap more of the Sun's heat like a blanket.
- Effects include **melting ice, rising seas and more extreme weather** — but people are responding with **clean energy, trees and everyday choices**, and children can be part of the solution.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the difference between weather (today) and climate (long-term pattern), and the water cycle. Keep it calm and solution-focused.

### Teach it (the explanation)
*(Tutor: keep this hopeful and factual. State the science simply, then spend time on what people are doing and what the child can do — avoid doom.)*

Remember that **weather** is what's happening today, and **climate** is the **usual long-term pattern**. **Climate change** means that long-term pattern is **slowly shifting** — and overall the Earth is getting a little **warmer** on average.

Why? Think of a cosy **blanket** of gases around the Earth that naturally keeps it warm enough for life — the **greenhouse effect**. The trouble is, when we burn **fossil fuels** (coal, oil and gas) for power, cars and factories, we add extra **greenhouse gases**, mostly **carbon dioxide (CO₂)**. A thicker blanket traps **more** of the Sun's heat, so the Earth warms up more than it should.

That warming **changes the climate**: ice at the poles and on mountains **melts**, **seas rise** a little, and we get **more extreme weather** — fiercer storms, droughts and floods in some places. This affects people, animals and habitats. But here's the hopeful part: we know the causes, so we can act. People are switching to **clean energy** (wind, solar), planting and protecting **trees** (which soak up CO₂), wasting less, and travelling more cleanly. And *you* can help too — using less, walking and cycling, saving energy, and caring for nature. Geographers measure all this with **data** over many years, which is how they can tell a real long-term change from one odd hot or cold day.

**Worked example.** "Why doesn't one snowy winter prove there's no climate change?" Separate weather from climate: **weather** is one day or one season and jumps around; **climate change** is the **average over many years**. A single cold week is *weather*; the *trend* across decades is what's warming. Geographers look at **long-term data**, not one day — that's how they tell the difference.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A jar/greenhouse demo idea: how glass traps warmth (the greenhouse effect in miniature).
- "Energy detectives" at home: which things use power, and how could we use less?
- Plant something / look after a tree — link a small action to soaking up CO₂.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "It was cold today, so climate change isn't real."
  **Why:** confusing weather (today) with climate (long-term).
  **Fix:** climate change is the **average over many years**; one cold day is just weather. Look at the long-term trend.
- **Wrong idea:** "There's nothing anyone can do."
  **Why:** the problem feels too big.
  **Fix:** people *are* acting (clean energy, trees, less waste), and small everyday choices add up — frame it hopefully.
- **Wrong idea:** "The greenhouse effect is entirely bad."
  **Why:** "greenhouse gases" sounds like pollution only.
  **Fix:** a *natural* greenhouse effect keeps Earth warm enough for life; the problem is the **extra** gases making the blanket too thick.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is the main cause of recent climate change? **A:** Extra greenhouse gases (especially carbon dioxide) from burning fossil fuels, trapping more of the Sun's heat.
- **Q:** Why doesn't one cold day disprove climate change? **A:** That's weather; climate change is the long-term average over many years — geographers look at the trend.
- **Q:** Name one thing people (or you) can do to help. **A:** Any of: use clean energy, plant/protect trees, waste less, save energy, walk/cycle.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize + speak.* Sort actions into "adds greenhouse gases" vs "helps the climate" (driving a petrol car / planting trees / using wind power / wasting energy / cycling). Then a hopeful *speak* prompt: name one action you could take and explain why it helps. (`categorize` then `speak`.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "Climate change" (child-level)** and **"Caring for the environment / sustainability"**.
- **Met Office — "Climate change for kids"** and **National Geographic Kids — "Climate change"** (free, age-appropriate).

## 11. Fieldwork methods — being a geographer outdoors

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–6 (ages 8–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – geographical skills & fieldwork (methods)

**Prerequisites:** My local area & the compass (KS1); map skills; settlements & land use.

### Key concepts
- **Fieldwork** is collecting your own geographical data outdoors by **observing, measuring, counting and recording**.
- Geographers ask a **question**, choose a **method** (a tally count, a survey, a sketch map, measuring), collect the data, then **present and explain** it.
- Good fieldwork is **systematic and fair** — same method each time, recorded carefully — so the results mean something.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can use a simple map and compass, and has met data/charts (tally, pictogram, bar chart). An outdoor space (garden, street, park) helps.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Geographers don't only read maps — they go **outside** to find things out for themselves. That's **fieldwork**: collecting your *own* data about a real place by **observing, measuring, counting and recording**. It follows a clear cycle, like a science investigation:

1. **Ask a question** — e.g. "What is the traffic like on our street?" or "Where are the litter bins, and is there enough?"
2. **Choose a method** — a **tally count** (count cars or people in 10 minutes), a **survey/questionnaire** (ask people their opinion), a **sketch map** (draw the area with a key), or **measuring** (e.g. how wide a path is, or the temperature in sun vs shade).
3. **Collect the data carefully** — use the *same* method each time so it's fair (count for the *same* number of minutes; ask everyone the *same* question).
4. **Present it** — a tally chart, pictogram, bar chart, or labelled sketch map.
5. **Explain and conclude** — answer your question using the data ("most traffic was at 8–9 am, so the street is busiest then").

The golden rule is **be systematic and fair**: messy, changing methods give meaningless results.

**Worked example.** Question: "Is our local park well used?" Method: stand safely (with a grown-up) and do a **tally count** of how many people use the park in **10 minutes** at two different times. Data: 9 am → 4 people; 4 pm → 22 people. Present it as a small bar chart. Conclude: the park is used **much more after school**, because that's when children and families are free. A real question, answered with your own fairly-collected data.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A "traffic survey" or "people count" outside the house or school (with a grown-up).
- Map your street and survey the family on what they'd improve (a real questionnaire).
- Measure temperature in sun vs shade, or count bird types in the garden over a week.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Fieldwork is just looking around / a day off."
  **Why:** outdoors feels unstructured.
  **Fix:** real fieldwork follows a method — ask, measure, record, present, explain — like a science experiment.
- **Wrong idea:** "I can count for 2 minutes here and 10 minutes there — it's all data."
  **Why:** not seeing why fairness matters.
  **Fix:** use the **same method** each time (same minutes, same question) or you can't compare — be **systematic**.
- **Wrong idea:** "I collect the numbers and I'm done."
  **Why:** stopping at data.
  **Fix:** you must **present and explain** it to *answer the question* — data only matters once you interpret it.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is fieldwork in geography? **A:** Collecting your own data outdoors by observing, measuring, counting and recording to answer a question.
- **Q:** Put the fieldwork steps in order: present, ask a question, collect data, choose a method, conclude. **A:** Ask a question → choose a method → collect data → present → conclude.
- **Q:** Why must you use the same method each time? **A:** So it's fair and you can compare the results — systematic data is meaningful; messy data isn't.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + build.* Order the fieldwork cycle, then plan a tiny investigation: write a question, pick a method (tally / survey / sketch map), and say how you'd present the result. Carry out a 10-minute count if a safe space is available, with a grown-up. (`ordering` then `speak`/build.)

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "Fieldwork / collecting and presenting geographical data"**.
- **Royal Geographical Society (online) — "Fieldwork in the primary classroom"** (parent/teacher guidance).

## 12. Biomes in depth — adaptation, food chains & people in the rainforest

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – physical geography (biomes, ecosystems) + science links

**Prerequisites:** Climate zones & biomes; rivers & the water cycle.

### Key concepts
- A **biome** isn't just a place — it's a whole **ecosystem** where climate, plants, animals and people are **linked together**.
- Living things are **specially adapted** to their biome (rainforest layers; desert water-savers; tundra cold-survivors), and they connect through **food chains**.
- **People live in and depend on** biomes too (e.g. rainforest peoples), and human actions like **deforestation** affect the whole system — so biomes need protecting.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can name the main biomes and the idea of adaptation; knows about climate by latitude.

### Teach it (the explanation)
You've met the world's **biomes** — rainforest, desert, grassland, temperate forest, tundra, polar. Now go deeper: a biome is a whole **ecosystem**, where the climate, plants, animals **and people** are all **linked**.

Take the **tropical rainforest**. Its hot, wet climate (near the Equator) grows dense forest in **layers**: tall **emergent** trees poking out the top, a thick green **canopy** roof, a dim **understorey**, and the dark **forest floor**. Animals are **adapted** to each layer — monkeys and birds in the canopy, jaguars on the floor, climbing plants reaching for light. Compare the **desert**: plants and animals there are **water-savers** (cacti store water; some animals are active only at cool night). In the **tundra**, life survives bitter cold (low-growing plants, thick-furred animals). The biome's climate shapes the adaptations.

Everything connects through **food chains**: in a grassland, grass → grasshopper → bird → hawk; the **Sun's energy** flows along the chain. And crucially, **people are part of biomes too** — many communities live *in* the rainforest and depend on it. Human actions matter: cutting down forest (**deforestation**) for timber or farmland breaks the system, harms wildlife and releases CO₂. That's why protecting biomes — and using them sustainably — is a big geographical concern (it links to the climate-change topic).

**Worked example.** Build a rainforest food chain and explain an adaptation. Chain: **leaves** (made using the Sun) → **insect** that eats leaves → **frog** that eats the insect → **snake** that eats the frog. Each arrow shows energy passing on. Now an adaptation: a sloth has **strong hooked claws** to hang in the **canopy** and **green-tinged fur** to hide — perfectly suited to its layer of the rainforest. Climate → layered forest → animals adapted to each layer, all linked in chains.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Build the four rainforest layers and place the right animal in each.
- "Adaptation detective": why does a camel have humps, a polar bear thick fur, a cactus spines?
- Link to climate change: why does protecting the rainforest help the whole planet?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "A biome is just a type of weather / a single place."
  **Why:** earlier we focused on climate.
  **Fix:** a biome is a whole **ecosystem** — climate *plus* its linked plants, animals and people.
- **Wrong idea:** "Nobody lives in the rainforest."
  **Why:** it looks like pure wilderness.
  **Fix:** many communities live in and depend on the rainforest; people are part of biomes.
- **Wrong idea:** "In a food chain, the arrow points to who eats whom."
  **Why:** intuitive but backwards.
  **Fix:** the arrow shows **energy flowing** *to* the eater — grass → grasshopper means energy goes *from* grass *to* the grasshopper that eats it.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name the four layers of a tropical rainforest. **A:** Emergent (top), canopy, understorey, forest floor.
- **Q:** Give one adaptation of an animal or plant to its biome. **A:** e.g. cactus stores water (desert); polar bear has thick fur/fat (polar); sloth has hooked claws for the canopy (rainforest).
- **Q:** Why does protecting biomes like the rainforest matter? **A:** They're whole ecosystems — homes to wildlife and people; cutting them down harms species, communities and the climate (releases CO₂).

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + categorize.* Build a correct food chain with arrows showing energy flow, then sort animals/plants by biome and match each to one adaptation. (`ordering` then `matchPairs`; optional `speak`: explain one adaptation and why the climate causes it.)

- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "Rainforest layers / biomes / food chains"** and **science "Habitats & adaptation"**.
- **National Geographic Kids — "Rainforest / desert / tundra"** habitat pages with animals.
