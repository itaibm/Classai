# History — Key Stage 1 (Years 1–2, ages 5–7)

KS1 history is told as **stories**, anchored on a **simple timeline**, and always tied to "how do we know?" and "what changed?". Children compare *now* with the past within living memory, meet a few events and people from beyond living memory, and start to use words like *past, present, before, after, a long time ago*. Keep dates light; keep order and meaning strong.

---

## 1. Changes within living memory — then and now

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 1 (ages 5–6) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** History – changes within living memory

**Prerequisites:** none.

### Key concepts
- The **past** is the time before now; the **present** is now. We can put events in order: first, then, next, now.
- Things change over time — toys, homes, schools and clothes were different when grandparents were young.
- We find out about the recent past by **asking people** who remember it and **looking at old photos and objects**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child knows roughly what their own day looks like and that they are younger than their parents and grandparents.

### Teach it (the explanation)
"A long time ago" doesn't have to mean dinosaurs! Some of the past is close — it's the time your **grandparents** can remember. That's called *living memory*: things that people alive today still recall.

Imagine a line. On the right is **today** — you. Move left to your **parents** as children, then your **grandparents** as children. That's about 60 or 70 years. Lots changed in that short time. Toys were made of wood and metal, not plastic and screens. Telephones had a dial and a wire and sat on a table — you couldn't carry them. Many homes had no computer and only a few TV channels in black and white. Children walked to a school with a blackboard and chalk.

How do we *know*? Two ways. We **ask** the people who were there ("Grandma, what toys did you play with?") — they are living sources. And we look at **old photographs and objects** kept in homes and museums.

**Worked example.** Sort three telephones in order from oldest to newest: (1) a phone with a round dial and a curly wire, (2) a chunky phone with buttons, (3) a flat smartphone. Oldest → newest = dial phone → button phone → smartphone. How do we know the dial one is oldest? Photos and grandparents tell us dial phones came first, then buttons, then touchscreens.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Bring out an old family photo or object and play "spot the difference: then vs now."
- Compare a toy the child loves with a toy from 60 years ago (a wooden train, a spinning top).
- "Interview" a grandparent or older friend about school when they were little.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "The olden days" = dinosaurs / castles / everything old all mixed together.
  **Why:** young children have no scale for time, so all "past" collapses into one blur.
  **Fix:** use the close timeline (you → parents → grandparents) and the phrase *living memory*; keep dinosaurs and castles off this particular line — they're much, much further back.
- **Wrong idea:** people in the past were silly or stupid because they didn't have phones/cars.
  **Why:** judging the past by today's gadgets.
  **Fix:** point out they were just as clever; they used what they had — they invented the very things we use now.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Put these in order, oldest first: smartphone, dial telephone, button telephone. **A:** dial telephone, button telephone, smartphone.
- **Q:** Name one way we can find out what life was like when your grandparents were young. **A:** Ask them (they remember), or look at old photos/objects.
- **Q:** Tell me one thing that has changed and one thing that has stayed the same about school since your grandparents were children. **A:** e.g. changed — computers/whiteboards; same — children learn to read and write, play at break.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering block.* Show 3–4 pictures of the same thing across time (phones, toys, or a classroom). Child drags them oldest → newest, then says how they know.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 History — "What was life like a long time ago?"** (changes within living memory, then-and-now comparisons).
- **BBC Teach — "How toys have changed"** (short class clips comparing old and new toys).

## 2. The Great Fire of London (1666)

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – events beyond living memory

**Prerequisites:** Changes within living memory — then and now.

### Key concepts
- An important event from **long before living memory** — over 350 years ago, in 1666.
- **Cause & consequence:** the fire spread fast *because* houses were wooden and crammed together; *as a result* London was rebuilt in brick and stone, with wider streets.
- **How we know:** we know so much because a man called **Samuel Pepys** wrote about it in his diary — a written source.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child understands "a long time ago" means before grandparents, and that we learn about the past from sources.

### Teach it (the explanation)
In September **1666**, a fire started in the middle of the night in a **baker's shop on Pudding Lane** in London. A small fire — but it became a disaster.

Why did it spread so fast? Three reasons, all *causes*: the houses were made of **wood** and packed **close together**, often almost touching across narrow streets; that summer had been **hot and dry**, so everything was like kindling; and a strong **wind** carried the flames from roof to roof. There was no fire brigade with engines and hoses — people fought it with leather buckets of water and by pulling houses down to make gaps.

The fire burned for about **four days** and destroyed thousands of houses and old St Paul's Cathedral. Amazingly, very few people are recorded as dying. Afterwards came the *consequences*: London was rebuilt in **brick and stone** (which don't burn like wood), with **wider streets**, and a proper fire service grew up over time. A tall column called **The Monument** still stands where the fire was, to remember it.

How do we know all this? A government worker named **Samuel Pepys** kept a **diary** and wrote down what he saw — even burying his cheese and wine to save them! His diary is a famous written **source**.

**Worked example.** Question: "Why did the fire spread so quickly?" Build the answer from causes: (1) houses were wooden → easy to burn; (2) houses were close together → flames jumped between them; (3) it was hot, dry and windy → fire moved fast. So: wooden, crowded houses + dry windy weather = fast-spreading fire.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Look at a painting of the burning city (the orange sky) and "read" it like a photo of the event.
- Compare 1666 firefighting (buckets, pulling houses down) with a modern fire engine.
- Read a line from Samuel Pepys's diary and talk about why a diary is useful to historians.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong answer:** "Lots and lots of people died in the fire."
  **Why:** assuming a big fire must mean a big death toll, like in films.
  **Fix:** explain the records show very few recorded deaths because people could run from a slow-moving fire; the *houses* were destroyed, not mostly people.
- **Wrong answer:** "It was just bad luck / no reason."
  **Why:** missing cause and consequence.
  **Fix:** name the causes (wood, crowding, dry windy weather, no fire brigade) so the child sees it had clear reasons and led to real changes.
- **Wrong idea:** the Great Fire happened "in the olden days" with knights and castles.
  **Why:** all of the past collapses together.
  **Fix:** place 1666 on the timeline — after castles and knights, long before cars; about 350 years ago.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Where and when did the Great Fire of London start? **A:** In a baker's shop on Pudding Lane, in 1666.
- **Q:** Give two reasons the fire spread so fast. **A:** Any two of: houses were wooden; houses were close together; the weather was hot, dry and windy; no fire engines to stop it.
- **Q:** How do we know so much about the Great Fire? **A:** Samuel Pepys wrote about it in his diary (a written source); paintings and records survive too.
- **Q:** Name one thing that changed in London because of the fire. **A:** Houses were rebuilt in brick/stone; streets were made wider; a fire service developed.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering block.* Sequence the story: fire starts in the bakery → wind spreads it across wooden houses → fire burns for days → London is rebuilt in brick. Then a *multipleChoice*: "Which house is safer from fire — wood or brick?" (brick) and "why?".

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 History — "The Great Fire of London"** (animated story, causes and consequences).
- **BBC Teach — "The Great Fire of London"** clips (eyewitness Samuel Pepys, how it spread).

## 3. Significant explorers — Columbus & Armstrong

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – significant individuals

**Prerequisites:** Changes within living memory — then and now.

### Key concepts
- An **explorer** travels to places to find out what is there. We compare two: **Christopher Columbus** (sailed across the Atlantic in 1492) and **Neil Armstrong** (first person to walk on the Moon, 1969).
- **Similarity & difference:** both were brave explorers of the unknown, but lived ~500 years apart and travelled in completely different ways (wooden sailing ships vs a rocket).
- Exploration changes how people understand the world — and can affect the people already living in the places "discovered."

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child knows that the past comes in different distances (grandparents vs much longer ago) and can compare two things for same/different.

### Teach it (the explanation)
An **explorer** is someone who goes to find out about places people don't know much about. Let's meet two, far apart in time.

**Christopher Columbus**, over 500 years ago (**1492**), sailed three small **wooden ships** west across the huge Atlantic Ocean. Most people in Europe didn't know the Americas were there. After weeks at sea his crew were frightened, but they reached land in the Caribbean. Important and honest point: Columbus didn't "discover an empty place" — **millions of people already lived in the Americas**, and the arrival of European ships brought great harm to them. So we remember Columbus as a significant explorer *and* we tell the whole truth about what followed.

**Neil Armstrong**, much more recently (**1969**), explored somewhere no human had ever stood: the **Moon**. He travelled not by ship but in a **rocket**, Apollo 11, and stepped onto the Moon's dust saying, "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind."

Same and different? **Same:** both were brave, went somewhere unknown, and changed what people understood about the world. **Different:** Columbus used wind and wooden sailing ships across an ocean; Armstrong used rocket engines across space — and they lived almost 500 years apart.

**Worked example.** Sort facts under the right explorer. "Sailed wooden ships in 1492" → Columbus. "Walked on the Moon in 1969" → Armstrong. "Was a brave explorer who travelled somewhere unknown" → **both**.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A child who likes space loves Armstrong; a child who likes the sea/pirates loves the wooden-ship voyage.
- Compare the tools: a compass and sail vs a rocket and spacesuit.
- Globe/Moon: trace Columbus's route across the Atlantic, then point up at the Moon.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Columbus discovered America (it was empty)."
  **Why:** the old simplified story leaves out the people who already lived there.
  **Fix:** say clearly that millions of people already lived in the Americas; Columbus was the first to sail there *from Europe*, and his arrival brought harm to those peoples.
- **Wrong idea:** Columbus and Armstrong lived around the same time.
  **Why:** "explorer" feels like one category, so they get squashed together.
  **Fix:** put both on the timeline — Columbus ~500 years ago, Armstrong in your grandparents' time; almost 500 years apart.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What does an *explorer* do? **A:** Travels to find out about places that aren't well known.
- **Q:** Which explorer walked on the Moon, and roughly when? **A:** Neil Armstrong, in 1969 (your grandparents' lifetime).
- **Q:** Name one thing that was the same and one thing that was different about Columbus and Armstrong. **A:** Same — both brave, both explored the unknown; different — ships vs rocket, ~500 years apart.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize block.* Drag facts/pictures (wooden ship, rocket, 1492, 1969, Atlantic Ocean, the Moon, brave explorer) into "Columbus", "Armstrong", or "Both".

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 History — "Significant explorers"** (Columbus, and other explorers compared).
- **BBC Teach — "Neil Armstrong and the Moon landing"** clips.

## 4. Significant nurses — Florence Nightingale & Mary Seacole

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 35 · **Subject strand:** History – significant individuals

**Prerequisites:** Changes within living memory — then and now.

### Key concepts
- **Florence Nightingale** and **Mary Seacole** both helped sick and wounded soldiers during the **Crimean War** (1850s), about 170 years ago.
- **Significance:** Nightingale changed how hospitals work — clean, quiet, well-organised wards — so we still call her the founder of modern nursing.
- We can be **significant** in different ways; Mary Seacole, a brave Jamaican-British woman, set up her own "British Hotel" near the battlefield to care for soldiers.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child understands "a long time ago" beyond living memory and that people can be remembered for what they changed.

### Teach it (the explanation)
About **170 years ago** there was a war (the Crimean War) where many soldiers were hurt and got sick. The hospitals were **dirty, dark and crowded**, and more men were dying from infection than from their wounds.

**Florence Nightingale** went out to nurse them. She made the wards **clean**, opened windows for fresh air, fed the men properly and organised the care. At night she carried a lamp as she checked on patients — people called her "**the Lady with the Lamp**." Fewer soldiers died. When she came home she trained new nurses and wrote about how hospitals should be run. That's why she is *significant*: she changed nursing for everyone, right up to today.

**Mary Seacole** was a brave nurse from **Jamaica** with both Jamaican and Scottish family. She knew a lot about treating fevers. She wanted to help too, but was turned away — partly because of the colour of her skin. So she paid her own way to the war and set up the "**British Hotel**" near the fighting, where she nursed and fed soldiers, sometimes right by the battlefield. The soldiers loved "Mother Seacole."

Both women were significant nurses; comparing them shows there's more than one way to be brave and to make a difference.

**Worked example.** "Why do we still remember Florence Nightingale?" Because she made hospitals clean and organised, fewer soldiers died, and she trained nurses and changed how all hospitals are run — that lasting change is what *significant* means.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Link to a child who's been to hospital or had a nurse: nurses keep things clean *because* of people like Nightingale.
- "Lady with the Lamp" — act out checking on patients at night with a torch.
- Map link: trace Mary Seacole's journey from Jamaica to the war.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** Nightingale is famous "just for being kind."
  **Why:** missing why she's *significant* rather than just nice.
  **Fix:** stress the lasting change — clean, organised hospitals and trained nurses that we still have today.
- **Wrong idea:** only one of them mattered / they're the same person.
  **Why:** two nurses in one war get merged.
  **Fix:** compare them — same war, both nursed soldiers; but Seacole came from Jamaica and ran her own "British Hotel," and faced unfair treatment because of her skin colour.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What did Florence Nightingale change about hospitals? **A:** She made them clean, organised and well-run, so fewer patients died; she trained nurses.
- **Q:** Who was Mary Seacole and where was she from? **A:** A brave nurse from Jamaica who set up the "British Hotel" to care for soldiers near the battlefield.
- **Q:** What does it mean to say someone is *significant* in history? **A:** They made a change that still matters / lasts a long time, not just that they were famous.

### Activity / practice
*MatchPairs block.* Match each nurse to facts: "Lady with the Lamp / made wards clean" → Nightingale; "from Jamaica / ran the British Hotel" → Seacole; "nursed soldiers in the Crimean War" → both.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 History — "Florence Nightingale"** and **"Mary Seacole"** (significant individuals).
- **BBC Teach — "Florence Nightingale and Mary Seacole"** clips (the Crimean War, what each did).

## 5. Local history — a significant place or person near home

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 2 (ages 6–7) · **Est. minutes:** 30 · **Subject strand:** History – local history

**Prerequisites:** Changes within living memory — then and now.

### Key concepts
- History happened **right where you live**, not only in faraway places. Your town/village has a past.
- A **significant local place or person** — an old building, a statue, a street name, a famous local — is a window into that past.
- We use **local sources**: old photos of your street, the museum, plaques, gravestones, and people who remember.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- The child can compare then-and-now and knows we learn about the past from sources (photos, objects, people).

### Teach it (the explanation)
*(Tutor: personalise this to the learner's actual town — ask the parent or learner for one local landmark, old building, statue or street name. The structure below works anywhere.)*

Every place has a story. Look around your own town. That **old church or building** might be hundreds of years old. That **statue** or **blue plaque** marks someone who mattered here. Even a **street name** can be a clue — a "Mill Lane" once had a mill; a "Castle Street" once had a castle.

We can be history detectives about our own area. Find an **old photograph** of your high street and hold it up next to how it looks today: what's the same (the church, the shape of the road)? what's different (shops, cars, clothes)? Visit a **local museum** or read a **plaque**. Ask an older neighbour what the area was like when they were young.

Why does this matter? Because it shows history isn't only about kings and faraway wars — it's under your feet. Your town changed over time, just like toys and telephones did, and you can find the evidence yourself.

**Worked example.** Pick a local landmark — say an old water mill. Ask: "How do we know it's old?" (the date carved on it; old photos show it working; the street is called Mill Lane). "What was it for?" (grinding grain into flour). "What changed?" (it's now a café/museum; flour comes from big factories). That's reading a single local source for cause, change and evidence.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A walk (real or on a digital map) past one old building near home.
- Old vs new photo of the same local street — "spot the difference."
- A family connection: did anyone in the family live or work locally long ago?

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Nothing historical ever happened *here* — history is only in London / Egypt / on TV."
  **Why:** history feels distant and grand, never local.
  **Fix:** point to one concrete old thing nearby (building, plaque, street name) as proof the past is local too.
- **Wrong idea:** an old building has "always looked like that."
  **Why:** no sense of change over time.
  **Fix:** show an old photo of the same place to reveal what changed and what stayed.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name one kind of clue that tells us about your area's past. **A:** An old building/church, a statue or plaque, a street name, old photos, or an older person's memories.
- **Q:** How could you find out what your high street looked like 100 years ago? **A:** Look at old photographs (library/museum/online), read plaques, or ask older residents.
- **Q:** A street is called "Mill Lane" but there's no mill now. What does that tell us? **A:** There probably used to be a mill there long ago — the name is a clue to the past.

### Activity / practice
*Speak / fillBlank block.* Child names one significant place or person from their own town and says one thing about its past ("___ is old because ___; it used to be ___ but now it is ___"). If a real local example isn't to hand, use old-vs-new photos of any high street.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS1 History — "Local history"** (how to investigate where you live).
- **Historic England — "Search the List" / local listed buildings** (look up an old building near you; parent-guided).
- **Your local library or museum website** (old photographs of your area; ask staff for the local history collection).
