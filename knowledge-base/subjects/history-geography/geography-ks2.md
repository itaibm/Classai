# Geography — Key Stage 2 (Years 3–6, ages 7–11)

KS2 geography deepens from naming places to understanding **systems and processes**: the grid of latitude/longitude, climate zones and biomes, the water cycle and rivers, volcanoes and earthquakes, settlements and land use, and real map skills (grid references, symbols, contours). It ends with comparing real **regions** of the UK, Europe and the Americas. Keep the globe, an atlas, and an Ordnance Survey map to hand.

---

## 1. Latitude, longitude, hemispheres & time zones

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 3–4 (ages 7–9) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – locational knowledge (the global grid)

**Prerequisites:** Continents & oceans; hot & cold places (KS1).

### Key concepts
- The Earth has an imaginary **grid**: lines of **latitude** run **across** (the Equator is 0°), lines of **longitude** run **up-and-down** (the Prime Meridian, at Greenwich, is 0°).
- These lines split the Earth into **hemispheres** (Northern/Southern by the Equator; Eastern/Western by the Prime Meridian) and let us give any place an exact location.
- Because the Earth **spins**, different places have different **times of day** — the world is divided into **time zones**.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can use a globe, find the Equator and the poles, and knows hot places are near the Equator.

### Teach it (the explanation)
To find any place on Earth exactly, we draw an imaginary **grid** on the globe. The across-lines are **latitude**. The most important is the **Equator** around the middle, called **0°**; latitude is measured in degrees north or south of it, up to 90° at each pole. Special latitude lines include the **Tropics** (the edges of the hottest zone) and the **Arctic and Antarctic Circles** (the cold zones).

The up-and-down lines, from pole to pole, are **longitude**. The starting one is the **Prime Meridian** at **0°**, which runs through **Greenwich in London** — a British claim to fame. Longitude is measured east or west of it.

These lines split the Earth into halves called **hemispheres**: the **Equator** divides Northern from Southern (the UK is in the **Northern** Hemisphere); the **Prime Meridian** divides Eastern from Western. Give a latitude *and* a longitude and you can pinpoint anywhere — that's how a GPS knows where you are.

Because the Earth **spins** once a day, the Sun reaches different places at different moments — so when it's lunchtime in London it's the middle of the night in Australia. To cope with this, the world is divided into **time zones**.

**Worked example.** "Which hemisphere is the UK in?" The UK is **north** of the Equator → **Northern Hemisphere**; and it sits right on the **Prime Meridian** (0° longitude, through Greenwich). So locating it uses both lines: north of the Equator, on the Prime Meridian.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Greenwich and the Prime Meridian — "the world sets its time from London."
- Time zones: why a video call to family abroad happens at a strange hour for them.
- GPS / map apps use latitude and longitude to find you exactly.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** the lines of latitude/longitude are real lines painted on the Earth.
  **Why:** they're drawn on every globe.
  **Fix:** they're **imaginary** reference lines we agreed on to measure position — you'd see nothing on the ground.
- **Wrong idea:** mixing up latitude and longitude.
  **Why:** the words are similar.
  **Fix:** **lat**itude lines are the flat "**lad**ders/rungs" going **across** (like the Equator); longitude lines run the **long** way, pole to pole.
- **Wrong idea:** it's the same time everywhere on Earth.
  **Why:** clocks at home all agree.
  **Fix:** the spinning Earth means the Sun reaches places at different times → **time zones**; it can be a different day in Australia.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is the Equator's latitude, and what does it divide the Earth into? **A:** 0°; it divides the Northern and Southern Hemispheres.
- **Q:** Through which UK place does the Prime Meridian (0° longitude) run? **A:** Greenwich, London.
- **Q:** Which hemisphere is the UK in? **A:** The Northern Hemisphere.
- **Q:** Why is it a different time of day in Australia than in the UK? **A:** The Earth spins, so the Sun reaches them at a different time — they're in a different time zone.

### Activity / practice
*Labelling / matchPairs.* Label a globe: Equator (0° lat), Prime Meridian (0° long), North/South Hemispheres. Match: latitude→across; longitude→up-down; Greenwich→Prime Meridian.

- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "Latitude and longitude" / "Lines on the globe"** (Equator, Prime Meridian, hemispheres).
- **National Geographic Kids — "Latitude and longitude"** and **Royal Observatory Greenwich** (the Prime Meridian, time).

## 2. Climate zones & biomes

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–5 (ages 8–10) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – climate zones, biomes & vegetation belts

**Prerequisites:** Latitude, longitude & hemispheres; hot & cold places (KS1).

### Key concepts
- **Climate** is the **usual weather of a place over many years** (not today's weather). The world has broad **climate zones** linked to latitude — hot near the Equator, cold near the poles.
- A **biome** is a large region defined by its climate, plants and animals — e.g. **rainforest, desert, grassland, temperate forest, tundra, polar**.
- Plants and animals are **adapted** to their biome (cacti store water in deserts; thick fur survives the tundra).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows hot places are near the Equator and cold near the poles, and what latitude is.

### Teach it (the explanation)
First, an important difference: **weather** is what's happening today; **climate** is the **usual pattern over many years**. London's climate is "mild and rainy" even on a rare hot day.

Climate follows **latitude**. Near the **Equator** it's hot all year; near the **poles** it's cold all year; in between (like the UK) it's **temperate** — mild, with seasons. These bands are **climate zones**: tropical, temperate (and dry, polar and mountain zones too).

Within these zones, large natural areas with their own climate, plants and animals are called **biomes**. Key ones: **tropical rainforest** (hot, wet, dense trees, huge biodiversity — near the Equator); **desert** (very dry, hot days/cold nights — e.g. the Sahara); **grassland/savanna** (grasses, scattered trees — lions, elephants); **temperate forest** (the UK's leafy woods, four seasons); **tundra** (cold, treeless, frozen ground near the Arctic); and **polar** (ice, near the poles).

Living things are **adapted** to their biome. A **cactus** has a fat stem to store water and spines instead of leaves to lose less water — perfect for a desert. A **polar bear** has thick fur and fat for the freezing Arctic. The biome shapes the life, and the life fits the biome.

**Worked example.** "Why are there no big trees in a desert but a jungle near the Equator?" Climate decides it: deserts get almost **no rain**, so only tough plants like cacti survive; the Equator gets **heat plus heavy rain**, so dense rainforest grows. Same planet, different climate → different biome.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Match a favourite animal to its biome (camel→desert, sloth→rainforest, reindeer→tundra).
- Cactus vs rainforest leaf — how each plant is built for its climate.
- A biome the learner has seen on a wildlife documentary (savanna, jungle).

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Climate and weather are the same."
  **Why:** both about hot/cold/rain.
  **Fix:** weather = today and changes fast; **climate** = the long-term average. One sunny December day doesn't change the UK's mild climate.
- **Wrong idea:** "All deserts are hot and sandy."
  **Why:** the Sahara is the famous image.
  **Fix:** a desert is defined by being **very dry**, not hot — Antarctica is a (cold) desert; many deserts are rocky, not sandy.
- **Wrong idea:** "Animals can live anywhere."
  **Why:** seeing pets everywhere.
  **Fix:** wild animals are **adapted** to a particular biome; a polar bear couldn't survive a desert, nor a camel the Arctic.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What's the difference between weather and climate? **A:** Weather is today's conditions; climate is the usual pattern over many years.
- **Q:** Name three biomes. **A:** Any three of: rainforest, desert, grassland/savanna, temperate forest, tundra, polar.
- **Q:** What defines a desert? **A:** Very little rainfall (it's dry) — not necessarily hot or sandy.
- **Q:** Give one way a cactus is adapted to the desert. **A:** Stores water in its stem / has spines instead of leaves to lose less water.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize / matchPairs.* Sort animals and plants into their biome (cactus & camel→desert; sloth & vine→rainforest; reindeer→tundra). Match biome→climate (rainforest→hot & wet; desert→dry; tundra→cold).

- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "Climate zones" / "Biomes and vegetation belts"** (zones by latitude, the main biomes).
- **National Geographic Kids — "Biomes" / "Habitats"** (rainforest, desert, tundra with animals).

## 3. Rivers & the water cycle

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 4–5 (ages 8–10) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – physical geography (rivers & the water cycle)

**Prerequisites:** Weather & seasons (KS1); climate zones & biomes.

### Key concepts
- The **water cycle** moves water endlessly: **evaporation** → **condensation** (clouds) → **precipitation** (rain/snow) → **collection** (back to seas and rivers).
- A **river** flows from its **source** (often high ground/mountains) **downhill** to its **mouth** (where it meets the sea) — always from **high to low**, whatever compass direction that is.
- Rivers matter to people: drinking water, farming, transport, and the reason many cities are built on them.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows rain comes from clouds and water flows downhill; understands evaporation informally (puddles dry up).

### Teach it (the explanation)
Where does river water come from, and why does it never run out? Because of the **water cycle** — a never-ending loop powered by the **Sun**. Step by step: the Sun heats the sea, lakes and puddles, turning water into invisible vapour that rises — **evaporation**. High up it cools and turns back into tiny droplets, making **clouds** — **condensation**. When the droplets get heavy they fall as rain, snow or hail — **precipitation**. That water **collects** in streams and rivers, flows back to the sea, and the cycle starts again.

A **river** is the journey of that collected water back to the sea. It begins at its **source** — usually high up in hills or mountains where rain and springs gather. It then flows **downhill**, because water always moves from **high ground to low ground** (gravity). Small streams (tributaries) join and the river grows, until it reaches the **mouth**, where it flows into the **sea**. The key rule: a river flows **downhill**, in **whatever direction** down happens to be — north, south, east or west.

Rivers are vital to people. They give **drinking water**, water **crops**, carry **boats and goods**, and that's why so many great cities — London on the **Thames**, Paris on the **Seine** — grew up beside them.

**Worked example.** "Does a river always flow south?" No. Trace it: a river starts at its **source** on high ground and flows to its **mouth** at the sea, always going **downhill**. If the high ground is in the south and the sea is in the north, the river flows **north** (like the Nile!). Direction depends on the land's slope, not on the compass.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Pour water down a tilted tray of sand to watch a "river" find the lowest path.
- The local river or a famous one (Thames, Severn, Nile) — trace source to mouth on a map.
- "Where does your tap water come from?" — back up the cycle to rain and rivers.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Rivers always flow from north to south" / "downhill means south."
  **Why:** maps put north at the top, so "down the map" feels like "south."
  **Fix:** rivers flow from **high to low ground** in any compass direction — the Nile flows **north**; "down" on a map is south, but real "downhill" is about height, not map direction.
- **Wrong idea:** "Rain is brand-new water made in the clouds."
  **Why:** rain seems to appear from nowhere.
  **Fix:** it's the **same water recycled forever** — evaporated from seas/rivers, condensed into clouds, fallen again. The water you drink could once have been in a dinosaur's drink!
- **Wrong idea:** "A river starts at the sea."
  **Why:** the sea is where you see lots of water.
  **Fix:** a river **starts at its source** (high ground) and **ends at the sea** (its mouth); it flows away from the high land *to* the sea.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Name the four stages of the water cycle in order. **A:** Evaporation → condensation → precipitation → collection.
- **Q:** What do we call the start and end of a river? **A:** The source (start, high ground) and the mouth (end, at the sea).
- **Q:** Do rivers always flow south? Explain. **A:** No — they flow downhill (high to low) in any direction; e.g. the Nile flows north.
- **Q:** Why do many cities grow up next to rivers? **A:** For drinking water, farming, and transport of goods/people.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + labelling.* Order/label the water-cycle stages on a diagram (sun, sea, evaporation arrow, cloud, rain, river). Then label a river: source, tributary, mouth. *MultipleChoice*: "Which way does a river flow?" (downhill, any direction).

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "The water cycle" / "Rivers"** (the cycle, source to mouth, river uses).
- **National Geographic Kids — "The water cycle" / "Rivers"** (diagrams and famous rivers).

## 4. Mountains, volcanoes & earthquakes

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5 (ages 9–10) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – physical geography (tectonics)

**Prerequisites:** Rivers & the water cycle (physical processes); latitude & continents.

### Key concepts
- Earth's outer shell is cracked into giant moving **tectonic plates**; where they meet, things happen.
- **Mountains** can form when plates **push together** and crumple the land up; **volcanoes** form where hot **magma** escapes; **earthquakes** are the **shaking** when plates suddenly slip.
- These are powerful natural processes that shape the land and affect where and how people live (often near them for fertile soil — despite the risk).

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows the Earth is a layered ball, can name continents, and understands "downhill"/landforms from rivers.

### Teach it (the explanation)
The ground feels solid, but the Earth's **outer shell (crust)** is cracked into enormous pieces called **tectonic plates**, floating on hot, slowly moving rock beneath. The plates creep — only centimetres a year — but where their **edges meet**, the action happens.

**Mountains:** when two plates **push into each other**, the land between them gets squeezed and **buckles upward** into mountains over millions of years — that's how the **Himalayas** (with **Mount Everest**, the highest on Earth) keep rising.

**Volcanoes:** in some places, super-hot melted rock (**magma**) from deep inside the Earth finds a weak spot and bursts out at the surface as a **volcano**. When it erupts, magma becomes **lava**, with ash and gas. Volcanoes can be dangerous, but volcanic soil is very fertile, so people farm near them.

**Earthquakes:** plates don't slide smoothly — they catch and stick, then suddenly **slip**, releasing a jolt of energy that makes the ground **shake**. That's an **earthquake**. Big ones can damage buildings, so people in earthquake zones design buildings to sway safely.

Most volcanoes and earthquakes happen along plate edges — many around the Pacific Ocean, nicknamed the "**Ring of Fire**."

**Worked example.** "Why do mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes often happen in the same areas?" Because all three are caused by **plate boundaries**: plates **collide** → mountains rise; magma escapes there → volcanoes; plates **slip** there → earthquakes. So plate edges (like the Ring of Fire) get all three.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Build a baking-soda-and-vinegar volcano (the eruption demo).
- Mount Everest — the world's highest mountain, still rising; tallest peaks as records.
- "Ring of Fire" map — why Japan, Indonesia and California get earthquakes.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "Lava and magma are the same thing."
  **Why:** they're the same molten rock.
  **Fix:** it's **magma** when **underground**, and **lava** once it's **erupted** onto the surface — same rock, different place and name.
- **Wrong idea:** "Volcanoes and earthquakes happen randomly anywhere."
  **Why:** they seem to strike out of nowhere.
  **Fix:** they cluster along **plate boundaries** (e.g. the Ring of Fire); that's why some countries get many and others almost none.
- **Wrong idea:** "Mountains were made quickly / all at once."
  **Why:** they just seem to "be there."
  **Fix:** mountains form **very slowly** as plates push for **millions of years** — the Himalayas are still rising a little each year.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What are tectonic plates? **A:** Giant pieces of the Earth's cracked outer shell (crust) that slowly move.
- **Q:** What's the difference between magma and lava? **A:** Magma is molten rock underground; it's called lava once it erupts onto the surface.
- **Q:** What causes an earthquake? **A:** Tectonic plates catch then suddenly slip, releasing energy that shakes the ground.
- **Q:** Why do mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes often occur in the same regions? **A:** All three happen at plate boundaries (e.g. the Ring of Fire).

### Activity / practice
*Categorize / matchPairs.* Match cause→feature (plates collide→mountains; magma erupts→volcano; plates slip→earthquake). Label a volcano diagram (magma chamber, crater, lava, ash). *MultipleChoice* on magma vs lava.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "Volcanoes" / "Earthquakes" / "Mountains"** (tectonic plates, how each forms).
- **National Geographic Kids — "Volcano facts" / "Earthquakes"** (eruptions, the Ring of Fire).

## 5. Settlements & land use

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – human geography (settlements, land use, economy)

**Prerequisites:** Rivers & the water cycle (why settlements grow by water); map skills.

### Key concepts
- A **settlement** is a place where people live, from a tiny **hamlet** to a **village**, **town** and big **city**. Bigger settlements have more people and services.
- Settlements began where there were **good reasons (site factors)**: fresh water, flat dry land, food, shelter, defence and good travel routes.
- **Land use** describes what land is *for* — **residential** (homes), **commercial** (shops), **industrial** (factories), **agricultural** (farming), **recreational** (parks) — and it differs between countryside and city.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows rivers provide water/transport, can read a simple map, and understands human vs physical features.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Think about where people choose to live. A **settlement** is any place people make their home, and they come in sizes: a **hamlet** (a handful of houses), a **village** (bigger, maybe a shop and school), a **town** (lots of homes, shops and services), and a **city** (very large, with everything — hospitals, stations, businesses).

Long ago, people didn't settle just anywhere — they looked for **good site factors**: **fresh water** to drink (so many settlements sit by a **river** or spring); **flat, dry land** to build on; **good soil and food** nearby; **shelter** from weather; **defence** (hills or river bends were easier to protect); and good **routes** for travel and trade (crossing points, coasts, later roads and railways). That's why a place like London grew where the Thames could be crossed and ships could reach the sea.

Once a settlement grows, land gets used for different jobs — that's **land use**: **residential** areas (houses and flats), **commercial** areas (shops, offices), **industrial** areas (factories, warehouses), **agricultural** land (farms and fields), and **recreational** space (parks, sports grounds). A city centre is mostly commercial; the edges and countryside are more residential and agricultural. On a map you can spot land use by the pattern of buildings, green space and roads.

**Worked example.** "Why might a town grow at a river crossing?" Site factors stack up: the river gives **water**; a crossing point lets **roads/trade** meet; flat banks give **building land**; boats bring **transport**. Lots of reasons to live there → a settlement grows and, over time, becomes a town or city.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Classify the learner's own home as hamlet/village/town/city and justify it.
- "If you were starting a new town, where would you put it and why?" (find good site factors).
- Look at an aerial map and spot housing vs shops vs parks vs farmland.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "A city is just a big town with no real difference."
  **Why:** the boundary feels vague.
  **Fix:** size **and services** matter — cities have far more people and major services (hospitals, stations, universities); in the UK a city traditionally had special status/a cathedral.
- **Wrong idea:** "People settled places randomly."
  **Why:** towns just seem to "be there."
  **Fix:** settlements grew for **reasons (site factors)** — water, flat land, defence, trade routes; that's why so many sit on rivers or coasts.
- **Wrong idea:** "Land is either town or countryside, nothing in between."
  **Why:** simple two-way split.
  **Fix:** land has many **uses** — residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, recreational — often mixed together in one area.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** Put these settlement types in order from smallest to largest: city, hamlet, town, village. **A:** Hamlet, village, town, city.
- **Q:** Give two reasons people built settlements by rivers. **A:** Fresh water to drink; transport/trade by boat (also: fertile land, a crossing point, defence).
- **Q:** What does "land use" mean? Give two examples. **A:** What land is used for; e.g. residential (homes), commercial (shops), industrial (factories), agricultural (farms), recreational (parks).
- **Q:** Name one site factor that makes a good place to build a settlement. **A:** Any of: fresh water, flat dry land, good soil/food, shelter, defence, travel routes.

### Activity / practice
*Ordering + categorize.* Order settlement types smallest→largest. Then categorize land-use photos (houses→residential, shop→commercial, factory→industrial, field→agricultural, park→recreational). *Speak* prompt: justify where you'd found a new town.

### Resources
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "Settlements" / "Land use"** (settlement hierarchy, site factors, types of land use).
- **Ordnance Survey — "MapZone"** (read land use and settlements from real maps, free).

## 6. Map skills — grid references, symbols & contours

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 5–6 (ages 9–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – geographical skills & fieldwork (OS maps)

**Prerequisites:** Simple maps & the compass (KS1); settlements & land use.

### Key concepts
- Real **Ordnance Survey (OS) maps** use **symbols** and a **key/legend** to pack lots of information into a small space.
- A **grid** of numbered lines lets us give a **four-figure grid reference** to find any square — **read along the bottom first, then up** ("along the corridor, up the stairs").
- **Contour lines** join points of equal **height**, showing hills and valleys; lines close together = steep, far apart = gentle.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Knows a map is a bird's-eye, shrunk, simplified drawing with a key and the four/eight compass points.

### Teach it (the explanation)
Now to read a **real map** — an **Ordnance Survey (OS)** map, the kind walkers use. It's crammed with information using **symbols**, explained in the **key** (a church with a tower, a campsite, a footpath, woodland). Always check the key to decode them.

Across the map runs a **grid** of light blue squares with **numbered lines**. To tell someone exactly which square something is in, we give a **four-figure grid reference**. The rule: **read the numbers along the bottom first, then up the side** — remember "**along the corridor, then up the stairs**." So a four-figure reference like **3-4-2-1** means: go along to line **34**, then up to line **21**; the feature is in the square that starts at that corner. (Six-figure references split each square into tenths for even more precision — a Year 6 extension.)

To show **height** — hills and valleys — maps use **contour lines**: thin lines that join all the points at the **same height** above sea level, each labelled with its height. Read them like this: lines **close together** mean the land rises steeply (a steep slope or cliff); lines **far apart** mean a gentle slope; **circles** inside circles mean a hilltop. Contours turn a flat map into a 3-D picture of the land in your head.

**Worked example.** Find a grid reference. To locate the windmill symbol: read **along the bottom** to the line just left of it — say **42** — then **up the side** to the line just below it — say **17**. The four-figure grid reference is **4217**: along the corridor to 42, up the stairs to 17.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- A treasure hunt using grid references on a real OS map of the local area.
- Battleships-style game to drill "along then up" before doing it on a map.
- Walk a hill and then find its contour rings on the map — feel "steep = lines close."

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** read a grid reference **up first, then along**.
  **Why:** people read top-to-bottom in books.
  **Fix:** always **along the bottom first, then up** — "**along the corridor, up the stairs**." Getting the order wrong sends you to the wrong square.
- **Wrong idea:** "contour lines are paths/rivers you can walk along."
  **Why:** they look like trails on the map.
  **Fix:** contours are **imaginary lines of equal height**, not real features; they tell you the shape and steepness of the land.
- **Wrong idea:** "the symbols are just decoration."
  **Why:** the map looks busy.
  **Fix:** every symbol **means something** specific — the **key** is the dictionary; close together contours warn of a steep climb.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** When giving a four-figure grid reference, which numbers do you read first? **A:** The numbers along the bottom (across), then up the side — "along the corridor, up the stairs."
- **Q:** What do contour lines show? **A:** Height — they join points of equal height above sea level.
- **Q:** On a map, contour lines are very close together. What does that tell you? **A:** The land is steep there.
- **Q:** Where do you look to find out what a map symbol means? **A:** The key (legend).

### Activity / practice
*Number/grid-entry + matchPairs.* Give a four-figure grid reference for a marked symbol on a sample map (drill "along then up"). Match OS symbols→meanings. *MultipleChoice*: "Close contour lines mean the slope is ___" (steep).

### Resources
- **Ordnance Survey — "MapZone" / "Map reading made easy"** (free): grid references, symbols, contours, games.
- **BBC Bitesize KS2 Geography — "Map skills" / "Using grid references"** (four-figure references, keys, contours).

## 7. A region study — UK, Europe & the Americas

**Year / Key Stage:** Year 6 (ages 10–11) · **Est. minutes:** 40 · **Subject strand:** Geography – place knowledge (regional comparison)

**Prerequisites:** Climate zones & biomes; rivers; settlements & land use; map skills.

### Key concepts
- A **region** is a defined area we study in depth (e.g. a county or national park in the UK, a region of a European country, or a region of North/South America).
- We describe and **compare regions** using physical geography (landscape, rivers, climate, biome) and human geography (people, settlements, jobs, land use).
- Comparing places shows real **similarities and differences** — and helps us avoid stereotypes by looking at evidence.

### Prior knowledge assumed
- Can use the tools built up across KS2: climate zones, rivers, settlements/land use, and map reading.

### Teach it (the explanation)
A **region** is just a chosen area we get to know really well — for example a UK region (like the **Lake District** or a county), a region of a **European** country (like a region of **France** or **Italy**), and a region of the **Americas** (a region of the **USA**, or part of the Amazon in **South America**). The skill is to **describe and compare** them using everything you've learned.

To describe any region, ask two sets of questions. **Physical geography:** what's the **landscape** like (mountains, coast, flat)? Are there big **rivers**? What **climate zone** and **biome** is it in (temperate, Mediterranean, tropical)? **Human geography:** how many **people** live there and in what kinds of **settlements** (cities, villages)? What **jobs** and **land use** dominate (farming, industry, tourism)? What's daily life like?

Then **compare**. Take your own UK region and a contrasting one — say a region of the Amazon in Brazil. **Similarities:** both have rivers, both have towns and people who go to school and work. **Differences:** the UK region is **temperate** (mild, four seasons, temperate forest/farmland), while the Amazon region is **tropical rainforest** (hot, very wet, dense jungle); land use, wildlife and daily life differ a lot. Comparing with **evidence** (maps, photos, data) keeps us fair — every region has cities *and* countryside, rich *and* poor; real places are more varied than stereotypes.

**Worked example.** Compare a UK region with a region of the Americas. Lay it out: **Climate** — UK temperate vs (say) tropical rainforest; **Physical** — both have rivers, but very different vegetation; **Human** — both have settlements and farming, but different crops and ways of life. Two columns, same categories, real evidence in each.

### Hooks (real-world angles)
- Compare the learner's own region with a place a relative or friend lives abroad.
- A region from a favourite holiday, sport or wildlife show (the Alps, the Amazon, Florida).
- Photos side by side: "spot what's similar and what's different" between two regions.

### Misconceptions (wrong answer → why → fix)
- **Wrong idea:** "People in [faraway region] all live in [one stereotyped way]."
  **Why:** single images stand in for whole places.
  **Fix:** use **evidence** — every region has cities and countryside, variety of jobs and lives; compare fairly, not by stereotype.
- **Wrong idea:** "A region and a country are the same."
  **Why:** both are areas on a map.
  **Fix:** a **region** is a part *within* (or across) a country — a county, a national park, an area like the Amazon basin.
- **Wrong idea:** "Comparing means saying one place is better."
  **Why:** comparison feels like ranking.
  **Fix:** comparing means noting **similarities and differences** neutrally (climate, rivers, jobs), not deciding a winner.

### Checks (ready-to-use)
- **Q:** What is a *region*? **A:** A defined area we study in depth — e.g. a county, a national park, or part of a continent.
- **Q:** Name two *physical* and two *human* things you'd describe about a region. **A:** Physical: landscape, rivers, climate, biome. Human: people/settlements, jobs, land use.
- **Q:** Give one similarity and one difference you'd expect between a UK region and an Amazon region. **A:** Similarity — both have rivers and settlements; difference — UK is temperate, the Amazon is tropical rainforest.
- **Q:** Why should we use maps, photos and data when comparing regions? **A:** To stay accurate and fair, and avoid stereotyping whole places.

### Activity / practice
*Categorize + compare table.* Sort features into "physical" vs "human" geography for a chosen region; then fill a two-column compare table (UK region vs an Americas/European region) across climate, rivers, settlements and land use. *Speak*: describe your own region using both physical and human features.
