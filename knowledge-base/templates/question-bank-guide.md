# Question-bank guide — how to generate great questions (+ stems & sample banks)

How to write questions that *reveal thinking*, with copy-paste stems (Bloom, SOLO, Depth of Knowledge), reasoning/justification prompts, misconception-probe design, and a **sample question bank** of ~10 primary questions each for maths, English/reading, science and history/geography — every item with an answer and one likely wrong answer (the app's `wrongAnswers` seed).

Pair this with `assessment-and-feedback.md` (what makes a check *diagnostic*) and `templates/assessment-and-rubric-bank.md` (the structures to drop these into). The golden rule throughout: **a good question distinguishes understanding from guessing**, and its wrong answers are *informative*.

---

## 1. Bloom's taxonomy — question stems for all six levels

Climb from recall to creation. Lower levels build the knowledge; higher levels make it think. A good lesson uses the **lower levels in the check, the higher levels in the practice/recap** to push for transfer.

**1. Remember** (recall facts)
- "What is…?" · "Name the…" · "List the…" · "How many…?" · "When did…?" · "Label the…"

**2. Understand** (explain in own words)
- "What does ___ mean?" · "Put it in your own words." · "Why does ___ happen?" · "What's the main idea?" · "Give an example of…" · "What's the difference between ___ and ___?"

**3. Apply** (use it in a new case)
- "Solve…" · "Show how you'd…" · "What would you do if…?" · "Use ___ to work out…" · "Which method fits this one?" · "Where else could you use this?"

**4. Analyse** (break it down, find patterns)
- "What do these have in common?" · "Sort these into groups — by what rule?" · "What's the odd one out, and why?" · "What caused…?" · "How is ___ related to ___?" · "What evidence shows…?"

**5. Evaluate** (judge, decide, critique)
- "Which is better, and why?" · "Do you agree? Convince me." · "Is this a good ___? How do you know?" · "What's the strongest reason for…?" · "Spot the mistake here." · "Which answer is most reasonable?"

**6. Create** (make something new)
- "Make up your own ___ problem." · "Design a…" · "What if ___ were different — invent the rule." · "Write a new ending where…" · "How else could we solve this?" · "Combine these ideas to…"

## 2. SOLO taxonomy — stems by structural complexity

SOLO (Structure of Observed Learning Outcomes) tracks how *connected* the understanding is — useful for deciding whether a child has isolated facts or a joined-up idea.

- **Unistructural** (one idea): "Give one ___." · "Name a ___." · "Define ___."
- **Multistructural** (several ideas, unconnected): "List the ___." · "Give three reasons…" · "What are the steps?"
- **Relational** (ideas connected): "How does ___ affect ___?" · "Why does ___ lead to ___?" · "Compare ___ and ___." · "Explain the link between…"
- **Extended abstract** (generalise / transfer): "What general rule does this show?" · "Would this always be true?" · "Predict what would happen if…" · "How does this apply to a completely different situation?"

> Use SOLO to *raise the bar*: a child who can list (multistructural) isn't yet at "explains how they connect" (relational). Push one level up.

## 3. Depth of Knowledge (DOK) — four levels of cognitive demand

DOK (Webb) describes *how deeply* you must think, independent of difficulty. Most primary checks sit at DOK 1–2; reasoning and projects reach 3–4.

- **DOK 1 — Recall & reproduce:** state a fact, follow a known procedure. ("What is 6 × 7?" "Name the capital of France.")
- **DOK 2 — Skills & concepts:** decide *how* to solve, explain relationships, classify. ("Which operation do you need here, and why?" "Sort these animals and say your rule.")
- **DOK 3 — Strategic thinking & reasoning:** justify, use evidence, solve a non-routine problem. ("Convince me your answer is right." "Why do you think the Romans built straight roads?")
- **DOK 4 — Extended thinking:** investigate, design, connect across time/topics — usually a project. ("Plan an investigation to find out which paper towel is most absorbent." "Make a guide that…")

> DOK ≠ hard. "Multiply 4823 × 7" is hard but DOK 1 (one known procedure). "Is it cheaper to buy the 6-pack or singles?" is easy numbers but DOK 2–3 (choose & justify). Aim checks at the DOK level that matches the *intention*.

## 4. Reasoning & justification stems (the "make them think" set)

These turn a closed answer into evidence of understanding. Use them *after* a right answer too (catches guessing) — see `assessment-and-feedback.md` §5.

- **Surface the thinking:** "How do you know?" · "How did you work that out?" · "Talk me through it." · "Show me your thinking."
- **Demand justification:** "Convince me." · "Prove it." · "Why must it be that?" · "What's your evidence?"
- **Generalise:** "Would that *always* work?" · "Is it ever not true?" · "What's the rule here?"
- **Hypothetical / vary it:** "What if it were ___ instead?" · "What would change if…?" · "What if I told you ___ — does your answer still hold?"
- **Compare & critique:** "Why is this one wrong?" · "Which is the odd one out?" · "Sam says ___ — is Sam right?"
- **Connect:** "What does this remind you of?" · "Where have we seen this before?" · "How is this like ___?"
- **Predict & check:** "What do you think will happen? … Were you right? Why?"

## 5. Misconception-probing question design

The most valuable checks are built *around the wrong answer*. To design one:

1. **Start from the misconception, not the topic.** Open the subject's `misconceptions.md`, pick the wrong belief you want to surface.
2. **Build a question the misconception gets *wrong* but a guesser can't get right by luck.** Include a distractor that is the misconception's answer.
3. **Make every distractor a real mistake** with its own diagnosis (slip / gap / misconception) and remedy.
4. **Prefer items that force a choice between the right rule and the wrong rule** — e.g. a case where "more digits = bigger" gives the wrong answer.

**Design template:**
```
Misconception to surface: [the wrong belief]
Question (it must expose it): [ ]
Right answer: [ ]
Distractor = the misconception's answer: [ ] → why: [ ] → remedy (one move, no reveal): [ ]
```

**Worked example:**
```
Misconception: "to multiply by 10, add a zero"
Question: "What is 2.5 × 10?"
Right answer: 25
Distractor (misconception): "2.50" → why: applied 'add a zero' to a decimal → remedy: place-value chart — ×10 shifts every digit one column left; 2.5 → 25, the 'zero' trick was only ever the shift in disguise.
```

## 6. Sample question banks (primary, with answers + one likely wrong answer each)

~10 questions per subject. Each is written as the app's `BeatCheck` seed: **Q · answer · likely wrong answer (+why)**. Levels noted as (Bloom · DOK). All correct as written; British spelling.

### 6a. Maths

1. **(Remember · 1)** What is 7 × 8? — **56**. ✗ *54* (recited the wrong fact / confused with 6×9).
2. **(Apply · 1)** What is 36 + 7? — **43** (make ten: 36+4=40, then +3). ✗ *42* (lost one bridging the ten).
3. **(Understand · 2)** Which is bigger, ¾ or ½? — **¾** (3 of 4 parts > 2 of 4). ✗ *½* (thinks smaller numbers = bigger fraction).
4. **(Understand · 2)** Which is bigger, 0.45 or 0.6? — **0.6** (6 tenths > 4 tenths). ✗ *0.45* ("more digits = bigger").
5. **(Apply · 2)** A pencil costs 30p. How much for 4? — **£1.20** (30×4=120p). ✗ *£1.02* (wrote 120p as £1.02, place-value slip).
6. **(Apply · 2)** What is half of 18? — **9** (split into two equal groups). ✗ *8* (halved 16 / miscounted).
7. **(Analyse · 2)** Odd one out: 12, 15, 20, 18 — which isn't in the 3 times table, and why? — **20** (3×? ≠ 20; others are 3×4, 3×5, 3×6). ✗ *15* (picks by 'looks different', no rule).
8. **(Remember · 1)** How many sides does a hexagon have? — **6**. ✗ *5* (confused with pentagon).
9. **(Apply · 2)** A film starts at 2:45 and lasts 30 minutes. When does it end? — **3:15** (bridge the hour). ✗ *2:75* (added 30 to 45 without converting to the next hour).
10. **(Evaluate · 3)** Sam says "9 is a multiple of 3 and of 9, so it's even." Is Sam right? — **No** — 9 is odd (multiples of 3/9 can be odd or even; evenness depends on ÷2). ✗ *"yes"* (assumes multiples of an odd number are even, or conflates 'in a times table' with 'even').

### 6b. English / reading

1. **(Remember · 1)** What is a noun? — **a naming word** (person, place or thing). ✗ *"a doing word"* (confused noun with verb).
2. **(Apply · 1)** Add the right capital: "i went to london." — **"I went to London."** (capital I and proper noun). ✗ *"I went to london"* (capitalised the pronoun but not the place name).
3. **(Understand · 2)** What's the difference between *their*, *there* and *they're*? — **their** = belonging; **there** = a place; **they're** = they are. ✗ uses *there* for possession ("there house").
4. **(Apply · 2)** Make this plural: *one box → many ___*. — **boxes** (add -es after x). ✗ *"boxs"* (added -s, didn't apply the -es rule).
5. **(Understand · 2)** In "The exhausted runner finally stopped," which word is the adjective? — **exhausted** (describes the runner). ✗ *"finally"* (picked an adverb).
6. **(Analyse · 2)** Read: *"Tom's tummy rumbled as he stared at the empty fridge."* How is Tom probably feeling, and how do you know? — **hungry** — evidence: tummy rumbled + empty fridge. ✗ *"sad"* (a feeling, but ignores the textual clues — inference unsupported).
7. **(Understand · 2)** What does a question mark tell the reader? — **the sentence is a question** (asking something). ✗ *"to shout it"* (confused with exclamation mark).
8. **(Apply · 2)** Choose a better word than "nice" in "It was a nice cake." — e.g. **delicious / scrumptious**. ✗ *"good"* (swapped one vague word for another).
9. **(Analyse · 3)** Why might an author choose short, snappy sentences in a scary part of a story? — **to build tension / make it feel fast and urgent**. ✗ *"because they ran out of words"* (no grasp of author choice).
10. **(Evaluate · 3)** Which is the topic (main idea) sentence of this paragraph, and why? *[paragraph about why bees matter]* — **the sentence stating bees are important/why they matter** — it's what the rest gives details about. ✗ picks a *detail* sentence (one example) as the main idea.

### 6c. Science

1. **(Remember · 1)** Name the three states of matter. — **solid, liquid, gas**. ✗ omits gas / says "ice, water, steam" (names examples, not states).
2. **(Understand · 2)** Is a paper towel waterproof or absorbent? — **absorbent** (soaks water up). ✗ *"waterproof"* (judged by 'looks thin/dry').
3. **(Understand · 2)** Why do we need to eat? — **food gives us energy (and materials) to live, move and grow**. ✗ *"so we're not hungry"* (describes the feeling, not the function).
4. **(Apply · 2)** A plant is kept in a dark cupboard with water. What will happen, and why? — **it will grow weak/yellow and may die — it needs *light* to make its food**. ✗ *"it's fine, it has water"* (thinks water alone is enough — misses light).
5. **(Analyse · 2)** Sort these as living / non-living: dog, rock, oak tree, toy car. — **living: dog, oak tree; non-living: rock, toy car**. ✗ calls the toy car living (because it moves — 'moves = alive' misconception).
6. **(Understand · 2)** What is a habitat? — **the place where an animal or plant lives** (that meets its needs). ✗ *"the animal's home/nest"* (too narrow — a nest is part of, not the habitat).
7. **(Apply · 2)** Which would make a circuit's bulb light: a complete loop with a battery, or a loop with a gap? — **the complete loop** (a circuit must be unbroken). ✗ *"the one with a gap"* (no model of a closed circuit).
8. **(Understand · 2)** Why does the Moon seem to change shape during the month? — **we see different amounts of its sunlit side as it orbits Earth** (phases). ✗ *"Earth's shadow covers it"* (confuses phases with an eclipse).
9. **(Analyse · 3)** In a fair test of which ball bounces highest, name one thing you must keep the same. — e.g. **the drop height** (and the surface). ✗ *"change the ball and the height"* (changes two variables — not a fair test).
10. **(Evaluate · 3)** A shadow is shortest at midday. Why? — **the Sun is highest in the sky, so light comes from more directly above**. ✗ *"the Sun is closest at midday"* (distance misconception, not angle).

### 6d. History / geography

1. **(Remember · 1)** What is a continent? — **one of Earth's seven huge land masses** (e.g. Africa, Asia). ✗ *"a country"* (confuses continent with country).
2. **(Remember · 1)** Name the four points of a compass. — **North, East, South, West**. ✗ gives "up, down, left, right" (no compass concept).
3. **(Understand · 2)** What's the difference between a city and a country? — **a country is a large area with its own government; a city is a large town *within* a country**. ✗ "they're the same" (e.g. thinks London = England).
4. **(Understand · 2)** Why did people long ago build castles? — **for defence / protection from attackers**. ✗ *"because they liked big houses"* (misses the defensive purpose).
5. **(Apply · 2)** Put these in time order: Romans, Stone Age, Victorians. — **Stone Age → Romans → Victorians**. ✗ Romans first (thinks 'oldest = the one I learned first' / no timeline sense).
6. **(Understand · 2)** What does a key (legend) on a map do? — **explains what the symbols and colours mean**. ✗ *"shows where treasure is"* (literal/from stories).
7. **(Analyse · 2)** Why did early settlements often start near rivers? — **water to drink, for crops and animals, and for travel/trade**. ✗ *"rivers look nice"* (no functional reasoning).
8. **(Remember · 1)** Which ocean is the largest? — **the Pacific Ocean**. ✗ *"the Atlantic"* (common mix-up).
9. **(Analyse · 3)** How do we *know* about life in Ancient Egypt if no one alive saw it? — **from evidence: artefacts, tombs, writing (hieroglyphs), paintings**. ✗ *"someone wrote it in a book recently / we just guess"* (no concept of historical evidence).
10. **(Evaluate · 3)** Was the invention of the wheel important? Give one reason. — **yes — it made moving heavy loads and travelling far easier** (transport, trade). ✗ *"no, we have cars now"* (judges the past by the present — presentism).

## 7. Quick rules for generating a question bank

- **Anchor every item to a misconception or a key concept** from the subject file — don't ask trivia for its own sake.
- **Write the likely wrong answer first sometimes** — it forces the question to be diagnostic.
- **Spread the levels:** mostly Remember/Understand/Apply (Bloom 1–3, DOK 1–2) for checks; a few Analyse/Evaluate (DOK 3) for reasoning and recap.
- **Keep numbers and contexts age-appropriate and correct** — a wrong "right answer" is worse than no question.
- **Add a reasoning follow-up** to closed items ("how do you know?") so a right answer is real, not luck.
- **Reuse, don't reinvent:** these become `BeatCheck` objects — `question` / `expectedAnswer` / `wrongAnswers[{answer, why, remedy}]`. Pull the *why* and *remedy* from `assessment-and-feedback.md` §2–3.
