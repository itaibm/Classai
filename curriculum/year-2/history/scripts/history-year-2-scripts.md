# Year 2 History — Tutor Scripts (Lessons 1–14)

Complete, minute-by-minute scripts for [Year 2 History](../history-year-2.md), built to the [scripting guide](../../../SCRIPTING-GUIDE.md) and grounded in `knowledge-base/subjects/history-geography/history-ks1.md`. Every beat ⏱ sums to the lesson's duration. History lessons lead with **story + sources** ("how do we know?") and **cause/consequence** ("what changed, and why?"). Each lesson gives the tutor verbatim Say / Show / Do / Ask, a real check with wrong-answer fixes, the "if the child is stuck" protocol, what to emphasize, differentiation and assessment evidence.

**How to use:** read the "What to emphasize" box first, lay out the materials, and follow the timed beats. Personalise the local-history unit (Lessons 11–14) to the learner's own town.

## Lesson 1 — A fire over 350 years ago
**History · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Great Fire of London)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can place the Great Fire of London on a timeline as 'beyond living memory' and say where and when it started."

**You will need:** the long home timeline (extending well left of "grandparents"), a map of old London, a picture of Pudding Lane / a baker's oven, a marker/arrow for 1666.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** last year's close timeline (you → parents → grandparents); "a long time ago."
**Key vocabulary:** **beyond living memory** ("longer ago than anyone alive can remember"), **1666**, **Pudding Lane**, **timeline**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** 1666 is **beyond living memory** — it sits far left on the timeline, **after** castles and knights but **long before** cars. The story starts in a real place (a **baker's shop on Pudding Lane**) at a real time (**1666**).

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Imagine a whole city on fire — thousands of houses burning — and it all started with one baker who forgot to put his oven out properly. Could one tiny fire really burn down a city? Let's travel back in time and find out."
**Show:** the picture of the baker's oven / Pudding Lane.  **Do:** point dramatically to where the fire 'starts'.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Last year our timeline went you → parents → grandparents. Which way is *longer ago* — left or right?" → *listen for:* "left."

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch me stretch our timeline much further left than last year. Here's you, your parents, your grandparents… now keep going — past cars, past your great-great-grandparents — all the way to **1666**. That's **beyond living memory**: no one alive today can remember it. It's after knights and castles, but long before cars. The Great Fire started here, in a **baker's shop on Pudding Lane**, in **1666**."
**Show / Do:** extend and mark the timeline; pin 1666 between "castles/knights" and "cars."
**Emphasize:** "1666 is *beyond living memory* — far to the left, after castles, before cars."

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Help me place it. Where does 1666 go — nearer the knights, or nearer the cars? And where did the fire begin?"
**Do:** child places the 1666 arrow on the timeline and points to Pudding Lane on the map.
**Ask:** "Beyond living memory means…?" → *listen for:* "longer ago than anyone alive can remember."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Where and when did the Great Fire of London start?"
- ✅ **Expected:** in a baker's shop on Pudding Lane, in 1666 — probe: *"Is that within living memory or beyond it?"*
- ❌ **If "a long time ago" with no detail:** vague → **fix:** "Give me the *place* and the *year* — point to them: Pudding Lane, 1666."
- ❌ **If places 1666 after cars / in grandparents' time:** chronology slip → **fix:** "Could your grandparents remember 1666? No — so it goes far left, before cars."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child re-tells the opening in one sentence ("In 1666 a fire started in a baker's shop on Pudding Lane") and places 1666 correctly on a fresh timeline strip. Once secure, interleave: "Put 'castles', 'the Great Fire', and 'cars' in time order."
**Watch for:** dropping the year/place; muddling the time order.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — detail or chronology; 3) point along the timeline together; 4) one hint: "place first, then year"; 5) re-ask with the picture in view; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me where and when the Great Fire started, and whether it's beyond living memory." → *listen for:* "Pudding Lane, 1666, beyond living memory."
**Process praise:** "You placed 1666 perfectly — after castles, before cars. That's real chronology."
**Revisit later:** tomorrow we find out *why* the fire spread so fast.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** place the fire between "knights and castles" and "cars" on a pre-made line.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain that 1666 is "beyond living memory" because no one alive today can remember it.

**Extension (if time allows):** draw the baker's shop and label it "Pudding Lane, 1666."
**Assessment evidence:** child places 1666 correctly as beyond living memory and states the start place and year.

## Lesson 2 — Why did the fire spread so fast? (cause)
**History · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Great Fire of London)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can give two reasons (causes) the fire spread so quickly."

**You will need:** paper "houses" (a row you can stand close together), cause-cards (*wooden* / *close together* / *hot, dry, windy* / *no fire brigade*), a painting of the burning city, the timeline.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** where/when the fire started (Lesson 1).
**Key vocabulary:** **cause** ("the reason something happens"), **spread**, **wooden**, **close together**, **fire brigade**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** the fire spread fast for clear **causes** — not "just bad luck": houses were **wooden**, **close together**, the weather was **hot, dry and windy**, and there was **no fire brigade**.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Picture this: your house is made of **wood**, and it's squashed so close to your neighbour's that you could shake hands out of the windows. Now one house catches fire. What happens next?"
**Show:** the paper houses placed touching each other.  **Do:** mime a flame jumping from one to the next.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Where did the fire start, and in what year?" → *listen for:* "a baker's on Pudding Lane, 1666."

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **cause** is the reason something happens. The fire spread fast for four causes. One: houses were made of **wood** — wood burns easily. Two: houses were **close together** — flames jumped across. Three: the weather was **hot, dry and windy** — wind blew the fire along. Four: there was **no fire brigade** to put it out. Watch my wooden houses — see how the flame leaps street to street."
**Show / Do:** lay out the cause-cards; demonstrate flames jumping along the close wooden houses; show the painting.
**Emphasize:** "The fire spread for *reasons* — causes — not bad luck: wood, close, windy, no fire brigade."

### ⏱ 12–19 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's build the answer together. Sort these cards into our 'Why it spread' box and say each one aloud."
**Do:** child sorts the four cause-cards into the box, naming each cause.
**Ask:** "Which cause is about the *weather*?" → *listen for:* "hot, dry and windy."

### ⏱ 19–26 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Give two reasons the fire spread so fast."
- ✅ **Expected:** any two of — wooden houses; houses close together; hot/dry/windy weather; no fire engines — probe: *"Why did that one make it worse?"*
- ❌ **If "it was bad luck / God did it":** missing causation → **fix (KB):** "There were real reasons. Look at the houses — what were they made of? How close were they?"
- ❌ **If gives only one:** → **fix:** "That's one good cause. Find one more on the cards — what about the weather?"

### ⏱ 26–31 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child lists two causes in full sentences ("It spread because the houses were wooden and close together"). Once secure, interleave: rank the causes — "which mattered most, and why?"
**Watch for:** "bad luck" answers; listing effects instead of causes.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — luck vs cause; 3) point to the houses/weather card; 4) one hint: "What were the houses made of?"; 5) re-ask for just one cause; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me two causes the fire spread so fast." → *listen for:* two valid causes.
**Process praise:** "You explained the *reasons*, not 'bad luck' — that's how historians think about cause."
**Revisit later:** next we find out *how we know* all this — meet Samuel Pepys.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** sort cause-cards (wood / close / windy / no fire brigade) into a "why it spread" box.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain which single cause mattered most, and why.

**Extension (if time allows):** design a safer street that would stop the fire spreading, and label why.
**Assessment evidence:** child gives at least two valid causes of the fire's rapid spread.

---

## Lesson 3 — How do we know? Samuel Pepys's diary (sources)
**History · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Great Fire of London)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name a source that tells us about the fire and explain why a diary is useful."

**You will need:** a portrait of Samuel Pepys, a simplified diary line (e.g. about burying his cheese and wine), a "my diary" strip for the child, a picture of The Monument.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the causes of the fire (Lesson 2).
**Key vocabulary:** **source** ("something that tells us about the past"), **diary**, **eyewitness**, **written source**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** we know about the fire because of **sources**, especially **Samuel Pepys's diary** — written **at the time** by someone who was **there**, which makes it specially useful.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "During the fire, one man was so worried about losing his treasures that he buried his **cheese** and his **wine** in the garden! How on earth do we know that? Because he wrote it all down in his **diary** — and we still have it, 350 years later!"
**Show:** the portrait of Pepys.  **Do:** mime burying treasure in the garden.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Tell me one reason the fire spread fast." → *listen for:* a cause from last lesson.

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **source** is something that tells us about the past. **Samuel Pepys** kept a **diary** — he wrote down what he saw *as it happened*. That's a **written source**. Because he was an **eyewitness** — actually *there* — his diary is incredibly useful. Paintings made at the time and records also survive. Sources are how historians *know*."
**Show / Do:** read one short diary line aloud; hold up a painting and The Monument as other sources.
**Emphasize:** "We *know* from **sources** — and an eyewitness diary written *at the time* is gold."

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's sort: which of these tells us about the fire — Pepys's diary, a painting from 1666, The Monument? And which is a *written* source?"
**Do:** child identifies all three as sources and names the diary as the written one.
**Ask:** "Why is a diary written *during* the fire so useful?" → *listen for:* "the person was there / saw it / wrote it at the time."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "How do we know so much about the Great Fire?"
- ✅ **Expected:** Samuel Pepys wrote about it in his diary (a written source); paintings and records survive too — probe: *"Why does it help that he was *there*?"*
- ❌ **If "someone just told us / a book":** no source idea → **fix:** "Where did the book learn it? From sources made at the time — like Pepys's diary."
- ❌ **If can't say why a diary helps:** → **fix:** "He *saw* it and wrote it the same day — so it's an eyewitness account."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child writes their own one-line "diary" of today on the strip, then explains how a future historian could use it. Once secure, interleave: "Name two different sources for the fire."
**Watch for:** thinking we just "know"; confusing a source with a guess.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — no source concept; 3) hold up the diary/painting; 4) one hint: "What did Pepys leave behind?"; 5) re-ask 'name one source'; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Name one source that tells us about the fire, and why it's useful." → *listen for:* "Pepys's diary — he was there and wrote it at the time."
**Process praise:** "You wrote your own source today — now you know exactly how historians learn about the past."
**Revisit later:** tomorrow — what *changed* in London because of the fire?

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match "diary / painting / The Monument" to "things that tell us about the fire."
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain why an eyewitness who was *there* is so valuable.

**Extension (if time allows):** draw what Pepys might have buried and write a one-line diary entry for him.
**Assessment evidence:** child names a source (Pepys's diary) and explains why an eyewitness account is useful.

---

## Lesson 4 — What changed afterwards? (consequence)
**History · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Great Fire of London)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name one thing that changed in London because of the fire."

**You will need:** before/after pictures of London streets, a small wood block and a brick, an image of The Monument, a sequence strip (start → spread → 4 days → rebuilt).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** causes of the fire and Pepys's diary (Lessons 2–3).
**Key vocabulary:** **consequence** ("what happened *because* of an event"), **rebuild**, **brick**, **stone**, **wider streets**, **The Monument**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** events have **consequences** — because of the fire, London was **rebuilt in brick and stone** with **wider streets**, and fire-fighting improved. A sad event led to a useful, lasting **change**.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "After the fire, London made a brand-new rule so a fire like that could never happen again. Can you guess what they decided to build the new houses out of — instead of wood?"
**Show:** the wood block and the brick side by side.  **Do:** invite the child to feel both.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Why did the wooden houses burn so easily?" → *listen for:* "wood burns / they were close together."

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **consequence** is what happens *because of* an event. Let me sequence the whole story: the fire **starts** → wind **spreads** it → it burns for **four days** → and afterwards, London is **rebuilt in brick and stone**, with **wider streets**, and fire-fighting gets better. They even built **The Monument** to remember it. The fire was terrible, but it led to a safer city."
**Show / Do:** lay the sequence strip; show before (wood, narrow) / after (brick, wide) pictures; show The Monument.
**Emphasize:** "Because of the fire → London rebuilt in **brick**, **wider streets** — a lasting **consequence**."

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Wood or brick for the new houses — which is safer in a fire, and why? Let's decide the new rule together."
**Do:** child compares wood vs brick, chooses brick, and explains; places the 'rebuilt' card last on the sequence.
**Ask:** "Name one thing that changed in London because of the fire." → *listen for:* "rebuilt in brick / wider streets / a fire service."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name one thing that changed in London because of the fire."
- ✅ **Expected:** houses rebuilt in brick/stone; wider streets; a fire service developed — probe: *"Why did that change make London safer?"*
- ❌ **If names a cause instead (e.g. 'wooden houses'):** mixing cause/consequence → **fix:** "That's why it *spread*. I want what changed *after* — what were the new houses made of?"
- ❌ **If "nothing changed":** → **fix:** "Look at the after-picture — brick houses, wider streets. That's the change."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child completes "Because of the fire, London…" with a real consequence and explains why it helped. Once secure, interleave: order the four sequence cards start → rebuilt.
**Watch for:** cause/consequence mix-up; missing the "because" link.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — cause vs consequence; 3) point to before/after pictures; 4) one hint: "What changed *after* the fire?"; 5) re-ask with the brick in hand; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Finish this: 'Because of the Great Fire, London…'." → *listen for:* a valid consequence (brick, wider streets, fire service).
**Process praise:** "You saw how a terrible event led to a *useful change* — that's deep historical thinking about consequence."
**Revisit later:** Unit 1 check next; then we meet explorers across time.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** choose "brick" or "wood" for the safer rebuild and say why.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain how a sad event led to a useful change (a safer city).

**Extension (if time allows):** design the "new London" rule poster: build in brick, wider streets.
**Assessment evidence:** child names a consequence of the fire and links it to the event with "because."

**End-of-unit check (Unit 1):** Place 1666 on the timeline as beyond living memory; give two causes of the fire's fast spread; name Pepys's diary as a source; name one change that followed. **Misconception watch:** very few people are recorded as dying (houses burned, not mostly people); the fire had clear *causes*, not "just bad luck"; 1666 sits after castles but long before cars.

## Lesson 5 — What is an explorer? Columbus sails west (1492)
**History · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Explorers across time)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can say what an explorer does and tell the story of Columbus's voyage truthfully."

**You will need:** a globe, pictures of wooden sailing ships, a child-friendly Columbus fact card, the long timeline.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** beyond living memory; placing events on the timeline (Unit 1).
**Key vocabulary:** **explorer**, **voyage**, **the unknown**, **1492**, **Atlantic Ocean**, **the Americas**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** an **explorer** travels to find out about places people don't know well — and we tell the story **truthfully**: Columbus was the first to sail there *from Europe* in **1492**, but **millions of people already lived in the Americas**, and his arrival **brought them great harm**.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Imagine climbing onto a small wooden ship and sailing for *weeks* across an ocean so huge you can't see any land — and you don't know what's on the other side. Scary? Exciting? That's what explorers did."
**Show:** the globe and a picture of a wooden ship.  **Do:** sweep a finger across the wide Atlantic.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Is 1492 within living memory or beyond it?" → *listen for:* "beyond — way back, before cars."

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "An **explorer** is someone who travels to find out about places people don't know well. In **1492**, Christopher Columbus sailed **three wooden ships** west across the **Atlantic Ocean**. Now — the *truthful* story: he was the first to sail there **from Europe**, but he did **not** find an empty land. **Millions of people already lived** in the Americas for thousands of years, and sadly his arrival **brought them great harm**. Both parts are true, and good historians tell both."
**Show / Do:** trace Columbus's route on the globe; show the ships; state the honest two-part story.
**Emphasize:** "First *from Europe* — but the land was **not empty**, and the arrival caused harm. Tell the whole truth."

### ⏱ 12–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Sail the ship with me across the globe — name the ocean. Now help me say the truthful version: was the land empty?"
**Do:** child traces the Atlantic route and states that people already lived there.
**Ask:** "Did Columbus discover an empty land?" → *listen for:* "no — millions already lived there."

### ⏱ 20–27 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Did Columbus discover an empty land? Explain."
- ✅ **Expected:** no — millions of people already lived in the Americas; he was first to sail there *from Europe*, and his arrival brought harm — probe: *"So why isn't 'he discovered America' the whole truth?"*
- ❌ **If "yes, he discovered it":** the empty-land myth → **fix (KB):** "People had lived there for thousands of years. He was first *from Europe* — that's different from 'discovered.'"
- ❌ **If only the harm or only the voyage:** half the story → **fix:** "Tell me *both* parts — the brave voyage AND the people already there."

### ⏱ 27–32 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child tells the truthful Columbus story in 2–3 sentences (voyage + people already there) and finds the Atlantic on the globe. Once secure, interleave: "Why do historians tell both parts?"
**Watch for:** the empty-land myth; "discovered" used without qualification.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — myth vs truth; 3) point to the Americas on the globe ("people lived all here"); 4) one hint: "first from where?"; 5) re-ask 'was it empty?'; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 32–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What does an explorer do, and what's the truthful story of Columbus?" → *listen for:* "travels to unknown places; first from Europe in 1492, but the land wasn't empty and it caused harm."
**Process praise:** "You told the *whole* truth — both the brave voyage and the people already there. That's honest history."
**Revisit later:** next, a very different explorer — who left the Earth!

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** trace Columbus's route with a finger on the globe and name the ocean.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain why "he discovered America" is not the whole truth.

**Extension (if time allows):** draw the three ships crossing the Atlantic and label the ocean.
**Assessment evidence:** child defines explorer and tells the Columbus story truthfully (voyage + people already there).

## Lesson 6 — Armstrong walks on the Moon (1969)
**History · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Explorers across time)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can tell the story of Neil Armstrong and place it in my grandparents' time."

**You will need:** an image of the Moon landing, a model rocket / spacesuit picture, the timeline.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** what an explorer is; Columbus's 1492 voyage (Lesson 5).
**Key vocabulary:** **1969**, **the Moon**, **rocket** (Apollo 11), **astronaut**, **spacesuit**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** another kind of explorer — Neil Armstrong left **Earth** and walked on the **Moon** in **1969**, in **grandparents'/great-grandparents' time** (near the recent, right-hand end of the timeline). Exploring **tools** changed: sail and compass → rocket and spacesuit.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Columbus crossed an ocean. But one explorer didn't cross any sea at all — he left the **whole Earth**! Where did Neil Armstrong put his footprints? Up there." *(point up)*
**Show:** the Moon-landing image.  **Do:** mime a slow Moon bounce.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does an explorer do?" → *listen for:* "travels to places people don't know well."

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "In **1969**, **Neil Armstrong** flew in a **rocket** (Apollo 11) all the way to the **Moon** and became the first person to walk on it. He said, 'one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.' This wasn't long ago like Columbus — it's near the *recent* end of our timeline, in your **grandparents' or great-grandparents'** lifetime. And look how the *tools* changed: Columbus had a sail and compass; Armstrong had a rocket and spacesuit."
**Show / Do:** place 1969 near the right end of the timeline; compare ship vs rocket pictures.
**Emphasize:** "1969, the **Moon**, a **rocket** — recent enough for grandparents to remember."

### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Place Armstrong on the timeline with me — nearer the cars or nearer the castles? And what did he travel in?"
**Do:** child places 1969 near the recent end and names the rocket/Moon.
**Ask:** "Which explorer walked on the Moon, and roughly when?" → *listen for:* "Neil Armstrong, 1969, grandparents' time."

### ⏱ 17–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which explorer walked on the Moon, and roughly when?"
- ✅ **Expected:** Neil Armstrong, in 1969 (grandparents' lifetime) — probe: *"How did he get there?"* (a rocket).
- ❌ **If places 1969 far left near Columbus:** chronology slip → **fix:** "Could your grandparents remember the Moon landing? Yes — so it's *recent*, near the cars end."
- ❌ **If muddles with Columbus:** → **fix:** "Columbus = ocean, ships, 1492. Armstrong = Moon, rocket, 1969."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child tells Armstrong's story in two sentences and places 1969 correctly. Once secure, interleave: "How are Armstrong's *tools* different from Columbus's?"
**Watch for:** placing 1969 too far back; tool confusion.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — chronology or facts; 3) point along the timeline; 4) one hint: "grandparents could remember this one"; 5) re-ask 'rocket or ship?'; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me who walked on the Moon, when, and what he travelled in." → *listen for:* "Armstrong, 1969, a rocket."
**Process praise:** "You placed 1969 in grandparents' time and spotted the rocket — careful with both *who* and *when*."
**Revisit later:** next we compare the two explorers across ~500 years.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match "rocket / the Moon / 1969" to Armstrong.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain why the Moon footprints are still there (no wind to blow them away).

**Extension (if time allows):** practise the "one small step" line and draw a footprint on the Moon.
**Assessment evidence:** child tells Armstrong's story and places 1969 in living/grandparents' memory.

## Lesson 7 — Same and different: comparing two explorers
**History · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Explorers across time)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name one thing that was the same and one thing that was different about Columbus and Armstrong."

**You will need:** fact/picture cards (wooden ship, rocket, 1492, 1969, ocean, Moon, "brave explorer"), a Columbus / Armstrong / Both sorting mat, the timeline.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the Columbus (Lesson 5) and Armstrong (Lesson 6) stories.
**Key vocabulary:** **same**, **different**, **compare**, **both**, **~500 years apart**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** comparing — some facts belong to one explorer, some to **both**. Same: both **brave**, both went somewhere **unknown**, both **changed what people understood**. Different: **ships vs rocket**, **~500 years apart**.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Two brave explorers — but they lived almost **500 years apart**. One sailed wooden ships, one flew a rocket. What could they possibly have in *common*? Let's find out."
**Show:** the sorting mat and the fact cards.  **Do:** place the timeline so the ~500-year gap is visible.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Quick — Columbus's year? Armstrong's year?" → *listen for:* "1492 and 1969."

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "To **compare**, I sort facts into Columbus, Armstrong, or **Both**. 'Wooden ships, 1492' → Columbus. 'Rocket, 1969, the Moon' → Armstrong. But 'brave', 'travelled somewhere unknown', 'changed what people understood' → those go in **Both**! Watch me place a few."
**Show / Do:** model sorting three cards, thinking aloud, including one into 'Both'; place both explorers on the timeline.
**Emphasize:** "Some facts are one explorer's; the big ideas — brave, unknown, changed understanding — belong to **both**."

### ⏱ 11–18 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn to place a card. Where does 'brave' go — and why?"
**Do:** child sorts cards across Columbus / Armstrong / Both, justifying the 'Both' ones.
**Ask:** "Name one thing the same and one thing different about the two explorers." → *listen for:* same: brave/explored the unknown; different: ships vs rocket / ~500 years apart.

### ⏱ 18–24 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name one thing the same and one thing different about Columbus and Armstrong."
- ✅ **Expected:** same — both brave / both explored the unknown / both changed understanding; different — ships vs rocket, ~500 years apart — probe: *"Why does 'brave' go in Both?"*

**Emphasize:** "Say *what it is*, *how we know it's old*, and *one thing it tells us* — use the frame."

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's start yours together. What's your source, and how do we know it's old?"
**Do:** child names their source and gives one 'how we know it's old' clue.
**Ask:** "Tell me one thing your chosen source tells us about the past." → *listen for:* a source-based fact (e.g. "Mill Lane tells us there was once a mill here").

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Tell me one thing your source tells us about the past — and how you know."
- ✅ **Expected:** a source-based fact + evidence (date/photo/name) — probe: *"Which clue proves it?"*
- ❌ **If gives an opinion, not evidence:** → **fix:** "What's your *clue*? A date? An old photo? The street name? Start with that."
- ❌ **If retells everything:** → **fix:** "Just *one* clear thing it tells us — use the frame."

### ⏱ 22–32 min · PRACTICE (you do — present)
**Do:** Child prepares and delivers their mini-presentation using the sentence frame, then answers one question from the 'audience' (tutor). Once secure, interleave: add *how we know* (which source proves it) and one *cause* of the change.
**Watch for:** opinion without evidence; not using the frame.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — evidence or structure; 3) fill the frame together, one blank at a time; 4) one hint: "Start: '___ is old because…'"; 5) re-ask just the first blank; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 32–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "In one sentence — what does your source tell us about the past?" → *listen for:* a confident, evidence-backed statement.
**Process praise:** "You presented a real clue from your own town and *proved* it with evidence — a true town historian!"
**Revisit later:** these source-and-evidence habits lead straight into Year 3's chronological KS2 history.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** complete the sentence frame with picture prompts.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add *how we know* (which source proves it) and one *cause* of the change.

**Extension (if time allows):** make a tiny "town guide" leaflet for the chosen source.
**Assessment evidence:** child presents one local source with evidence and one fact about the past.

**End-of-unit check (Unit 4):** Name three local sources; compare an old and new photo (one change, one same); suggest one cause of local change; present one source with a fact about the past. **Misconception watch:** history is local too (not only London/faraway); old buildings haven't "always looked like that" — sources show the change.

## Bridge to Year 3 (Key Stage 2)

KS2 history begins next year with a disciplined, roughly **chronological journey** using **BC/AD** and a long timeline: the deep past (**Stone Age → Iron Age Britain**, then **Ancient Egypt** and **Sumer & the Indus Valley**), learning through archaeology and sources, keeping every period in order. The Year 2 habits scripted here — **"how do we know?" (sources)** and **"what changed, and why?" (cause and consequence)** — are exactly what those lessons build on.

## End-of-year mastery checks (History)

A Year 2 child who has mastered this year can:
1. **(Beyond living memory)** Place the Great Fire (1666) as beyond living memory, give two causes of its spread, and name one change that followed.
2. **(Sources)** Name Pepys's diary as a source and explain why an eyewitness diary is useful.
3. **(Explorers)** Tell the Columbus (1492) and Armstrong (1969) stories truthfully, give one similarity and one difference, and place them ~500 years apart.
4. **(Significance)** Explain what Nightingale changed, tell Seacole's story, and define *significant* with an example of each.
5. **(Local history)** Name three local sources, compare an old and new picture of one place, and suggest one cause of how their town changed.
6. **(Presenting)** Present one local source and explain one thing it tells us about the past.
