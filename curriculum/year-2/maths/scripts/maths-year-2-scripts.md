## How to use these scripts

Each script lets a tutor (or confident parent) pick the lesson up cold and deliver it well.
Read the **header** and **What to emphasize** first, lay out the **You will need** materials, then
teach the **timed beats** in order. The `> **Say:**` blocks are real words to say aloud — warm and
pitched for 6–7 year-olds; adapt the child's name. Every **CHECK** lists the expected answer plus
the two most common wrong answers with a *fix you guide toward* (never reveal the answer — diagnose
and step down). The **"If the child is stuck"** protocol in Practice is mandatory. Keep the
**Concrete → Pictorial → Abstract (CPA)** spine: build it, draw it, then write it.

**28 lessons, 8 units.** Beats in every script sum to the lesson's stated duration.

## Lesson 1 — Tens and ones to 100
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Place value to 100)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can build any number to 100 and say how many tens and ones it has."

**You will need:** base-10 ten-rods and ones-cubes (enough for numbers to 100), a place-value mat with a Tens column and a Ones column, a mini-whiteboard and pen.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 1 tens and ones; counting to 100.
**Key vocabulary:** **tens** (rods of ten), **ones** (single cubes), **digit** (a number symbol 0–9), **place** (where a digit sits tells its value), **two-digit number**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A digit's **place** decides its **value** — the 6 in 65 means *six tens (sixty)*, not six. Repeat: "Look at the tens first." Pre-empt the misconception that 34 and 43 are "the same" because they share digits — the *position* changes everything.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "I've written two numbers: 34 and 43. They use exactly the same two digits — a 3 and a 4. So… are they the same number? Hmm. Which would you rather have, 34 sweets or 43 sweets?"
**Show:** "34" and "43" written large on the whiteboard.  **Do:** Let the child react; don't resolve it yet.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "In a number like 34, which digit tells the tens?" → *listen for:* "the 3" / "the first one."
*(If shaky: hand the child one ten-rod and one cube — "this rod is a ten, this cube is a one." Count a rod in tens: 10, 20…)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch me build 65. Six tens first." *(lay 6 ten-rods in the Tens column, counting)* "10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60. Now five ones." *(place 5 cubes in the Ones column)* "61, 62, 63, 64, 65. So 65 is **6 tens and 5 ones**. The 6 isn't just six — it's six *tens*, sixty."
**Show / Do:** Build 65 on the place-value mat, tens on the left, ones on the right.
**Emphasize:** "The place tells the value. Six in the tens place means sixty."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's build 24 together. You put down the tens — how many?"
**Do:** Child lays 2 ten-rods; tutor prompts the count (10, 20); child adds 4 cubes.
**Ask:** "How many tens and how many ones in 24?" → *listen for:* "2 tens and 4 ones."

### ⏱ 12–15 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Build 47 on your mat. How many tens and ones?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 4 tens and 7 ones — then probe: *"How do you know it's 4 *tens*, not just 4?"*
- ❌ **If "7 tens and 4 ones":** read the digits in the wrong columns → **fix:** "Point to the tens column. Which digit lives there? Count those rods in tens."
- ❌ **If "4 and 7" (no value):** reading digits, not place → **fix:** "Count just the rods: 10, 20, 30, 40 — so the 4 is worth…?"

### ⏱ 15–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child builds and names 32, 58, then 70 (a tricky "7 tens and **0** ones"). For each, they say "__ tens and __ ones." Once secure, interleave: "Build a number with 5 tens and 3 ones — what is it?" (53).
**Watch for:** child putting ones in the tens column; child saying "five-three" for 53 instead of "fifty-three."
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "I think the columns got mixed up"; 3) drop to fully concrete — clear the mat, hand only ten-rods first; 4) one hint: "Tens go on the left, like reading"; 5) re-ask a smaller number (e.g. 23); 6) keep it safe — "Mistakes show us what to practise. Let's take a smaller step."

### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me your own rule: why are 34 and 43 different?" → *listen for:* "the place changes the value" / "43 has 4 tens."
**Process praise:** "You built every number before you named it — that's exactly how mathematicians check themselves."
**Revisit later:** Quick "how many tens and ones?" starter at the next 2–3 lessons; bring 70 back to keep the zero-ones idea alive.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** keep the labelled place-value mat; sort rods and cubes into the right column before counting; start with "teen" numbers (15, 18) so there's only one ten.
- **Stretch (challenge):** "What is 7 tens and 0 ones?" → 70, said without equipment; "Make the biggest two-digit number you can with a 9 and a 2." → 92, "why?"

**Extension (if time allows):** "Place Value Basketball" — call a number, the child 'shoots' each digit into the right column on the mat as fast as they can, saying its value.
**Assessment evidence:** Child independently builds 2–3 given numbers and states tens/ones correctly, including a number ending in 0.

## Lesson 2 — Partitioning numbers
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Place value to 100)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can partition a two-digit number into tens and ones and put it back together."

**You will need:** base-10 ten-rods and ones-cubes, place-value mat, mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** tens and ones (Lesson 1); a digit's place gives its value.
**Key vocabulary:** **partition** (split a number into parts), **recombine** (put the parts back together), **tens**, **ones**, **part-whole**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Partition by **value**, not by digit: 50 splits into **50 and 0**, not "5 and 0". The whole equals its parts — nothing is lost when we split and rebuild. Repeat: "The 5 is 5 *tens*, which is fifty."

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's a game. I'm going to *smash* a number into pieces, then build it back perfectly — like a Lego model. If I do it right, no piece goes missing. Watch the number 43… then you'll try."
**Show:** "43" on the board.  **Do:** Build 43 with rods and cubes on the mat.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "How many tens and ones in 43?" → *listen for:* "4 tens and 3 ones."
*(If shaky: rebuild from Lesson 1 — count the rods in tens out loud.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Smash! I pull the tens away from the ones." *(physically slide the 4 rods apart from the 3 cubes)* "The tens are worth 40. The ones are worth 3. So 43 partitions into **40 and 3**. Now I recombine — push them back — 40 and 3 make 43 again. Nothing lost."
**Show / Do:** Slide apart, write "43 = 40 + 3," then push back together.
**Emphasize:** "Forty *and* three — I say the value, not 'four and three'."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn to smash 76. Build it first… now pull the tens from the ones."
**Do:** Child builds 76, separates 7 rods and 6 cubes; tutor writes "76 = ___ + ___" for the child to fill.
**Ask:** "What two parts?" → *listen for:* "70 and 6."

### ⏱ 13–16 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Partition 50." *(the trap: only one part has value)*
- ✅ **Expected:** 50 + 0 (or "50 and nothing in the ones") — probe: *"Why isn't it 5?"*
- ❌ **If "5 and 0":** reading digits, not place value → **fix:** "Build it. How many *rods*? Count them in tens — so the 5 is worth…?"
- ❌ **If "25 and 25" or random:** guessing parts → **fix:** "Partition means pull the *tens* away from the *ones*. Show me the tens."

### ⏱ 16–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child partitions 38, 64, then 90 (another zero-ones case), writing each as "__ + __" and rebuilding to check. Once secure, interleave a **non-standard** partition for stretch: "Can you make 43 a different way — 30 and 13?" (build 3 rods + 13 cubes).
**Watch for:** writing "3 + 8" for 38; forgetting to rebuild and check.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "the tens value slipped"; 3) go fully concrete — separate the rods physically before writing; 4) one hint: "Count only the rods, in tens"; 5) re-ask with a teen number (16 = 10 + 6); 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — let's take a smaller step."

### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the rule for partitioning, and why 50 is 50 and 0." → *listen for:* "split into tens and ones by their value."
**Process praise:** "You rebuilt every number to check — that's a real mathematician's habit."
**Revisit later:** Partitioning starter before Lesson 6 (it powers column addition); bring back 90 to keep the zero idea.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** always build then physically separate the rods before writing the number sentence.
- **Stretch (challenge):** non-standard partitions (43 = 30 + 13; 76 = 60 + 16) and "why does that still equal 43?"

**Extension (if time allows):** "Smash and rebuild" race — partition five numbers, rebuild each, beat your own time.
**Assessment evidence:** Child partitions 2–3 numbers correctly into tens + ones (including a "tens and 0" case) and recombines them.

## Lesson 3 — Comparing two-digit numbers
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Place value to 100)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare two two-digit numbers by looking at the tens first."

**You will need:** base-10 rods and cubes, place-value mats (two side by side), large **>** and **<** cards, mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** tens and ones; partitioning (a digit's place gives its value).
**Key vocabulary:** **compare**, **greater than (>)**, **less than (<)**, **tens first**, **digit**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **Compare the tens first.** Only when the tens are equal do you look at the ones. Pre-empt the big misconception "19 > 21 because 9 > 1" — line the tens-towers side by side so the taller tower wins. The open mouth of **>** / **<** "eats the bigger number."

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Two houses on a street: number 38 and number 41. The postman walks from number 1 upwards. Which house does he reach first — which number is smaller?"
**Show:** "38" and "41" on the board.  **Do:** Let the child predict.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "How many tens in 38? How many in 41?" → *listen for:* "3 tens" and "4 tens."
*(If shaky: build both numbers and count the rods in tens.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "I'll build both and put their tens side by side." *(build 38 and 41 on two mats; line up the rod-towers)* "41 has 4 tens — a taller tower. 38 has only 3 tens. More tens means a bigger number, so **41 is greater than 38**, even though 38 has more ones. I look at the **tens first**." *(write 41 > 38)* "The hungry mouth opens to eat the bigger number."
**Show / Do:** Two towers compared; write "41 > 38" with the > card.
**Emphasize:** "Tens first. The taller tens-tower wins."

### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Now the tricky one — 47 and 43. Build both. Compare the tens."
**Do:** Child builds both; sees the tens are equal (4 each).
**Ask:** "Tens are the same — so what do we check now?" → *listen for:* "the ones — 7 beats 3, so 47 is bigger."

### ⏱ 14–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which is bigger, 19 or 21?" *(the classic trap)*
- ✅ **Expected:** 21 (2 tens beats 1 ten) — probe: *"How did you decide?"*
- ❌ **If "19, because 9 > 1":** compared the ones, ignored tens → **fix:** "Build both. Whose tens-tower is taller — 1 ten or 2 tens?"
- ❌ **If guesses without checking tens:** no strategy → **fix:** "Where do we always look first?" (tens) — point to the tens columns.

### ⏱ 17–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child compares and writes with > or <: 27 vs 72, 64 vs 61 (equal tens), 50 vs 49 (across a ten). Once secure, interleave: "Make a number bigger than 55 — how do you know it's bigger?"
**Watch for:** the > / < mouth pointing the wrong way; ignoring tens when ones look bigger.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "the ones distracted us"; 3) go concrete — build both, compare tens-towers physically; 4) one hint: "Tens first — count the rods"; 5) re-ask with very different tens (30 vs 80); 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 22–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Say the golden rule for comparing two numbers." → *listen for:* "look at the tens first; if equal, look at the ones."
**Process praise:** "You stopped yourself from being tricked by the ones and checked the tens — brilliant control."
**Revisit later:** Compare-two starter before Lesson 4 (ordering builds on it).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** line the rod-towers physically side by side so the taller tens-stack is obvious (KB fix for "19 > 21").
- **Stretch (challenge):** use the > and < symbols fluently; "Is 60 > 59? By how much?"; compare three numbers.

**Extension (if time allows):** "Rod duel" — build two secret numbers, reveal, the bigger tens-tower wins the round.
**Assessment evidence:** Child compares 2–3 pairs correctly using tens-first reasoning, including a pair with equal tens, and records with > or <.

---

## Lesson 4 — Ordering numbers to 100
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Place value to 100)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can put a set of two-digit numbers in order, smallest first."

**You will need:** numeral cards (e.g. 25, 50, 52, plus extras), a 0–100 number track, base-10 rods/cubes for backup, mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** comparing two numbers tens-first (Lesson 3).
**Key vocabulary:** **order**, **smallest**, **largest**, **before / after**, **number track**, **compare**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Ordering is **comparing, repeated** — find the smallest by comparing tens first, set it down, repeat. A number track lets you *see* which comes first. Watch numbers with equal tens (50 vs 52 → compare ones).

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Oh no — someone dropped the book and the page numbers got muddled: 52, 25, 50. The pages must go in order, smallest first, or the story won't make sense. Can you be the book-fixer?"
**Show:** Three numeral cards: 52, 25, 50, jumbled.  **Do:** Spread them out.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "To compare two numbers, where do we look first?" → *listen for:* "the tens."
*(If shaky: quick "which is bigger, 30 or 50?" with rods.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "I find the **smallest** first. Compare the tens: 52 has 5 tens, 25 has 2 tens, 50 has 5 tens. The smallest tens is 2 — so **25** comes first." *(place 25 on the track)* "Now 52 and 50 are left — same tens (5). Look at the ones: 0 beats 2, so **50** is next, then **52**." *(place them on the track in order)*
**Show / Do:** Lay the three cards along the 0–100 track: 25, 50, 52.
**Emphasize:** "Find the smallest, set it down, repeat. Tens first; if tied, ones."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together: order 41, 14, 40. Which has the fewest tens?"
**Do:** Child compares tens, picks 14 first, then sorts 40 and 41 by ones.
**Ask:** "What's the order, smallest first?" → *listen for:* "14, 40, 41."

### ⏱ 13–16 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Order smallest first: 52, 25, 50."
- ✅ **Expected:** 25, 50, 52 — probe: *"How did you decide 50 comes before 52?"*
- ❌ **If "25, 52, 50":** compared ones before tens, or mis-ordered the equal-tens pair → **fix:** "50 and 52 have the same tens — so compare the ones. Which is smaller, 0 or 2?"
- ❌ **If orders by first digit only (e.g. 25, 50, 52 'because 2,5,5')** but can't justify → **fix:** put them on the track; "which sits furthest left?"

### ⏱ 16–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child orders 33, 13, 31 then a four-card set with equal tens: 62, 26, 60, 66 (smallest first). Use the track to check. Once secure, interleave: "Now order them **largest** first."
**Watch for:** stopping after one comparison; mishandling equal tens.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "we forgot to compare again"; 3) drop to the track — place each card where it belongs; 4) one hint: "Find just the smallest first"; 5) re-ask with only two cards; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Teach me how to order numbers smallest first." → *listen for:* "compare tens, pick smallest, repeat; use ones if tens are equal."
**Process praise:** "You used the number track to *check* yourself instead of guessing — that's careful maths."
**Revisit later:** Mixed ordering starter in the end-of-unit check and before Unit 2.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** place every number on a 0–100 track to *see* which comes first; build any tricky pair with rods.
- **Stretch (challenge):** order four+ numbers including several with equal tens; order largest-first and smallest-first for the same set.

**Extension (if time allows):** "Number line on the floor" — child slots each new card into the right place among cards already laid along the floor track.
**Assessment evidence:** Child orders a set of 3–4 two-digit numbers correctly, justifying with tens-first reasoning and handling at least one equal-tens pair.

> **End-of-Unit 1 check:** (1) 38 = __ tens and __ ones → 3 and 8. (2) Partition 76 → 70 + 6. (3) Which is bigger, 27 or 72? → 72 (7 tens). (4) Order 52, 25, 50 → 25, 50, 52.

## Lesson 5 — Counting in 2s, 5s and 10s
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Calculation)** | **Duration:** 18 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can skip-count in 2s, 5s and 10s and spot the pattern in the last digits."

**You will need:** a tub of cubes, several 5p and 10p coins, a 0–30 number line, mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** counting to 100; what a "ten" is.
**Key vocabulary:** **skip-count** (count in equal jumps), **step** (the size of each jump), **equal groups**, **even**, **pattern**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Skip-counting means jumps of the **same size** every time. Watch the last-digit pattern: **5s end in 0 or 5**, **10s end in 0**, **2s are even**. This is the seed of multiplication — "lots of" equal groups.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "I've got a big pile of socks to count. One… two… three… that's *slow*. But socks come in pairs! Watch: 2, 4, 6, 8 — done! Counting in 2s is like having super-speed. Let's find that speed for 2s, 5s and 10s."
**Show:** A handful of cubes paired up.  **Do:** Count a few one-by-one, then in 2s to show the speed-up.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If I count in tens, what comes after 30?" → *listen for:* "40."
*(If shaky: count rods in tens together: 10, 20, 30, 40.)*

### ⏱ 3–7 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "The rule for skip-counting: every jump is the **same size**. In 5s I jump 5 each time." *(make jumps on the number line)* "5, 10, 15, 20, 25 — say it with me. Look at the last digits: 5, 0, 5, 0 — only ever 0 or 5! In 10s: 10, 20, 30 — always end in 0. In 2s: 2, 4, 6, 8 — all even."
**Show / Do:** Draw equal jumps of 5 on the number line; circle the last digits.
**Emphasize:** "Same-size jumps — and 5s always end in 0 or 5."

### ⏱ 7–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Count these 5p coins with me — each coin is worth 5." *(lay out five 5p coins)*
**Do:** Child touches each coin and counts: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25.
**Ask:** "How much is that altogether?" → *listen for:* "25p" (that's five 5s).

### ⏱ 11–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Counting in 5s, could you ever say 23?"
- ✅ **Expected:** No — 5s end in 0 or 5, and 23 ends in 3 — probe: *"What number near 23 would you say instead?"* (20 or 25)
- ❌ **If "yes":** hasn't spotted the 0/5 pattern → **fix:** "List 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 — circle the last digit of each. Do you ever see a 3?"
- ❌ **If counts unevenly (5, 10, 14, 20):** lost the constant step → **fix:** "Make equal groups of 5 cubes; say 'plus 5' each jump."

### ⏱ 14–17 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child fills gaps: "10, 20, __, 40" / "2, 4, __, 8" / "5, __, 15, __". Then spots the odd one out: "5, 10, 15, 17, 25 — which breaks the rule?" Once secure, interleave 3s for stretch (3, 6, 9, 12).
**Watch for:** uneven jumps; restarting from 1 each time.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "the jumps got different sizes"; 3) go concrete — make physical groups of the step size; 4) one hint: "Say the step out loud — 'plus 5'"; 5) re-ask a shorter sequence; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 17–18 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me one pattern you spotted today." → *listen for:* "5s end in 0 or 5" / "10s end in 0" / "2s are even."
**Process praise:** "You used the pattern to *check* your counting — that's how you catch your own slips."
**Revisit later:** Daily skip-count chant (2s, 5s, 10s) before every Unit 2 lesson — it feeds the times tables.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** physically make equal groups, saying the step each jump; touch each coin/cube as you count.
- **Stretch (challenge):** skip-count in 3s to 30; count *backwards* in 10s from 50.

**Extension (if time allows):** "Hit the Button"-style skip-count race — how far can you get in 2s/5s/10s before the timer?
**Assessment evidence:** Child completes skip-count sequences in 2s, 5s and 10s and states one last-digit pattern.

## Lesson 6 — Adding two-digit numbers (with regrouping)
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Calculation)** | **Duration:** 28 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can add two-digit numbers, exchanging ten ones for a ten when needed."

**You will need:** base-10 ten-rods and ones-cubes (plenty of spare rods), a place-value mat marked Tens | Ones, mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** partitioning into tens and ones (Lesson 2); bonds within 20.
**Key vocabulary:** **add / total**, **regroup / exchange** (swap ten ones for one ten), **carry**, **tens**, **ones**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** When the ones make **ten or more**, you must **exchange** ten ones for one ten — you can't have 13 in the ones column. Pre-empt the "713" error (writing 7 tens and 13 ones). Repeat: "Ten ones become one ten — trade them in."

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "In the shop, a toy car is 36p and a sticker pack is 27p. I want both. How much do I need altogether? Let's find out with our blocks — and watch for a clever swap we'll have to make."
**Show:** "36 + 27" on the board.  **Do:** Set out the place-value mat.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Partition 36 — tens and ones?" → *listen for:* "30 and 6."
*(If shaky: rebuild 36 with rods; count the tens.)*

### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Build 36 — 3 tens, 6 ones. Build 27 below it — 2 tens, 7 ones. **Add the ones first:** 6 and 7 is 13 ones. But I can't keep 13 ones in the ones column! So I **exchange** — ten ones become one shiny ten-rod, and 3 ones stay." *(physically swap 10 cubes for 1 rod, move it to the tens)* "Now the tens: 30 + 20 + my new 10 = 60. Plus 3 ones = **63**."
**Show / Do:** The exchange acted out on the mat; write "36 + 27 = 50 + 13 = 63."
**Emphasize:** "Ten ones become one ten — trade them in."

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together: 27 + 46. Build both. Add the ones — what do they make?"
**Do:** Child builds, adds ones (7 + 6 = 13), exchanges ten ones for a ten, then totals the tens.
**Ask:** "After the swap, how many tens, how many ones?" → *listen for:* "7 tens and 3 ones — 73."

### ⏱ 16–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "45 + 38 = ?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 83 — probe: *"Did you have to exchange? Show me the swap."*
- ❌ **If "713":** wrote 7 tens and 13 ones, no exchange → **fix:** "You can't keep 13 in the ones. Swap ten ones for one ten — now how many tens?"
- ❌ **If "73" (forgot the carried ten):** added ones but dropped the new ten → **fix:** "Where did your traded-in ten go? Add it to the tens column."

### ⏱ 20–25 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child solves 48 + 37 and 29 + 25 with blocks then writing. Once secure, interleave one with **no** exchange (42 + 35) so they decide *whether* to trade. Stretch: add three two-digit numbers (12 + 23 + 14).
**Watch for:** writing two digits in the ones column; forgetting to add the carried ten.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "the ten didn't get traded"; 3) go fully concrete — act out the exchange slowly; 4) one hint: "Count the ones — is it ten or more?"; 5) re-ask with smaller ones (43 + 24, no exchange) then build up; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 25–28 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me when we have to do 'the great exchange'." → *listen for:* "when the ones make 10 or more."
**Process praise:** "You spotted *exactly* when to trade ten ones for a ten — that's the whole secret of adding big numbers."
**Revisit later:** A two-digit add with exchange as a starter in Lessons 8 and 12; revisit in money (Lesson 16).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** physically swap 10 ones for 1 ten every time so 45 + 38 can never become "713" (KB fix); keep numbers with a clear exchange.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add three two-digit numbers; estimate the total first, then check.

**Extension (if time allows):** "The great exchange" challenge — find a sum that needs *two* trades isn't possible here, so: invent two prices that total exactly 80p.
**Assessment evidence:** Child solves 2 two-digit additions with regrouping (and one without), exchanging correctly and recording the right total.

## Lesson 7 — Subtracting two-digit numbers (with exchange)
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Calculation)** | **Duration:** 28 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can subtract two-digit numbers, exchanging a ten for ten ones when the ones are too small."

**You will need:** base-10 ten-rods and ones-cubes, place-value mat, mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** partitioning; the exchange idea from addition (Lesson 6, reversed).
**Key vocabulary:** **subtract / take away**, **exchange / borrow** (break one ten into ten ones), **not enough ones**, **tens**, **ones**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** When there aren't enough ones to take away, **break one ten into ten ones** — don't just do the smaller-from-larger flip. Pre-empt "7 − 2 because you can't do 2 − 7". Repeat: "Not enough ones? Break a ten."

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "You had 63p in your pocket and you spent 28p on a snack. How much is left? Let's build 63 and take 28 away — but watch, we'll hit a problem in the ones."
**Show:** "63 − 28" on the board.  **Do:** Build 63 on the mat (6 tens, 3 ones).
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Yesterday we swapped ten ones for a ten. What might we do today, the other way round?" → *listen for:* "break a ten into ten ones."
*(If shaky: show a rod, count it as ten cubes.)*

### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "I want to take 8 ones away, but I only have 3 ones — **not enough**! So I **break one ten** into ten ones." *(physically swap 1 rod for 10 cubes)* "Now I have 5 tens and 13 ones. Take 8 ones from 13 → 5 ones. Take 2 tens from 5 tens → 3 tens. That leaves **35**."
**Show / Do:** Act out breaking the ten; write "63 − 28: 50 + 13 − 28 = 30 + 5 = 35."
**Emphasize:** "Not enough ones? Break a ten — never flip 3 − 8 into 8 − 3."

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together: 52 − 27. Build 52. Are there enough ones to take 7?"
**Do:** Child sees 2 ones isn't enough, breaks a ten (52 → 40 and 12), subtracts.
**Ask:** "12 − 7, and 40 − 20?" → *listen for:* "5 ones and 2 tens — 25."

### ⏱ 16–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "63 − 28 = ?" *(revisit the hook to confirm)*
- ✅ **Expected:** 35 — probe: *"Show me where you broke the ten."*
- ❌ **If "45" (did 8 − 3 in the ones):** flipped the column → **fix:** "Can you really take 8 from only 3? Break a ten first — now how many ones do you have?"
- ❌ **If forgets a ten was used (answer 10 too big):** didn't reduce the tens → **fix:** "You used one of your tens to make ones — how many tens are left now?"

### ⏱ 20–25 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child solves 71 − 26 and 84 − 38 with blocks then writing. Interleave one needing **no** exchange (57 − 23) so they decide whether to break. Stretch: subtract across a multiple of ten (70 − 23).
**Watch for:** the smaller-from-larger flip; forgetting to reduce the tens after breaking one.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "the ones got flipped"; 3) go fully concrete — break the ten slowly together; 4) one hint: "Are there enough ones to take away?"; 5) re-ask a no-exchange version (48 − 25) then build up; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 25–28 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "When do we have to 'break the ten'?" → *listen for:* "when there aren't enough ones to take away."
**Process praise:** "You stopped yourself flipping the ones and broke a ten instead — that's exactly right."
**Revisit later:** Subtraction-with-exchange starter in Lesson 8 (inverse) and Lesson 12; reappears in money change (Lesson 16).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** model the exchange with blocks before any writing (KB fix for "7 − 2 because you can't do 2 − 7"); keep one ten clearly breakable.
- **Stretch (challenge):** subtract across a multiple of ten (70 − 23, 80 − 45); estimate first.

**Extension (if time allows):** "Break the ten" puzzle — find a subtraction where you must break a ten, and one where you don't; explain the difference.
**Assessment evidence:** Child solves 2 two-digit subtractions with exchange (and one without), breaking a ten correctly and recording the right answer.

## Lesson 8 — Inverse: addition and subtraction check each other
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Calculation)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can use an addition fact to check a subtraction (and the reverse)."

**You will need:** base-10 rods and cubes, fact-family triangle/cards, mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** adding and subtracting two-digit numbers (Lessons 6–7).
**Key vocabulary:** **inverse** (the opposite operation), **check**, **fact family**, **part-whole**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Addition and subtraction are **opposites** — one undoes the other, so you can use one to *check* the other. The same three numbers (the two parts and the whole) make a **fact family**. Repeat: "Add to put together, subtract to take apart — same three numbers."

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Imagine you finished a sum and you're not sure it's right. How can you *prove* it, like a detective proving a case? Today we learn a trick that checks your own answers."
**Show:** "38 + 25 = 63" on the board.  **Do:** Build it with blocks (38 and 25 together = 63).
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's the opposite of adding?" → *listen for:* "taking away / subtracting."
*(If shaky: "If I put 2 and 3 together I get 5. What takes me back to 2?")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Here's 38 + 25 = 63 built in blocks. The whole is 63; the parts are 38 and 25. Now I **undo** it: if I take the 25 away from 63, I should land back on 38." *(physically remove the 25)* "And look — 38! So **63 − 25 = 38**. Subtraction *undoes* addition. They check each other."
**Show / Do:** Remove a part from the whole; write the fact family: 38 + 25 = 63, 25 + 38 = 63, 63 − 25 = 38, 63 − 38 = 25.
**Emphasize:** "Same three numbers — add to join, subtract to take apart."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "You solved 27 + 46 = 73 last lesson. Let's *check* it. Take 46 away from 73 — where should we land?"
**Do:** Child removes 46 from 73 in blocks (or counts), landing on 27.
**Ask:** "Did we land back on 27?" → *listen for:* "yes — so the sum was right."

### ⏱ 12–15 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "If 38 + 25 = 63, what is 63 − 25?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 38 — probe: *"How did you know without calculating?"*
- ❌ **If recalculates from scratch and slips:** didn't use the inverse → **fix:** "You already have the three numbers — which one do you get back if you take 25 off 63?"
- ❌ **If says 25 (gives the wrong part):** took the whole minus the wrong part → **fix:** "Point to the two parts and the whole. We took one part away — what's left?"

### ⏱ 15–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child writes the full fact family for 40 + 15 = 55, then checks a given subtraction (82 − 31 = 51) by adding back. Once secure, interleave: "I got 64 − 28 = 36. Prove it." Stretch: write all four facts for any pair.
**Watch for:** mixing parts and whole; only writing two of the four facts.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "the parts and whole got swapped"; 3) keep the blocks built so the relationship is visible; 4) one hint: "The biggest number is the whole — start there for subtraction"; 5) re-ask with a bond within 20 (7 + 3 = 10 → 10 − 3 = ?); 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "How can subtraction prove an addition is right?" → *listen for:* "it undoes it — you land back on the part."
**Process praise:** "You checked your own work instead of just trusting it — that's a true mathematician."
**Revisit later:** "Prove it with the inverse" becomes a habit in every calculation lesson; revisit in Lesson 12.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** keep the blocks built so the part-whole relationship stays visible; use a fact-family triangle.
- **Stretch (challenge):** write all four facts in the family unaided; use the inverse to find a missing number (□ + 25 = 63).

**Extension (if time allows):** "The detective check" — solve three sums and prove each one correct with its inverse.
**Assessment evidence:** Child uses an addition fact to state the matching subtraction (and vice versa) and writes a correct fact family.

## Lesson 9 — Multiplication: equal groups and arrays
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Calculation)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can show a multiplication as equal groups and as an array."

**You will need:** counters, small plates or paper circles, dot-array cards, grapes/props (optional), mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** skip-counting in equal steps (Lesson 5).
**Key vocabulary:** **equal groups**, **lots of / times (×)**, **array** (rows and columns), **commutative** (same answer either way), **multiply**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Multiplication is **equal groups** — "3 × 5" means 3 groups of 5. An **array** shows it as rows and columns, and turning the array 90° proves **3 × 5 = 5 × 3** (commutative). Pre-empt "3 × 5 and 5 × 3 are different sums." Repeat: "Same dots, same total — turn it and check."

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Four plates, and on each plate there are 5 grapes. I want to know how many grapes there are — but I'm too lazy to count them all one by one. Is there a faster way?"
**Show:** 4 plates, 5 counters ("grapes") on each.  **Do:** Gesture across the equal plates.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Count them in 5s — 5, 10…?" → *listen for:* "15, 20 — twenty!"
*(If shaky: skip-count one plate at a time together.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Three groups of 5 counters — that's '3 times 5', written **3 × 5**. Now watch me line them up in neat rows: 3 rows, 5 in each — that's an **array**." *(build a 3×5 dot array)* "Count: 5, 10, 15 — fifteen. Now I **turn** the array a quarter turn. Now it's 5 rows of 3 — but it's the *same dots*! Still 15. So **3 × 5 = 5 × 3**."
**Show / Do:** Build the 3×5 array, rotate it 90°, count both ways.
**Emphasize:** "Turn the array — same dots, same answer."

### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together: show me 2 × 5. How many groups, how many in each?"
**Do:** Child makes 2 groups of 5 counters, then arranges as a 2×5 array.
**Ask:** "How many altogether, and what happens if you turn it?" → *listen for:* "10 — and 5 × 2 is also 10."

### ⏱ 14–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "3 × 5 = ?  And if 2 × 10 = 20, what is 10 × 2?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 15; and 20 — probe: *"How do you know 10 × 2 without recounting?"*
- ❌ **If "8" for 3 × 5 (adds 3 + 5):** confused × with + → **fix:** "× means *groups of*. Make 3 groups of 5 — count them."
- ❌ **If thinks 10 × 2 needs a new calculation:** doesn't see commutativity → **fix:** "Turn the array for 2 × 10 — does the number of dots change?"

### ⏱ 17–21 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child builds and writes the fact for: 4 groups of 2, a 5×2 array, 3 groups of 10. For each, they write **both** facts (e.g. 4 × 2 and 2 × 4). Once secure, interleave: "Draw an array that shows 5 × 5."
**Watch for:** unequal groups; adding instead of grouping; only writing one of the two facts.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "the groups weren't equal" or "× got read as +"; 3) go concrete — physically make the equal groups; 4) one hint: "How many groups? How many in each?"; 5) re-ask with 2 groups of 2; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 21–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What does the × sign tell us to do, and why is 3 × 5 the same as 5 × 3?" → *listen for:* "groups of; turning the array keeps the same dots."
**Process praise:** "You turned the array to *prove* the two facts match — you earned two facts for the price of one."
**Revisit later:** Arrays return in Lesson 10 (tables) and Lesson 11 (division); skip-count starter keeps it warm.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** count the array in skip-counts; keep the groups physically separated on plates.
- **Stretch (challenge):** write both facts for the same array; "would turning *any* array always keep the same total? Why?"

**Extension (if time allows):** "Spin the array" — make an array, predict the turned fact, spin and check; collect five matching pairs.
**Assessment evidence:** Child represents a multiplication as equal groups *and* an array, states the total, and writes both commutative facts.

## Lesson 10 — The 2, 5 and 10 times tables
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Calculation)** | **Duration:** 28 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall facts from the 2, 5 and 10 times tables."

**You will need:** a number line, 5p and 10p coins, fact cards and matching array cards, mini-whiteboard, a timer.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** equal groups and arrays (Lesson 9); skip-counting (Lesson 5).
**Key vocabulary:** **times table**, **multiply (×)**, **fact**, **array**, **recall** (say it fast, from memory).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A times table is just **repeated skip-counting** written as facts — five 5s (5, 10, 15, 20, 25) *is* 5 × 5. Use the count or the array to *find* a fact, then aim to **recall** it. Keep commutativity alive (2 × 10 = 10 × 2).

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Count five 5p coins with me: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. Guess what — you just did a times-table fact in disguise! Five 5s is 5 × 5 = 25. Today we collect these facts so they pop into your head super-fast."
**Show:** Five 5p coins in a row.  **Do:** Count them, then reveal "5 × 5 = 25."
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does 3 × 5 mean?" → *listen for:* "3 groups of 5."
*(If shaky: rebuild a 3×5 array quickly.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "I'll build the 5 times table from skip-counting. One 5 is 5. Two 5s — 10. Three 5s — 15. Each jump is a new fact: 1 × 5 = 5, 2 × 5 = 10, 3 × 5 = 15… The 10s are even easier — they all end in 0. And the 2s are the doubles: 2, 4, 6, 8."
**Show / Do:** Jumps on the number line, each labelled with its fact; lay matching array cards beside three of them.
**Emphasize:** "A fact is just the skip-count, frozen — find it by counting, then remember it."

### ⏱ 9–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's match facts to arrays. Here's 4 × 5 — find the array with 4 rows of 5 and tell me the total."
**Do:** Child matches fact cards to array cards; uses the number line to confirm (5, 10, 15, 20).
**Ask:** "What is 4 × 5, and what is 4 × 10?" → *listen for:* "20, and 40."

### ⏱ 15–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "If 2 × 10 = 20, what is 10 × 2?  And what is 5 × 5?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 20; and 25 — probe: *"How could you check 5 × 5 quickly?"* (count in 5s five times)
- ❌ **If recalculates 10 × 2 the long way:** ignores commutativity → **fix:** "You already know 2 × 10 — turning it doesn't change the total."
- ❌ **If "5 × 5 = 10" (adds):** × read as + → **fix:** "Five groups of 5 — skip-count: 5, 10, 15…"

### ⏱ 18–25 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Quick-fire on whiteboards: 3 × 2, 6 × 5, 4 × 10, 7 × 5, 2 × 8. For any they don't recall, they *find* it by skip-counting on the number line, then try to remember it. Once secure, interleave a beat-your-time round. Stretch: mix in 10 × 9, 5 × 8.
**Watch for:** defaulting to counting when recall is emerging — gently nudge toward memory; adding instead of multiplying.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "this fact isn't memorised yet"; 3) go concrete — skip-count along the number line to it; 4) one hint: "Start from a fact you *do* know and jump on" (e.g. 5 × 5 = 25, so 6 × 5 = 30); 5) re-ask a nearby easy fact; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 25–28 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me one fact you can now say without counting." → *listen for:* a recalled 2/5/10 fact.
**Process praise:** "You used the facts you already knew to jump to new ones — that's the smart way to learn tables."
**Revisit later:** Daily 60-second tables round (2, 5, 10); revisit in Lessons 11, 12 and money/fractions.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** skip-count along a number line to reach a fact; keep coins and array cards on hand.
- **Stretch (challenge):** "Hit the Button" speed round; derive bigger facts (8 × 5, 9 × 10) from known ones.

**Extension (if time allows):** beat-your-own-time tables challenge — record today's score, try to beat it next session.
**Assessment evidence:** Child recalls a spread of 2/5/10 facts (and finds the rest by skip-counting), including a commutative pair.

## Lesson 11 — Division as sharing and grouping
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Calculation)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide by sharing equally and by making equal groups."

**You will need:** 20+ counters, paper plates, mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** equal groups / arrays and the 5 times table (Lessons 9–10).
**Key vocabulary:** **divide (÷)**, **share equally**, **group**, **equal**, **inverse of multiplication**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Division means **fair, equal shares** — deal one-to-each like cards until they're gone. It can also mean **grouping** ("how many 5s in 20?"). It **undoes** multiplication: 4 × 5 = 20, so 20 ÷ 5 = 4. Pre-empt "count 12 then guess" — share, don't guess.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "20 sweets, and 5 friends are coming over. We have to share them so it's totally **fair** — no one gets more than anyone else, or there'll be tears! How do we make sure it's fair?"
**Show:** 20 counters, 5 plates.  **Do:** Let the child suggest a method.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What is 4 × 5?" → *listen for:* "20."
*(If shaky: skip-count 5, 10, 15, 20.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "To share fairly, I **deal one to each plate**, like dealing cards. One, one, one, one, one… round again… until they're all gone." *(deal the 20 counters one-to-each across 5 plates)* "Now every plate has the same — count one plate: 4. So **20 shared between 5 is 4 each**: 20 ÷ 5 = 4. And look — that's the opposite of 4 × 5 = 20!"
**Show / Do:** Deal one-to-each; show every plate has 4; write "20 ÷ 5 = 4."
**Emphasize:** "Deal one to each, round and round, until they're gone — that's fair sharing."

### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together, the other way — **grouping**. Here are 20 counters. How many groups of 5 can we make? Make groups of 5 and count the groups."
**Do:** Child makes groups of 5 (4 groups).
**Ask:** "How many 5s in 20?" → *listen for:* "4 — so 20 ÷ 5 = 4 again."

### ⏱ 14–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Share 20 equally between 5 — how many each?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 4 — probe: *"How does that link to 4 × 5?"*
- ❌ **If guesses (e.g. "5"):** counted then guessed, didn't deal equally → **fix:** "Deal one to each plate like cards — keep going until they're gone; now count one plate."
- ❌ **If unequal piles:** didn't share one-to-each → **fix:** "Each round, every plate gets exactly one. Try again, slowly."

### ⏱ 17–21 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child solves: share 12 between 3; share 10 between 5; "how many 2s in 10?" by grouping. For each, link to the matching × fact. Once secure, interleave: "From 4 × 5 = 20, write both division facts." Stretch: a sharing with a remainder context discussed aloud (13 between 4 — "one left over").
**Watch for:** guessing instead of dealing; not dealing one-to-each; forgetting the link to multiplication.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "the sharing wasn't one-to-each"; 3) go concrete — deal like cards together; 4) one hint: "Every plate gets one before any plate gets two"; 5) re-ask with 6 between 3; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 21–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the two ways we can divide, and how division links to times tables." → *listen for:* "sharing and grouping; division undoes multiplication."
**Process praise:** "You dealt fairly one-to-each instead of guessing — that's exactly how division works."
**Revisit later:** Division feeds fractions of quantities (Lesson 14) directly; revisit in Lesson 12.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** deal one-to-each like cards until gone (KB fix for guessing); keep the plates labelled.
- **Stretch (challenge):** find *both* division facts from 4 × 5 = 20; discuss a share that leaves one left over.

**Extension (if time allows):** play "fair dealer" — deal a small deck so every player gets the same; write the division fact.
**Assessment evidence:** Child shares a quantity equally and groups it, gives the correct quotient, and links it to a multiplication fact.

## Lesson 12 — Retrieval & fluency: calculation
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Calculation)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can quickly add/subtract two-digit numbers and recall 2/5/10 facts."

**You will need:** a mini-whiteboard and pen, base-10 blocks and a number line (as backup tools), a "train track" with stations (for the solo relay), a timer.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** everything from Unit 2 — regrouping add/subtract, tables, division, inverse.
**Key vocabulary:** (all Unit 2 vocab revisited) **regroup**, **exchange**, **inverse**, **estimate**, **fact**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** This is a **fluency workout** — speed *and* accuracy, with **estimating first** to catch silly answers. Mix the strands so the child chooses the right method. No new idea today: it's all retrieval.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "It's Maths Gym Day! We've built lots of strong maths muscles this unit — adding, taking away, times tables, sharing. Today we flex *all* of them and see how fast and strong they've got. Warm up: count in 5s to 30!"
**Show:** A "gym" theme on the board; the train track ready.  **Do:** Lead the skip-count warm-up.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's the first thing a good mathematician does before solving — guesses roughly. Why?" → *listen for:* "to check the answer makes sense."

### ⏱ 3–6 min · EXPLAIN (I do — model the routine)
> **Say:** "Here's our routine for each question: **estimate**, **solve**, **check**. Take 48 + 37. Estimate: 50 and 40 is about 90. Solve with the ones first — 8 + 7 = 15, exchange — answer 85. Check: is 85 near my estimate of 90? Yes! ✓"
**Show / Do:** Work 48 + 37 on the board using the estimate–solve–check routine.
**Emphasize:** "Estimate first — it's your safety net."

### ⏱ 6–9 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together on whiteboards: 71 − 26. Estimate first."
**Do:** Child estimates (~50), solves with exchange (45), checks against the estimate; then 6 × 5.
**Ask:** "6 × 5, and then 30 ÷ 5?" → *listen for:* "30, then 6 — they're inverses."

### ⏱ 9–12 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Solve 30 ÷ 10, then prove 48 + 37 = 85 using subtraction."
- ✅ **Expected:** 3; and 85 − 37 = 48 (so the sum is right) — probe: *"How does the check prove it?"*
- ❌ **If 30 ÷ 10 = 20 or 300:** confused operation → **fix:** "How many 10s in 30? Skip-count: 10, 20, 30."
- ❌ **If can't use the inverse to check:** → **fix:** "The whole is 85; take a part (37) away — where should you land?"

### ⏱ 12–17 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Quick-fire round on the whiteboard, estimate-solve-check each: 56 + 28, 64 − 39, 7 × 5, 4 × 10, 25 ÷ 5, plus one inverse check of their choice. Run a **solo relay** — each correct answer moves the train one station. Once secure, interleave a tiny word problem ("5 bags of 5 — how many?").
**Watch for:** skipping the estimate; regrouping slips; reaching for tools when recall is there.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — slip vs missing fact vs wrong operation; 3) hand them the backup tool (blocks / number line) for that one; 4) one hint or sentence starter; 5) re-ask a smaller version, then build back up; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 17–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Which maths muscle felt strongest today, and which one needs more gym time?" → *listen for:* honest self-assessment naming a strand.
**Process praise:** "You estimated before every answer — that's why your silly mistakes nearly vanished."
**Revisit later:** Use the child's named weak strand to pick the next warm-up; full mixed retrieval again before Unit 8.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** base-10 blocks and a number line stay available; fewer questions, same routine.
- **Stretch (challenge):** timed streak with no tools; add a two-step question.

**Extension (if time allows):** be the "quiz master" — write three calculation questions (with answers) for the tutor to solve.
**Assessment evidence:** Child completes a mixed calculation set with mostly accurate, fluent answers, estimating first and using an inverse check.

> **End-of-Unit 2 check:** (1) Count in 3s to 15 → 3, 6, 9, 12, 15. (2) 48 + 37 → 85. (3) 71 − 26 → 45. (4) 3 × 5 → 15. (5) Share 12 between 3 → 4 each.

## Lesson 13 — Thirds and quarters of shapes
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Fractions)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can find ⅓, ¼ and ¾ of a shape and explain that the parts must be equal."

**You will need:** paper strips and paper circles (several each), colouring pencils, scissors, a real chocolate bar or a paper one marked in squares.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** halves and quarters from Year 1 — a fraction is one of *equal* parts.
**Key vocabulary:** **equal parts**, **third (⅓)**, **quarter (¼)**, **three-quarters (¾)**, **denominator** ("the bottom number says how many equal parts").
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** the parts must be **EQUAL**, and — the big idea — **the more equal parts you cut, the SMALLER each part is** (so ⅓ is smaller than ½, not bigger). Pre-empt the classic misconception "⅓ > ½ because 3 > 2."

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Three friends, one chocolate bar — and everyone wants a fair share. How do we split it so nobody grumbles? Quick first — what does *equal* mean?"
**Show:** the chocolate bar held up.  **Do:** mime trying to break it unfairly (one giant piece, two tiny) — let them protest.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Last year we made halves and quarters. What made a piece a *real* half?" → *listen for:* "both parts the same / equal."
*(If 'equal' is shaky: fold a strip into two matching halves and say "equal means they land exactly on top.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch. To make **thirds** I fold this strip into **three equal parts** — like a letter going in an envelope. Three equal parts: each one is a *third*, and we write ⅓. The bottom number, 3, tells me how many equal parts I made. Now this strip into **four** equal parts — quarters, ¼. If I colour **three** of the four, that's **three-quarters**, ¾."
**Show / Do:** fold one strip into thirds, another into quarters; shade 3 of the 4 on the quarters strip. Line the third-strip beside the quarter-strip.
**Emphasize:** "More pieces means each piece is *smaller*. Look — a third is bigger than a quarter, because I cut fewer pieces."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's fold one together. I'll start the circle into three — you tell me when the three parts look *equal*."
**Do:** fold a paper circle into thirds with the child judging equality; child shades one third and says "⅓"; then together shade three quarters of a quarters-circle.
**Ask:** "I've shaded three of four equal parts — what fraction is shaded?" → *listen for:* "three-quarters / ¾."

### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which is bigger — ½ or ¼? Show me with the strips." *(distractor in their head: "¼ because 4 is bigger.")*
- ✅ **Expected:** ½ — probe: *"How do the strips prove it?"* (the half-piece is the longer one).
- ❌ **If "¼" (or "⅓ > ½"):** treating the bigger bottom number as the bigger piece → **fix (KB):** fold the *same* strip into 2 vs 3 vs 4 and lay the single pieces side by side — "more cuts, smaller pieces."
- ❌ **If calls an unequal split "thirds":** counting pieces, not equal pieces → **fix:** "Do the three parts match if we fold them on top of each other? If not, they aren't thirds."

### ⏱ 17–21 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child folds/shades to show ⅓, ¼ and ¾ on fresh strips. Then a sorting task: cards of shapes split into parts — "tick the ones split into *equal* thirds/quarters; cross the unfair ones." Once secure, interleave: "Show me ¾ of a square a *different* way."
**Watch for:** unequal folds called fractions; "¾ shaded" muddled with "¾ left."
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — unequal parts vs more-pieces-bigger; 3) drop to concrete — fold and overlay the pieces; 4) one hint: "Do the parts match exactly?"; 5) re-ask with halves vs quarters only; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — let's take a smaller step."

### ⏱ 21–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "In your own words — what makes a piece a *third*, and which is bigger, a third or a quarter?" → *listen for:* "three equal parts; a third is bigger."
**Process praise:** "You checked the parts were *equal* before you named the fraction — that's a real mathematician's habit."
**Revisit later:** feeds straight into fractions of a *quantity* next lesson; quick recall in Lesson 25.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** fold the same bar into 2 vs 3 to feel that thirds are smaller (KB fix for "⅓ > ½"); pre-creased strips.
- **Stretch (challenge):** "Is this split into quarters?" with a deliberately unequal split → no; show ¾ two different ways.

**Extension (if time allows):** make a "fraction flag" — design a flag that is exactly ¾ one colour, ¼ another.
**Assessment evidence:** child folds/shades correct thirds and quarters, identifies ¾, and explains that ½ > ¼ because fewer parts are larger.

## Lesson 14 — Fractions of a quantity
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Fractions)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can find a unit and non-unit fraction of an amount by sharing."

**You will need:** counters (at least 24), small paper plates or sharing mats (4), bowls, a picture of 12 sweets.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** sharing equally / division as one-to-each (Lesson 11); a quarter = 4 equal parts.
**Key vocabulary:** **fraction of an amount**, **share equally**, **unit fraction** (¼), **non-unit fraction** (¾), **denominator = number of piles**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** finding ¼ of an amount means **share into the bottom-number of equal piles** (¼ → 4 piles), then count **one** pile; ¾ means count **three** piles. The trap: doing "12 − 4" instead of sharing.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "I've got 12 sweets and I want to give you a *quarter* of them. How many sweets is that? Hmm — how could we work it out fairly?"
**Show:** 12 counters ("sweets") in a pile.  **Do:** slide the pile forward invitingly.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "When we shared 12 between 3 last week, what did we do?" → *listen for:* "dealt one to each pile until gone."

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A quarter means *four* equal parts. So to find a quarter of 12, I share all 12 into **4** equal plates. Watch me deal: one, two, three, four… one, two, three, four… until they're gone. Now each plate has 3. **One** plate is a quarter — so ¼ of 12 is **3**. That's the same as 12 ÷ 4."
**Show / Do:** deal 12 counters one-to-each across 4 plates; point to one plate = 3.
**Emphasize:** "The bottom number tells me **how many plates**. Share first, then count the plates I need."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn to deal. Find ¾ of 12 — first, how many plates?"
**Do:** child shares 12 across 4 plates (3 each); for ¾ they count **three** plates → 9. Tutor scribes "¾ of 12 = 9."
**Ask:** "One plate is ¼ = 3. So three plates, ¾, is…?" → *listen for:* "9 (3 + 3 + 3 / 3 × 3)."

### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What is ¾ of 12?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 9 — probe: *"How did the plates help?"*
- ❌ **If "8" (did 12 − 4):** subtracting the denominator instead of sharing → **fix (KB):** "We *share*, not take away. Deal 12 into 4 plates — how many in three plates?"
- ❌ **If "3" (gave a quarter):** stopped at one plate → **fix:** "¾ means *three* of the four plates — count three."

### ⏱ 17–21 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child finds ¼ of 8, ½ of 10, ¾ of 8 by dealing then counting. Record each as "share into __ plates, count __." Once secure, interleave: "Find ⅓ of 9" (new denominator → 3 plates).
**Watch for:** subtracting the denominator; uneven dealing; counting the wrong number of plates.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — subtracting vs sharing, or wrong plate count; 3) go fully concrete — deal like cards together; 4) one hint: "Bottom number = number of plates"; 5) re-ask with ½ of 6; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 21–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the steps to find ¾ of a number." → *listen for:* "share into 4 equal piles, then count 3 of them."
**Process praise:** "You dealt one-to-each instead of guessing — fair sharing is the secret to fractions."
**Revisit later:** links division (L11) to fractions; revisit in Lesson 25 and the Unit 8 mixed problems.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** physically deal into the denominator's number of piles (KB fix for "12 − 4"); label the plates 1–4.
- **Stretch (challenge):** find ⅓ of 9 and ¾ of 8; "if ¼ of a number is 5, what's the whole number?"

**Extension (if time allows):** "fair feast" — share a bag of (pretend) grapes and write the fraction each friend gets.
**Assessment evidence:** child finds unit and non-unit fractions of amounts by sharing, links ¼ to ÷4, and avoids the subtract-the-denominator error.

> **End-of-unit 3 check:** (1) ¼ of 8 → 2. (2) ¾ of 12 → 9. (3) Which is bigger, ½ or ¼? → ½. (4) Is an unequal split into 3 "thirds"? → no, parts not equal.

## Lesson 15 — Pounds, pence and making amounts
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 4 (Money)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can show that £1 = 100p and make an amount the efficient way."

**You will need:** a tray of plastic coins (lots of 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1, £2), a clear "pound pot" jar, price labels.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** counting in 2s/5s/10s; a coin's value isn't its size (Year 1 money).
**Key vocabulary:** **pound (£)**, **pence (p)**, **£1 = 100p**, **fewest coins**, **total**, **decimal point** ("the dot splits pounds from pence").
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **£1 = 100p**, and to make an amount efficiently start with the **biggest coins first**. Pre-empt "£1.5 means one pound five pence" — the two digits after the dot are *pence*, so £1.50 = 1 pound and 50 pence.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "How many pennies do you think it takes to make a whole pound? Let's count them into the pot and find out — call out the tens with me!"
**Show:** the empty pound pot and a heap of 1p coins.  **Do:** drop coins counting "10, 20, 30…" inviting the child to join.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Which is worth more — a tiny 5p or a big 2p?" → *listen for:* "5p" (value, not size).

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "One hundred pennies make **one pound** — £1 = 100p. But carrying 100 pennies is silly! Watch me make 80p the clever way: biggest coin that fits first — a 50p… then a 20p makes 70… then a 10p makes 80. Three coins, not eighty. And when we write money like **£1.50**, the dot splits it: 1 *pound* and 50 *pence*."
**Show / Do:** swap two 50p coins for £1; build 80p with 50p + 20p + 10p; write £1.50 and point to the parts.
**Emphasize:** "Start with the **biggest** coin that fits — fewest coins."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Make 65p with the fewest coins — which big coin first?"
**Do:** child chooses 50p, then 10p, then 5p (= 65p); tutor checks the count by counting up "50, 60, 65."
**Ask:** "Is £1.05 one pound and five pence, or one pound and fifty pence?" → *listen for:* "one pound and five pence" (the 0 holds the tens place).

### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "How many pence are in £1? And show me £1.50 in coins."
- ✅ **Expected:** 100p; £1 + 50p — probe: *"How do you know the .50 is fifty pence not five?"*
- ❌ **If reads £1.5 as "one pound five pence":** ignoring place value after the dot → **fix (KB):** "£1.50 = 1 pound and 50 pence; £1.05 = 1 pound and 5 pence" — build both with coins side by side.
- ❌ **If uses many small coins:** not starting big → **fix:** "What's the *biggest* coin that still fits?"

### ⏱ 17–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child makes 30p, 75p, 90p and £1.20 with the **fewest** coins; records the coins used. Once secure, interleave: "Make 80p a *different* way" (shows several totals work; fewest is one choice).
**Watch for:** size-not-value errors; misreading the decimal; not minimising coins.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — value vs size, or decimal place; 3) concrete — lay coins big→small; 4) one hint: "Biggest coin that fits first"; 5) re-ask with 30p; 6) keep it safe — "smaller step."

### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Two things to remember about money?" → *listen for:* "£1 = 100p; start with the biggest coin."
**Process praise:** "You reached for the 50p first every time — that's efficient, real-shop thinking."
**Revisit later:** straight into totalling and change next lesson; revisit in Unit 8.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** read £1.50 as "1 pound and 50 pence" with coins laid out (KB fix for £1.5); fewer target amounts.
- **Stretch (challenge):** make £1.35 the fewest-coins way; "what's the fewest coins for 99p?"

**Extension (if time allows):** price three toys and find the fewest coins to buy each.
**Assessment evidence:** child states £1 = 100p, makes amounts with fewest coins, and reads simple £.pp amounts correctly.

## Lesson 16 — Adding amounts and finding change
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 4 (Money)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can total a small shopping list and work out change by counting up."

**You will need:** plastic coins, a pretend shop with priced items (pen 70p, rubber 30p, ruler 25p, toy 65p), a £1 and £2 coin, mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** £1 = 100p (Lesson 15); counting up to the next ten (number bonds).
**Key vocabulary:** **total**, **cost**, **change**, **count up**, **money given − cost = change**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** to find change, **count UP** from the price to the money given (don't try to count back / subtract awkwardly). Pre-empt subtracting the wrong way round.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "A pen costs 70p. You hand over a whole pound coin. Does the shopkeeper keep the extra — or give you some back? How much back?"
**Show:** the pen with its 70p label and a £1 coin.  **Do:** play shopkeeper holding out a hand.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "How many pence in £1?" → *listen for:* "100p."

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "First, totalling. A rubber 30p and a ruler 25p — I count on: 30… 40, 50 (that's the 20)… 55. Total 55p. Now **change**. A 65p toy, I pay £1. I **count up** from the price: 65 → 70 is 5p… 70 → 100 is 30p. 5 and 30 is **35p change**. Counting up is easier than taking away."
**Show / Do:** total 30p + 25p with coins; then count up from 65p to 100p, laying coins as you go (5p, then 10p×3).
**Emphasize:** "For change, start at the **price** and count **up** to what you paid."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together: a pen is 70p, you pay £1. Count up with me from 70."
**Do:** child counts 70 → 100 = 30p, placing coins; tutor confirms by re-counting "70, 80, 90, 100."
**Ask:** "So the change is…?" → *listen for:* "30p."

### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "A pen costs 70p; you pay £1. What's your change?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 30p — probe: *"Show me the count up."*
- ❌ **If "70p" or a muddle (counted the wrong direction):** subtracted price *from* nothing / reversed → **fix (KB):** "Start at the price, 70, and count up to 100 — how far is it?"
- ❌ **If totals 30p + 25p as 50p or 65p:** miscount over the ten → **fix:** "Add the tens: 30 + 20 = 50, then the 5 → 55."

### ⏱ 17–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child runs the shop: total two items, then give change from £1 (e.g. rubber + ruler = 55p, change from £1 = 45p). Once secure, interleave a change-from-£2 problem.
**Watch for:** counting up across the 100 boundary; forgetting to total before finding change.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — direction of counting or the over-ten jump; 3) coins out, count up physically; 4) one hint: "How far from the price to the pound?"; 5) re-ask change from £1 for a 90p item; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "How do we work out change — count up or count back?" → *listen for:* "count up from the price."
**Process praise:** "You counted up coin by coin instead of guessing — that's how real shopkeepers do it."
**Revisit later:** money problems return in Lesson 27 and Unit 8.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** count up coin by coin from the price (KB fix); pre-totalled first item.
- **Stretch (challenge):** two items and change from £2; "what if you paid with the fewest coins?"

**Extension (if time allows):** be the shopkeeper for the tutor — set prices, take payment, give change.
**Assessment evidence:** child totals two amounts and finds correct change by counting up.

> **End-of-unit 4 check:** (1) How many pence in £1? → 100p. (2) 40p + 35p → 75p. (3) A 70p pen, pay £1 — change? → 30p. (4) Make 80p with the fewest coins → 50p + 20p + 10p.

## Lesson 17 — Measuring length with a ruler
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 5 (Measurement)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can measure a length in cm by lining the object up with zero."

**You will need:** rulers (cm), a tape measure, a set of objects to measure (pencil, book, crayon, ribbon), sticky notes, a strip of paper for a height mark.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** counting on a number line / number track from 0.
**Key vocabulary:** **length**, **centimetre (cm)**, **metre (m)**, **zero**, **measure**, **compare**, **longer/shorter**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **always line the end of the object up with the 0 on the ruler**, then read where the *other* end lands. Pre-empt the classic error of starting at 1 (which makes every answer one short).

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "How tall do you think you are in centimetres? Bigger than a metre stick? Today we become measuring experts and find out exactly."
**Show:** the tape measure stretched out; a metre stick.  **Do:** hold the metre stick against the child.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "On a number line, where do we usually start counting?" → *listen for:* "at 0."

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch the most important rule. I put the **end of the pencil exactly on the 0** — not the 1, the **0**. Now I read where the *other* end stops: 14. So the pencil is **14 cm**. If I'm lazy and start at 1, every answer is one too small — so: end on zero!"
**Show / Do:** measure a pencil correctly from 0; then deliberately start at 1 and show the wrong, one-short reading.
**Emphasize:** "Start at **zero**, read the far end."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "You hold the ruler, I'll hold the crayon. Tell me — is the end on zero? Now read the other end."
**Do:** child aligns the ruler to 0 and reads the crayon's length; tutor checks alignment.
**Ask:** "The crayon's end lines up with 9 — how long is it?" → *listen for:* "9 cm."

### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "An object lies along the ruler from 0 to 15 — how long is it?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 15 cm — probe: *"Where did the object start?"*
- ❌ **If "14" (started at 1) or off-by-one:** didn't align to 0 → **fix (KB):** "Show me the *start* of the object — is it on 0 or 1? Slide it to 0 and read again."
- ❌ **If reads the near end:** read the wrong end → **fix:** "We read where it *finishes*, the far end."

### ⏱ 17–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child measures four objects to the nearest cm, recording each on a sticky note, then **orders** them shortest→longest. Once secure, interleave: "Which two together are longer than the book?"
**Watch for:** starting at 1; the ruler slipping off zero; reading the wrong end.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — alignment vs reading end; 3) put a finger on the 0 and the object's end together; 4) one hint: "End on zero"; 5) re-measure a short crayon; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What's the golden rule for using a ruler?" → *listen for:* "line the end up with 0."
**Process praise:** "You checked the zero every single time — that's why your measurements were spot on."
**Revisit later:** comparing/ordering lengths returns in Unit 8 problems.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** show how starting at 1 makes the answer one short (KB fix); pre-place objects at 0.
- **Stretch (challenge):** measure to the nearest cm and compare three objects; estimate first, then measure.

**Extension (if time allows):** measure their own height against a wall strip and label it in cm.
**Assessment evidence:** child aligns to 0, reads length in cm accurately, and orders objects by length.

## Lesson 18 — Reading scales: mass and capacity
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 5 (Measurement)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can read a scale and choose the right unit for mass and capacity."

**You will need:** kitchen scales, a marked measuring jug, water, a tall thin glass and a short wide cup (same capacity), fruit to weigh, a jug of coloured water for visibility, a towel.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** counting in 100s/in intervals; reading numbers on a scale (like a ruler).
**Key vocabulary:** **mass**, **gram (g)**, **kilogram (kg)**, **capacity**, **millilitre (ml)**, **litre (l)**, **scale**, **interval**, **compare**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** read the **intervals** on a scale (each mark is worth a set amount), and the big surprise — **a taller container does NOT always hold more**. Pre-empt "the tall glass holds more."

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Two containers — this tall skinny glass and this short fat cup. Which one holds more water? Tell me your guess… let's actually pour and find out, because it might surprise you."
**Show:** the tall glass and short wide cup side by side.  **Do:** ask the child to commit to a guess.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "On the ruler, what did each little mark stand for?" → *listen for:* "an amount / a number we read."

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **scale** has marks called **intervals**, and each is worth a set amount. On this jug, each big mark is 100 ml. I pour to the second mark — 100, 200 — so that's 200 ml. For heavy things we use **grams** and **kilograms**: 1 kg is 1000 g, so 1 kg is heavier than 500 g. We pick the unit that fits — grams for an apple, kilograms for a bag of potatoes."
**Show / Do:** pour water to 200 ml reading the intervals aloud; weigh an apple in grams; hold up the 1 kg vs 500 g comparison.
**Emphasize:** "Read what **each mark is worth** first, then count the marks."

### ⏱ 8–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do — and the reveal)
> **Say:** "Now the big test. We poured the tall glass full. Watch — I tip *all* of it into the short wide cup. Same water! The tall one looked like more, but it isn't. Your turn to read the jug — pour to the third mark, what's the amount?"
**Do:** pour the tall glass into the wide cup to show equal volume; child pours to a marked level and reads it.
**Ask:** "Which is heavier — 1 kg or 500 g?" → *listen for:* "1 kg."

### ⏱ 14–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which is heavier, 1 kg or 500 g? And read this jug (filled to the second 100 ml mark)."
- ✅ **Expected:** 1 kg; 200 ml — probe: *"How much is each mark worth?"*
- ❌ **If "the tall glass holds more":** judging by height → **fix (KB):** "Remember the pour — same water filled both. Height doesn't decide; the jug's marks do."
- ❌ **If miscounts intervals:** ignored the value per mark → **fix:** "Each mark is 100 — touch and count: 100, 200."

### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child weighs three fruits and **orders** them; reads two jug levels; picks the sensible unit for items ("a strawberry — g or kg?"). Once secure, interleave a scale where each mark is 100 ml and the pointer is between marks ("about 150 ml").
**Watch for:** height = more thinking; miscounting intervals; unit confusion.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — appearance vs scale, or interval value; 3) re-pour / re-count marks together; 4) one hint: "What is one mark worth?"; 5) re-ask an easier read; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 22–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What surprised you today about the tall glass?" → *listen for:* "taller doesn't mean more — read the scale."
**Process praise:** "You trusted the *measurement* over how it looked — that's careful science-maths thinking."
**Revisit later:** measures combine in Unit 8 problems; revisit reading scales then.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** count the marked intervals aloud together; coloured water for clear reading.
- **Stretch (challenge):** read a scale where each mark is 100 ml and the level is between marks; convert 1 kg = 1000 g.

**Extension (if time allows):** "junior chef" — measure out 300 ml of water and 200 g of an ingredient.
**Assessment evidence:** child reads mass and capacity scales using interval value, compares units, and rejects the height-means-more error.

> **End-of-unit 5 check:** (1) Which is longer, 25 cm or 30 cm? → 30 cm. (2) Which is heavier, 1 kg or 500 g? → 1 kg. (3) A jug marked every 100 ml reaches the second mark → 200 ml. (4) Object from 0 to 15 → 15 cm.

## Lesson 19 — Minutes past the hour

## Lesson 20 — Minutes to the hour
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 6 (Time)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can read 'minutes to' times once the minute hand passes the 6."

**You will need:** a geared teaching clock with the past/to halves marked (a sticky line down the middle), number cards, a timer.

**Key vocabulary:** **to**, **quarter to**, **half past**, **count down**, **next hour**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** once the minute hand passes the **6** (half past), we switch to counting **how many minutes are LEFT until the next hour** — "minutes to." Pre-empt "40 past" when it should be "20 to."

---

**Do:** child counts 9→12 (5, 10, 15) and says "quarter to / 15 to"; then on the 10 → 10 to.
**Ask:** "Minute hand on 9 — say it as '___ to.'" → *listen for:* "quarter to / 15 minutes to."

### ⏱ 14–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "The minute hand is on the 8 — read the time as '___ to'." 
- ✅ **Expected:** 20 to — probe: *"How many minutes are left to the top?"*
- ❌ **If "40 past":** still counting up past 6 → **fix (KB):** "After half past we count what's **left**: 8 up to 12 is 20 — so 20 *to*."
- ❌ **If counts the wrong direction:** → **fix:** "Count from the hand *up* to the 12."

### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)

> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — wrong half, or direction; 3) mark the past/to line and count left side together; 4) one hint: "How far to the 12?"; 5) re-ask 'on the 10'; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 22–24 min · RECAP

**Extension (if time allows):** "what's the time?" race — the tutor sets a time, the child reads it, then the child sets a time for the tutor to read.

## Lesson 22 — Block diagrams: most, least, how many more
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 7 (Statistics, shape & symmetry)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can read a block diagram to answer 'most', 'least' and 'how many more'."

**You will need:** block-diagram cards (cars by colour; pets), squared paper, a window or picture of passing traffic, mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** subtraction (how many more = difference); reading data (Lesson 21).
**Key vocabulary:** **block diagram**, **bar**, **most**, **least**, **how many more**, **total**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** the **tallest bar is the most**, the shortest the least; **"how many more" means subtract** the two bars. 

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Which colour car drives past the window the most? We could argue — or we could chart it and *know*."
**Show:** a block diagram of car colours.  **Do:** point along the bars.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If I have 6 and you have 4, how many more do I have?" → *listen for:* "2."

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Each block is one count. The **tallest** bar is the **most**; the shortest is the **least**. To find **how many more**, I compare two bars and **subtract**. Dogs 6, cats 4: 6 − 4 = 2, so 2 more dogs. To **total** two groups I add the bars."
**Show / Do:** read the tallest bar; subtract two bars to answer 'how many more'; add two bars for a total.
**Emphasize:** "'How many more' = find the **difference** — subtract."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Read this chart with me — which is most? Now, how many more red cars than blue?"
**Do:** child names the tallest bar; subtracts two bars; tutor scribes the calculation.
**Ask:** "Dogs = 6, cats = 4 — how many more dogs?" → *listen for:* "2."

### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Dogs = 6, cats = 4 — how many more dogs than cats?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 2 — probe: *"Which two bars did you compare?"*
- ❌ **If "10" (added):** read 'more' as 'altogether' → **fix:** "'How many *more*' means the gap — subtract: 6 − 4."
- ❌ **If reads the wrong bar:** → **fix:** "Put your finger on the *top* of each bar and read across to the number."

### ⏱ 17–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child answers most/least/how-many-more from two block-diagram cards, then builds a small block diagram from a tally on squared paper. Once secure, interleave a 'total of two groups' question.
**Watch for:** adding instead of subtracting for 'more'; misreading bar heights.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — 'more' vs 'altogether', or misreading; 3) line the two bars up and count the gap; 4) one hint: "more = the difference"; 5) re-ask with 5 and 3; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What does 'how many more' tell us to do?" → *listen for:* "subtract / find the difference."
**Process praise:** "You read the tops of the bars carefully and chose subtraction — clear data thinking."
**Revisit later:** data reading returns in Unit 8.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** point to each bar's top and read across; pre-drawn axis.
- **Stretch (challenge):** build a block diagram from a tally; answer a two-step question ("how many more, and the total").

**Extension (if time allows):** tally real cars passing for two minutes, then chart them.
**Assessment evidence:** child reads most/least and finds 'how many more' by subtraction from a block diagram.

## Lesson 23 — Properties of 2-D and 3-D shapes

### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP

**Extension (if time allows):** shape hunt — find a cube, cuboid, sphere and cylinder at home and state their faces.

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)

**Show / Do:** fold the square to show matching halves (and show 4 lines); try the scalene triangle to show no match.

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)

**Ask:** "Does a square have a line of symmetry?" → *listen for:* "yes — in fact 4."

### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Does a square have a line of symmetry? Prove it by folding."
- ✅ **Expected:** yes (4) — probe: *"How did the fold prove it?"*

### ⏱ 17–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child tests several shapes by folding, marking the true lines of symmetry, then makes a **paint-fold butterfly** (paint one side, fold, press, open). Once secure, interleave: "Draw all the lines of symmetry on a rectangle vs a square."

> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — assuming vs testing; 3) fold together and check overlap; 4) one hint: "Do the halves land on top?"; 5) re-test the square; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "How do we *know* a line is a line of symmetry?" → *listen for:* "fold on it — both halves match."
**Process praise:** "You tested by folding instead of guessing — that's how mathematicians check symmetry."
**Revisit later:** symmetry returns in the Unit 7 review.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** fold to test — only a true line makes halves match (KB fix for "any line"); start with the square.
- **Stretch (challenge):** draw all lines of symmetry on a rectangle and a square and compare the counts.

**Extension (if time allows):** design a symmetrical butterfly or mask with matching halves.
**Assessment evidence:** child identifies lines of symmetry by folding and rejects non-matching lines.

**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** change by counting up (L16); reading scales (L18); telling time past/to (L19–20).
**Key vocabulary:** **total**, **change**, **compare**, **length**, **time**, **quarter past/to**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **pick the right tool for the context** (coins for money, clock for time, ruler for length) and read each carefully — the same estimate–solve–check habit applies everywhere.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Today's a whole pretend trip out: you'll shop, check the clock for the bus, and measure something. Can you handle the money *and* the time like a grown-up?"
**Show:** the shop, clock and ruler laid out.  **Do:** hand over a coin purse.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "How do we find change — count up or back?" → *listen for:* "count up from the price."

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "I'll model the trip. Shopping: a snack 30p and a drink 25p — total 55p; I pay £1, count up 55→60→100 = 45p change. The bus leaves at **quarter past 3** — I set the clock, long hand on 3. I check the ribbon is 20 cm long, ruler end on 0. Different tools, same careful reading."
**Show / Do:** total two items, find change with coins; set the clock to quarter past 3; measure a ribbon.
**Emphasize:** "Match the **tool** to the job, and read it carefully."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together: two snacks at 30p and 25p — total, then change from £1."
**Do:** child totals 55p, counts up to find 45p change with coins.
**Ask:** "Total and change from £1?" → *listen for:* "55p; 45p change."

### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Two snacks cost 30p and 25p; you pay £1 — total and change?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 55p; 45p — probe: *"Show the count-up for the change."*
- ❌ **If totals wrong over the ten:** → **fix:** "30 + 20 = 50, then the 5 → 55."
- ❌ **If finds change by guessing:** → **fix:** "Count up from 55: 60, 70, 80, 90, 100 — how much?"

### ⏱ 17–21 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child works three scenario cards — a money problem, a 'set the clock for the bus' (quarter past 3 / twenty to 4), and a 'compare two lengths'. Once secure, interleave a card combining two measures.
**Watch for:** wrong tool for the context; clock past/to slips; ruler not on zero.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — context/tool or reading; 3) hand the matching tool and model one step; 4) one hint; 5) re-ask a one-context version; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 21–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Which everyday job today did you feel most confident with — money, time, or measuring?" → *listen for:* reflection naming a context.
**Process praise:** "You switched tools for each job and read every one carefully — that's exactly real-life maths."
**Revisit later:** all strands meet in the Lesson 28 challenge.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** coins and a clock to hand; one context per card.
- **Stretch (challenge):** a problem combining two measures; a two-step money problem.

**Extension (if time allows):** plan a pretend day out with times and a budget.
**Assessment evidence:** child solves money (total + change), time (set/read past & to) and length problems accurately.

## Lesson 28 — Year 2 maths challenge & review
**Maths · Year 2 · Unit 8 (Year-end consolidation)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can show what I have mastered across the whole year."

**You will need:** six station cards (place value, two-digit sum, a times-table fact, fraction of an amount, read a clock, read a chart), manipulatives at each, answer cards for self-checking, a "Year 2 Maths Champion" badge/sticker.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the whole year — every unit.
**Key vocabulary:** (all year) **place value, regroup, times table, fraction, change, scale, time, data**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** a **celebration of mastery** — the child works independently, **self-checks**, and reflects on strengths. Low-stakes, success-rich; this is a confidence builder, not a test.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Welcome to the Year 2 Maths Champion Challenge! Six stations, one lap, and a badge at the finish. You've learned *so* much this year — today you show it off. Ready, champion?"
**Show:** the six stations laid out in a circuit; the badge on display.  **Do:** walk the lap quickly to name each station.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Which station looks like your favourite kind of maths?" → *listen for:* a confident choice.

### ⏱ 3–6 min · EXPLAIN (I do — model the routine)
> **Say:** "At each station: read the card, solve it, then flip the **answer card** to check yourself. If it matches — tick and move on. If not — try again or take a smaller step. Watch me do the place-value station: 76 is 7 tens and 6 ones… check… yes!"
**Show / Do:** model one station with the self-check routine.
**Emphasize:** "**Check yourself** at every station — being your own marker is a champion skill."

### ⏱ 6–9 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's do the times-table station together — then you're solo for the rest."
**Do:** child solves a ×5 fact, self-checks with the answer card.
**Ask:** "5 bags of 5 — and self-check: how many?" → *listen for:* "25 — checked, correct."

### ⏱ 9–13 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "At the fraction station: what is ¾ of 12?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 9 — probe: *"How did you self-check?"*
- ❌ **If "8" (subtracted):** → **fix:** "Share 12 into 4 plates, count three — and check the card."
- ❌ **If skips the self-check:** → **fix:** "Flip the answer card — does yours match? That's the champion habit."

### ⏱ 13–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child completes the **full circuit** independently — place value (partition 76), a two-digit sum (e.g. 48 + 37), a ×5 fact, ¾ of 12, set/read quarter past 3, read 'most' from a bar — self-checking each. Once round, they redo any they got wrong with a smaller step.
**Watch for:** which strands are secure vs shaky (note for handover); honest self-checking.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice) at any station:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why*; 3) hand the station's manipulative; 4) one hint; 5) re-ask an easier version of that station; 6) "Mistakes show us what to keep practising — you're still a champion."

### ⏱ 20–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Which station did you find easiest, and which would you like more practice on? Tell me one thing you're proud of this year." → *listen for:* reflection + a genuine pride point.
**Process praise:** "You checked your own work at every station and kept going when one was tricky — that's a true mathematician." *(Award the Champion badge.)*
**Revisit later:** carry the named shaky strand into Year 3 as an early warm-up; celebrate the secure ones.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** choose the stations they feel ready for first; manipulatives at every station.
- **Stretch (challenge):** design a bonus question at one station for the tutor to solve; beat-the-clock the circuit.

**Extension (if time allows):** create a seventh "design-your-own" station and challenge the tutor.
**Assessment evidence:** child completes a mixed end-of-year circuit, self-checks, and reflects on strengths and next steps — evidence across all eight units.

> **End-of-unit 8 check:** (1) 5 bags of 5 → 25. (2) Total 30p + 25p and change from £1 → 55p; 45p. (3) ¾ of 12 → 9. (4) Set quarter past 3. (5) Read "most" from a bar chart.
