## Lesson 1 — Living, dead, or never alive
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Living things & habitats)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can sort things into living, dead and never alive and give a reason."

**You will need:** a real or pictured **butterfly**; a **dead leaf** (a real fallen one is best); a **pebble** or small rock; a small **potted plant** (or cress/grass); a **teddy** or plastic toy; **three sorting hoops** or three labelled paper plates ("living", "dead", "never alive"); a small **seashell** (for the stretch); optional: a wooden block + a picture of a tree.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Year 1 — can tell plants and animals apart from non-living objects in an everyday way; knows animals and plants are alive.
**Key vocabulary:** **living** (it does, or could, grow, feed and have babies); **dead** (it was once alive but isn't now); **never alive** (it was never living at all); **life process** (a job all living things do — move, grow, feed, make babies).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** The single **test** that decides every object: *"Does it, or did it ever, grow, feed and make babies?"* The trap to pre-empt is "it moves, so it's alive" — a car moves and is never alive. Repeat the line: **"Moving isn't enough — living things also grow, feed and have babies."** Three groups, not two: *dead* (once alive) is its own group, separate from *never alive*.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "I've got a little puzzle for you. Look — here is a wooden block, and here is a picture of a great big tree. The block came from a tree, so they're both wood. But here's my question… is the tree alive? And is the block of wood alive? Are they the *same*?"

**Show:** the wooden block beside the tree picture.  **Do:** hold one in each hand, look genuinely puzzled, let the child wonder.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "In Year 1 you learned about plants and animals. Tell me one thing that is *alive* and one thing that is *not* alive." → *listen for:* a plant/animal as alive; a rock/toy as not alive.
*(If the prerequisite is shaky: point around the room — "Is that chair alive? Is that spider alive? How do you know?" and accept their everyday reasons.)*

### ⏱ 4–9 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore first)
> **Say:** "Before I tell you the secret, let's be detectives. I'll put things in front of you and you *really look*. Don't say a group yet — just tell me what each thing *does* or *did*."

**Do:** lay out the **butterfly**, **dead leaf**, **pebble**, **plant**, **teddy** in a row. Hand the child each one (or the picture) to handle.
**Ask:** "This butterfly — does it move? Does it eat? Did it have a mummy butterfly?" → *listen for:* yes, yes, yes. "This pebble — has it ever grown? Has it ever eaten? Could it have a baby pebble?" → *listen for:* no, no, no. "This leaf — it's not moving now… but where did it come from?" → *listen for:* a tree / it was on a plant.

### ⏱ 9–15 min · EXPLAIN (I do — now name it)
> **Say:** "You found it! Everything in the whole world goes into **three** groups. **Living** things grow, feed, move, and can have babies — like the butterfly and the plant. **Dead** things were *once alive* but aren't any more — like this leaf; it grew on a living tree, but now it's brown and dry. And **never alive** things were *never* living — like this pebble; it never grew or ate or had babies."

**Show / Do:** place each object into its hoop as you name the group. Say the test slowly while pointing to the test words on the board: **"Does it, or did it ever, grow, feed and make babies?"**
**Emphasize:** "If yes → it's **living** or **dead**. If it never could → **never alive**. And remember our trick: **moving isn't enough**. A car moves, but it never grows, eats or has babies — so a car is *never alive*."

### ⏱ 15–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Here's a tricky one. A **leaf on the ground** that fell from a tree — is it living, dead, or never alive?"
- ✅ **Expected:** *Dead* — "it was once alive (part of a living tree) but it isn't growing now." Then probe: *"How do you know it isn't *never* alive?"* (because it grew on a living tree).
- ❌ **If "never alive":** the child sees it lying still and lifeless and assumes it was never living. *Why:* they're judging by "it's not moving now." → **fix:** "Where was this leaf *before* it fell? Was that tree alive? So the leaf grew on something living — that means it was once alive too. Which group is for things that *were* alive?"
- ❌ **If "living":** the child thinks anything that came from a plant is still alive. *Why:* confusing where it came from with being alive *now*. → **fix:** "Is this leaf growing right now? Is it green and drinking water, or brown and dry? If it has stopped growing and feeding, it isn't living any more — but it *was*. What do we call that?"

### ⏱ 20–26 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Give the child a small pile of mixed things (or picture cards) — e.g. teddy, plant, pebble, seashell, a sock, a sunflower, a feather, a worm picture. They sort each into the three hoops and must say **one reason** out loud: "It's never alive because it never grew or ate."
**Watch for:** the *dead vs never-alive* slip (treating a feather or shell as never-alive). Catch it: "Did this come off a living bird?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the group; 2) name *why* — "I think the tricky part is telling *dead* from *never alive*"; 3) drop back to the concrete — put the real object in their hands; 4) give ONE hint: "Run our test — *did it ever grow, feed or have babies?*"; 5) re-ask the smaller question first ("Was it ever part of a living thing — yes or no?"), then build back to the three-way sort; 6) keep it safe — "Mistakes show us what to practise. Let's take a smaller step together."

### ⏱ 26–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the teacher now — tell me the three groups, and give me one example of each." → *listen for:* living (butterfly/plant), dead (leaf/shell), never alive (pebble/toy).
**Process praise:** "You used our test every single time — *did it grow, feed and have babies?* — instead of just guessing. That's exactly how a scientist sorts things."
**Revisit later:** Bring back the "is a car alive?" question as a 1-minute retrieval starter at the top of Lesson 2, and again before Unit 2.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** sort only **living vs never alive** first (the clearest contrast), with real objects in hand; add the "dead" group once those two are secure.
- **Stretch (challenge):** give the **seashell** — "This is tricky. Living, dead or never alive? Tell me *why*." (Dead/once-living: it was the home of a living sea creature.) Ask "would it always be in that group?"

**Extension (if time allows):** Go on a "three-group hunt" around the room or garden — find one real living thing, one dead thing, one never-alive thing, and draw them in three boxes with a reason under each.
**Assessment evidence:** Child sorts at least 3 mixed objects correctly into living/dead/never-alive **and gives a reason** for each; correctly places the fallen leaf as *dead*; states the test ("did it ever grow, feed and have babies?").

## Lesson 2 — The seven life processes (a child's list)
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Living things & habitats)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name things all living things do."

**You will need:** **picture cards** showing each life process (a child running = move; a child measured tall = grow; an animal eating + a plant by a window = feed/grow to light; animals with babies = make babies; optional cards for breathe, sense, get rid of waste); **mixed object cards** (worm, sunflower, you/child, car, rock, dog); space to move and act out.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 1 — living things grow, feed, move and have babies; the "did it ever grow, feed, have babies?" test.
**Key vocabulary:** **life process** (a job every living thing does); **move**, **grow**, **feed** (eat or make food), **make babies** (have young); also **breathe**, **sense** (see/hear/feel).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** The child-sized core list — **move, grow, feed, make babies** — is what *all* living things share, including plants. Pre-empt "plants don't move/feed": a plant **grows toward the light** (that's moving slowly) and **makes its own food** in its leaves. Repeat: **"You, a worm and a sunflower all do these same secret jobs."**

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "I have a riddle. What do *you*… and a wriggly **worm**… and a tall **sunflower**… all secretly have in common? You look so different! But you all do the same secret jobs every day. Let's find them."

**Show:** the three pictures — child, worm, sunflower — side by side.  **Do:** point to each, look mysterious.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From last time — name two things living things do." → *listen for:* any two of grow, feed, move, have babies.
*(If shaky: "Do you grow? Do you eat? Did you have a mummy? Those count!")*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore first)
> **Say:** "Let's *be* each living thing and find the secret jobs with our bodies. Stand up!"

**Do:** Act out together, one card at a time:
- **Move:** "Run on the spot — you can MOVE. Now be a sunflower turning slowly to the sun." (arms turn)
- **Grow:** "Crouch down tiny like a seed… now grow up TALL. You GROW. The worm grows too."
- **Feed:** "Pretend to munch a snack — you FEED. The plant feeds by drinking sunlight in its leaves."
- **Make babies:** "Animals have babies — a worm makes baby worms, a sunflower makes seeds that grow into new sunflowers."
**Ask:** "Which job did we just do with our turning arms?" → *listen for:* move.

### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name the list)
> **Say:** "Here's the secret. The jobs every living thing does are called **life processes**. Our four big ones are: **MOVE, GROW, FEED, MAKE BABIES.** Say them with me." (Tap each card.) "Grown-up scientists add three more — living things **breathe**, they **sense** the world, and they get rid of waste — but our four are the heart of it."

**Show / Do:** line up the four cards left to right; point and chant once more.
**Emphasize:** "Even a plant does all four — it grows, it moves slowly toward the light, it makes its own food, and it makes seeds. So a worm, a sunflower and YOU are all living, because you all do these jobs."

### ⏱ 14–19 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name **two** things ALL living things do. And then tell me — does a **car** do them?"
- ✅ **Expected:** any two of *move, grow, feed, make babies*; and "no — a car doesn't grow, feed or have babies." Probe: *"A car moves — so why isn't it alive?"*
- ❌ **If "a car is alive because it moves":** the child uses movement alone as the test. *Why:* movement is the most obvious sign of life. → **fix:** "Moving isn't enough. Does a car *grow* bigger? Does it *eat* food? Does it have *baby cars*? If it can't do those, it isn't living."
- ❌ **If "plants don't feed / don't move":** the child thinks only animals do the jobs. *Why:* a plant's actions are slow and quiet. → **fix:** "Watch a sunflower over a day — it slowly turns to face the sun. And a leaf is like a little kitchen making food from sunlight. So plants *do* move and feed — just slowly."

### ⏱ 19–23 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Spread the **mixed object cards** (worm, sunflower, child, car, rock, dog). Child sorts them into "does the life processes = LIVING" and "doesn't = not living", and names at least one process for each living one ("the dog *feeds* and *grows*").
**Watch for:** the child shelving the sunflower as "not living". Catch it with the turning-to-light reminder.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name why — "the tricky one is whether plants count"; 3) go concrete — look at the real potted plant from Lesson 1; 4) ONE hint: "Run the four jobs — grow? feed? move? babies (seeds)?"; 5) re-ask just one job at a time, then rebuild; 6) keep it safe — "Good thinking. Let's take a smaller step."

### ⏱ 23–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "You be the scientist: tell me the four jobs every living thing does." → *listen for:* move, grow, feed, make babies.
**Process praise:** "You didn't just guess — you tested each thing against our four jobs, and you remembered that plants do them too. That's careful scientist thinking."
**Revisit later:** Use "name two life processes" as a retrieval warm-up before Lessons 6–8 (plants) and Lesson 9 (animals).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match **one** picture card to **one** life process ("which card shows *feeding*?"), with the word said aloud.
- **Stretch (challenge):** "Explain how a *plant* moves." (It slowly grows and turns toward the light.) "Does a rock do *any* of the four jobs?"

**Extension (if time allows):** Invent four actions — one for each life process — and teach them to a family member as a "living things dance".
**Assessment evidence:** Child names at least two (ideally four) life processes unprompted and correctly explains why a car is *not* alive despite moving.

## Lesson 3 — What is a habitat?
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Living things & habitats)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can say what a habitat is and how an animal is suited to it."

**You will need:** **animal cards** (polar bear, fish, camel, frog/owl for woodland); **habitat scene cards** (Arctic ice, ocean/water, hot desert, woodland); something furry to touch (a fluffy glove or toy) to feel "thick fur"; optional pet photo (goldfish tank / hamster cage).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 2 — living things need to feed (and they need water and a safe place too).
**Key vocabulary:** **habitat** (the home where a living thing gets what it needs — food, water, shelter); **suited** (its body fits the place — thick fur for cold, gills for water); **shelter** (a safe place); **survive** (stay alive).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A habitat **gives food, water and shelter**, and every animal is **suited** to its home. Pre-empt "any animal could live anywhere if it tried" — a polar bear would *overheat* in a desert. Repeat the rule: **"Living things live where they get what they need, and their bodies are suited to it."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Imagine a big fluffy **polar bear**… plonked in the middle of a boiling hot **desert**. Sun beating down, sand everywhere. How do you think that polar bear would feel? Why would it be so miserable?"

**Show:** polar bear card next to the desert scene.  **Do:** mime being too hot — fanning, panting. Let the child touch the fluffy glove: "Imagine wearing this thick coat in the hot sun!"
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Living things need to feed. What *else* do they need to stay alive?" → *listen for:* water, a safe place, air.
*(If shaky: "What do *you* need at home — food in the kitchen, water from the tap, a warm bed? Animals need those too.")*

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore first)
> **Say:** "Let's be matchmakers. I have animals, and I have homes. Help me find each animal's *perfect* home — the place where it gets food, water and a safe spot to live."

**Do:** lay out the four habitat scenes; hand the child the animal cards one at a time.
**Ask:** "Where should the **fish** go? Why can't it live on the ice?" → *listen for:* in the water; it breathes/swims in water. "Where does the **camel** belong?" → *listen for:* the hot desert. "What does the polar bear's home give it that the desert can't?" → *listen for:* cold air that suits its thick fur; the sea to hunt.

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name it)
> **Say:** "You matched them perfectly. The home of a living thing is called its **habitat**. A habitat gives an animal three big things: **food, water, and shelter** — a safe place to live and raise babies. And here's the clever part: each animal is **suited** to its habitat. That means its body *fits* the place. The polar bear has thick fur for the cold. The fish has gills to breathe underwater. The camel can go a long time without water in the dry desert."

**Show / Do:** point to each animal's special body part as you say it (fur, gills, camel's hump).
**Emphasize:** "So our rule is: **living things live where they get what they need — and their bodies are *suited* to it.** A polar bear in the desert would overheat and have nothing right to eat."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Why does a **polar bear** live in the cold Arctic and **not** in the hot desert?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "Its thick fur keeps it warm and suits the cold; in the desert it would overheat (and have no seals to hunt)." Probe: *"What does the Arctic give the polar bear that the desert can't?"*
- ❌ **If "any animal could live anywhere if it tried":** the child doesn't yet link body to home. *Why:* they imagine animals are flexible like people with coats. → **fix:** "Could the polar bear take its thick fur *off* in the desert? No — it's stuck wearing its warm coat. Would it be too hot or too cold? So is the desert the right home for it?"
- ❌ **If "because it likes the cold":** a feeling, not a reason about being suited. *Why:* "likes" is the child's everyday explanation. → **fix:** "Let's say *why* its body fits. What does it have that keeps it warm in the cold? (Thick fur.) That's what *suited* means — its body matches its home."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child re-sorts all animals to habitats independently, then for **one** animal says a full sentence: "The fish lives in water because it has gills to breathe and it can swim to find food."
**Watch for:** correct match but no "suited" reason — push for the body part that fits.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the match; 2) name why — "I think we need to look at the animal's *body*"; 3) go concrete — feel the fluffy glove for "fur = warm"; 4) ONE hint: "What special thing does this animal have on its body, and what is that good for?"; 5) re-ask the smaller question ("Is it built for hot or cold? wet or dry?"), then rebuild; 6) keep it safe — "Mistakes help us learn. Smaller step."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me in your own words: what is a **habitat**? And pick one animal — how is its body **suited** to its home?" → *listen for:* the home that gives food/water/shelter; a body part that fits the place.
**Process praise:** "You didn't just say *where* — you explained *why its body fits*. That's a real scientist's reason."
**Revisit later:** Carry the word "suited" into Lesson 4 (why woodlice fit under a log) and the food-chain habitats in Lesson 5.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match just **two** obvious pairs (polar bear–Arctic, fish–water) with the body clue named for them.
- **Stretch (challenge):** "Choose your favourite wild animal and design its perfect habitat — what food, water and shelter does it need, and what part of its body suits that home?"

**Extension (if time allows):** Draw a goldfish's habitat (the tank) and label what it gives the fish: water to breathe, food, plants for shelter.
**Assessment evidence:** Child defines habitat as the home that provides food/water/shelter, correctly matches animals to habitats, and explains how *at least one* animal is suited to its home using a body feature.

## Lesson 4 — Microhabitats (lift a log)
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Living things & habitats)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can find a microhabitat and say why minibeasts live there."

**You will need:** an **outdoor space** with a log, large stone or plant pot to lift (or a damp shady patch); a **magnifier**; a **"found it" recording sheet** (boxes to draw/tally what's under the log); optional clipboard; an adult to lift the log safely. **Safety:** an adult lifts, the child watches; replace everything gently; wash hands afterwards.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 3 — a habitat gives food, water and shelter; animals live where they get what they need.
**Key vocabulary:** **microhabitat** (a *tiny* habitat inside a bigger one); **damp** (a bit wet); **minibeast** (a small creature — woodlouse, worm, beetle, snail); **shelter** (safe, hidden place).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A **microhabitat** is a real, full home even though it's tiny — under a log it is **damp, dark and safe**, which is exactly what woodlice and worms need. Pre-empt "it's too small to be a real habitat" — size doesn't matter; it gives food, water and shelter. Working-scientifically: **observe, gather, and return the habitat exactly as you found it.**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Come close — I want to tell you a secret. Right under that log there is a *whole hidden world* of tiny creatures, living their lives where we never look. Shall we lift it up *very gently* and peek inside their world?"

**Show:** the log/stone you'll lift.  **Do:** crouch down, whisper, build the wonder.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Every creature needs a habitat that gives it three things — can you remember them?" → *listen for:* food, water, shelter.
*(If shaky: "Food, water, and a safe place — let's see if the under-the-log world has all three.")*

### ⏱ 4–9 min · EXPLAIN-lite (set up the investigation)
> **Say:** "Here are our scientist rules. **One:** the grown-up lifts the log, you watch. **Two:** we *look*, we don't grab. **Three:** when we're done we put the log back *exactly* as it was, because it's somebody's home. **Four:** we wash our hands after. Ready, scientist?"

**Show / Do:** hand the child the magnifier and recording sheet; demonstrate looking, not touching.
**Emphasize:** "We are *visitors* in their home. Look carefully and we'll draw what we find."

### ⏱ 9–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore/observe outdoors)
> **Say:** "Lifting now… slowly… look! What can you see? How does it feel under here — is it dry and sunny, or damp and dark? Count how many little creatures you can spot."

**Do:** adult lifts; child observes with the magnifier and records (draw/tally each minibeast — woodlice, worms, beetles, maybe a snail or centipede). Then gently replace the log.
**Ask:** "Is it damp or dry under there? Light or dark?" → *listen for:* damp, dark. "How many woodlice did you count?" → *listen for:* a number from their tally. "Why do you think they like it under here and not out on the hot path?"

### ⏱ 20–26 min · EXPLAIN (I do — now name it)
> **Say:** "What you just found has a special name: a **microhabitat** — 'micro' means tiny. It's a *little* habitat hiding inside a bigger one. The garden is the big habitat; under the log is the tiny one. And even though it's small, it's a *real, full home* — it gives the woodlice everything they need: it's **damp** so they don't dry out, it's **dark** so birds can't easily see them, and there's rotting wood to **feed** on."

**Show / Do:** point to the recording sheet — "Here's the proof: real creatures, really living there."
**Emphasize:** "Tiny doesn't mean not real. Under that stone is a complete home — food, water and shelter — just woodlouse-sized."

### ⏱ 26–31 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name a **microhabitat** where you might find **woodlice**. And tell me — would a woodlouse be happy on a hot, sunny path?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "Under a log or stone (any damp, dark, hidden spot)"; "No — the path is dry and bright, the woodlouse would dry out and be seen." Probe: *"What does the under-the-log home give it that the path can't?"*
- ❌ **If "a microhabitat isn't a real habitat — it's too small":** "habitat" sounds big to the child. *Why:* size confusion. → **fix:** "Did we find real, living woodlice under there? Did it have food, water and a safe place? If it gives a creature everything it needs, it *is* a real habitat — just a tiny one."
- ❌ **If "they live anywhere":** missing the link between creature and damp/dark. *Why:* they haven't connected the conditions to the animal. → **fix:** "Was it damp and dark under the log? What would happen to a soft little woodlouse out in the hot, dry sun? So why do they choose the damp, dark spot?"

### ⏱ 31–35 min · PRACTICE + RECAP
**Do (you do):** Child finishes their recording sheet and writes/says one sentence: "Woodlice live under the log because it is damp, dark and safe."
**Watch for:** a child who names the creatures but not *why* they're there — prompt for damp/dark/safe.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name why — "I think we need the *reason* they live there"; 3) go concrete — re-feel/look: "Is it damp? Is it dark?"; 4) ONE hint: "A woodlouse likes places that are d… and d… (damp, dark)"; 5) re-ask smaller ("Damp or dry? Dark or bright?"), then rebuild the sentence; 6) keep it safe — "Great looking. Let's build it up bit by bit."

> **Say (recap):** "Tell me what a **microhabitat** is, and why the woodlice chose it." → *listen for:* a tiny habitat; damp, dark and safe with food.
**Process praise:** "You observed so carefully with your magnifier, you counted your creatures, and you put the log back kindly. That's exactly how a real field scientist works."
**Revisit later:** Bring "microhabitat" back when you build pond/garden food chains in Lesson 5, and in the Unit 1 review.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** spot and name just **one** minibeast and say "it's damp and dark under here".
- **Stretch (challenge):** "Explain why a woodlouse wouldn't survive on a hot, sunny path." (It would dry out and be eaten — it needs damp, dark shelter.)

**Extension (if time allows):** Hunt for a *second* microhabitat (under a plant pot, in a hedge, in a crack in a wall) and compare: which had more creatures, and why?
**Assessment evidence:** Child names a microhabitat (under log/stone), records what was found, and explains *why* minibeasts live there (damp, dark, safe — provides food/water/shelter).

## Lesson 5 — Food chains
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Living things & habitats)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can build a food chain with the plant first and arrows the right way."

**You will need:** **food-chain picture cards** (grass, rabbit, fox; also lettuce, caterpillar, bird; for stretch: pondweed, tadpole, fish, heron); large **arrow cards** (printed arrows on card); a box of cereal or a picture of breakfast cereal; space on a table/floor to lay out a chain.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Year 1 — animals eat plants or other animals (herbivore/carnivore); from Lesson 3 — animals get food in their habitat.
**Key vocabulary:** **food chain** (a picture of who eats whom); **arrow** (means "is eaten by" — points to the eater); **plant** (makes its own food from sunlight — comes first); **predator** (the eater), **prey** (the eaten).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Two ideas that *must* land: **(1) a food chain starts with a plant** (only plants make their own food from sunlight), and **(2) the arrow means "is eaten by" and points TO the eater.** Pre-empt "the arrow points to what the animal eats" and "chains can start with any animal." Repeat: **"The arrow points the way the food goes — to whoever does the eating."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Let's trace your **breakfast** back like a detective. You ate cereal, right? Cereal is made from a grain — and a grain is a tiny seed from a kind of **grass**! So your breakfast started… as a *plant*. Almost all food chains start with a plant. Let's find out why, and how we draw them."

**Show:** the cereal box.  **Do:** point from cereal → "a grass plant in a field".
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Do animals make their own food, or do they have to eat plants or other animals?" → *listen for:* they eat plants/other animals; they can't make their own.
*(If shaky: "Can a rabbit grow its own carrots inside itself? No — it has to eat them. But a plant *can* make its own food. Hold that thought.")*

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore/build first)
> **Say:** "Here are three cards: **grass**, a **rabbit**, a **fox**. Let's figure out who eats who. Who eats the grass?"

**Do:** lay grass, rabbit, fox on the table; let the child arrange the order of who-eats-whom.
**Ask:** "Who eats the grass?" → *listen for:* the rabbit. "Who eats the rabbit?" → *listen for:* the fox. "So which one should go FIRST — the one that makes its own food?" → *listen for:* the grass (the plant).

### ⏱ 10–15 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name it + the arrow rule)
> **Say:** "You built it! This is a **food chain** — a picture of who eats whom. It starts with the **plant**, the grass, because only plants can make their own food from sunlight; the plant feeds the whole chain. Now the clever bit — the **arrows**. An arrow does NOT mean 'eats'. An arrow means **'is eaten by'**, and it points TO the one doing the eating — the way the food travels."

**Show / Do:** place an arrow grass→rabbit, then rabbit→fox. Read it aloud, sweeping a finger along: "Grass *is eaten by* the rabbit; the rabbit *is eaten by* the fox."
**Emphasize:** "Plant first. Arrow points to the eater — the way the food goes. Say it: *'is eaten by'.*"

### ⏱ 15–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Point to the arrow between grass and rabbit. **What does that arrow mean?**"
- ✅ **Expected:** "'Is eaten by' — the grass is eaten by the rabbit; it points to the eater / the way the food goes." Probe: *"So which way does the arrow point — to the food or to the eater?"* (the eater).
- ❌ **If "it means 'eats'" / points to what the animal eats:** the most common error. *Why:* children naturally read arrows as "eats". → **fix:** "Let's read it the right way: the *grass* doesn't eat the rabbit, does it? Try 'is eaten by' — *grass is eaten by the rabbit*. Does that make sense? So the arrow points to whoever does the eating."
- ❌ **If the chain starts with an animal (e.g. fox first):** "chains can start with any animal." *Why:* the child picks a favourite. → **fix:** "Who can make their *own* food from sunlight — the fox, or the grass? Only the plant can. So who has to go first to feed everyone?"

### ⏱ 20–24 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Give a fresh, shuffled set (lettuce, caterpillar, bird) plus arrows. Child builds it solo — plant first, arrows pointing to the eater — and reads it aloud using "is eaten by". Then, if secure, interleave the four-card pond chain (pondweed → tadpole → fish → heron).
**Watch for:** arrows drawn the wrong way, or an animal placed first.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name why — "I think the arrows are the tricky part"; 3) go concrete — act it out: *be* the lettuce, child eats (mimes) as the caterpillar, then becomes the bird; 4) ONE hint: "Read it as 'is eaten by' — does it sound right?"; 5) re-ask smaller ("Who makes their own food? They go first."), then rebuild; 6) keep it safe — "Tricky one! Let's take it step by step."

### ⏱ 24–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Two scientist facts before we finish: What goes **first** in a food chain? And what does the **arrow** mean?" → *listen for:* a plant; "is eaten by" (points to the eater).
**Process praise:** "You read every arrow as 'is eaten by' instead of guessing — and you always put the plant first. That's exactly right."
**Revisit later:** Use "what does the arrow mean?" as a quick retrieval before the Unit 1 end check and again in Lesson 15.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** order a **three-card** chain with the **plant already placed** first; the child just adds the two animals and the arrows.
- **Stretch (challenge):** build the **four-step pond chain** (pondweed → tadpole → fish → heron) and read every arrow correctly as "is eaten by".

**Extension (if time allows):** Make your *own* food chain from animals in the garden or a favourite habitat, draw it with arrows the right way, and label the predator and prey.
**Assessment evidence:** Child builds a 3-card chain with the **plant first** and **arrows pointing to the eater**, and states that the arrow means "is eaten by".

---

**End-of-unit 1 check (oral/quick task):** Sort three things into living/dead/never-alive with a reason; name two life processes; say what a habitat is and how one animal is suited to it; name a microhabitat; build a food chain with the plant first and the arrows the right way.

## Lesson 6 — Seeds and bulbs
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Plants)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can say new plants grow from seeds and bulbs and name examples."

**You will need:** **assorted seeds** (a broad/runner bean, a sunflower seed, an apple pip, a few cress/poppy seeds); **bulbs** (an onion, a daffodil or tulip bulb, garlic); a **soaked bean** (soak a broad bean overnight) you can split open to show the baby plant; a magnifier; two sorting trays/hoops labelled "seed" and "bulb". **Safety:** remind not to eat seeds/bulbs; wash hands after handling onion/daffodil bulbs (daffodil bulbs are mildly irritant).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Year 1 — the parts of a plant (root, stem, leaf, flower) and that plants are living.
**Key vocabulary:** **seed** (has a tiny baby plant and a food store inside); **bulb** (a round underground store a plant grows from, like an onion); **food store** (the seed's "packed lunch").
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** New plants grow from **two** things — **seeds AND bulbs**. The wonder to land: inside a seed is **a tiny baby plant plus its own packed lunch** (food store). Show the split soaked bean. Don't worry about *what they need* yet (that's Lesson 7) — today is just "where new plants come from" and sorting seed vs bulb.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Hold out your hand. I'm putting one tiny **seed** in it. It's so small… but here's the amazing secret — inside this little seed is a *whole plant* waiting to grow, AND its very own packed lunch to help it start! Shall we crack one open and see the baby plant hiding inside?"

**Show:** the tiny seed in the child's palm, then the soaked bean ready to split.  **Do:** build awe — "all of that, in *this* little thing."
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From Year 1 — name a part of a plant you know." → *listen for:* root, stem, leaf, flower.
*(If shaky: point to the potted plant — "Where are its roots? Its leaves?")*

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore first)
> **Say:** "Let's open the secret. Watch — I'm splitting this soaked bean right down the middle… look very closely with the magnifier. Can you see the tiny pale baby plant tucked inside? The rest is the food store — its packed lunch!"

**Do:** split the soaked bean; let the child find the little embryo (the tiny root-and-shoot) with the magnifier.
**Ask:** "What can you see inside? Which bit is the baby plant?" → *listen for:* the tiny pale plant part; the big part is food. "So where does a new bean plant come from?" → *listen for:* the seed.

### ⏱ 10–15 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name it)
> **Say:** "So new plants grow from **seeds** — like this bean, a sunflower seed, an apple pip. Inside every seed is a baby plant and a food store. But seeds aren't the *only* way! Some plants grow from **bulbs** — round, underground food stores. This **onion** is a bulb. So is a **daffodil** bulb. Plant a bulb and a plant grows up out of it, just like from a seed."

**Show / Do:** hold up a seed, then a bulb. "Seed — small, has the baby inside. Bulb — round, fat, grows a plant too."
**Emphasize:** "Two ways to start a new plant: **seeds and bulbs.** Both have food stored inside to help the new plant begin."

### ⏱ 15–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name a plant that grows from a **bulb**."
- ✅ **Expected:** daffodil, tulip, onion, garlic (any bulb plant). Probe: *"How is a bulb different from a seed?"* (bigger, round, underground store).
- ❌ **If they name a seed plant (e.g. "sunflower"):** mixing the two. *Why:* both make new plants, so they blur. → **fix:** "A sunflower grows from a little flat *seed* — show me. A bulb is the big round one, like this onion. Now — which round bulb did we look at? What plant grows from *it*?"
- ❌ **If "they all grow from seeds":** the child thinks seeds are the only route. *Why:* seeds are the common example. → **fix:** "Look at this onion — is it a little flat seed, or a big round store? It's a *bulb*, and a green plant grows right out of it. So plants grow from seeds *and*…?"

### ⏱ 20–24 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child sorts the mixed pile (bean, sunflower seed, apple pip, onion, daffodil bulb, garlic) into the **"seed"** and **"bulb"** trays, saying which is which and naming at least one plant for each.
**Watch for:** garlic/onion mistakenly called seeds (they're bulbs); naming the example plant.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name why — "the tricky part is telling seed from bulb"; 3) go concrete — hold one in each hand: "small and flat, or big and round?"; 4) ONE hint: "Bulbs are the fat round ones you'd find under the ground"; 5) re-ask smaller ("Is this one small-and-flat or big-and-round?"), then sort; 6) keep it safe — "Good sorting. Let's compare them side by side."

### ⏱ 24–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Quick — new plants grow from two things. What are they? Give me one example of each." → *listen for:* seeds (bean/sunflower) and bulbs (daffodil/onion).
**Process praise:** "You opened that bean so gently and found the real baby plant inside — careful observing, just like a scientist."
**Revisit later:** Keep a soaked bean to plant in Lesson 7; ask "where do new plants come from?" as a warm-up.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** sort seeds from bulbs **by sight** into two trays, no naming required at first.
- **Stretch (challenge):** name **two** seed plants and **two** bulb plants; explain what the food store inside a seed is for.

**Extension (if time allows):** Cut an onion in half (adult helps) and find the rings and the little growing point in the middle; draw the bulb and label "food store".
**Assessment evidence:** Child states new plants grow from seeds *and* bulbs, sorts examples correctly, and names at least one of each.

## Lesson 7 — What a seed needs to grow
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Plants)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name three things a seed needs to grow."

**You will need:** a **clear jar or clear plastic cup**; **broad/runner beans** (pre-soaked overnight grow faster); **kitchen paper** (to line the jar); a small **jug of water**; a sticky label + pen to name the bean; **picture cards** of possible needs (water, sun/light, a warm radiator, soil, sweets, music) for the support sort; a warm, light windowsill to keep the jar.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 6 — new plants grow from seeds, which have a baby plant and a food store inside.
**Key vocabulary:** **water**, **warmth** (a suitable temperature — not too cold), **light**; **germinate** (when a seed starts to grow — meet the word, use it fully in Lesson 8); **soil** (helps later, but isn't needed to *start* sprouting).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A seed needs **water, warmth and light** to grow. Pre-empt two misconceptions: **(1) "seeds need soil"** — many sprout on wet paper with NO soil (soil only adds support/minerals later); and **(2) "more water is better"** — too much *drowns* the seed; it needs *enough*, not flooding. Repeat: **"Water, warmth and light — and just *enough* water, not a flood."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Lots of people are *sure* a seed needs **soil** to grow — no soil, no plant. But I think we can grow a bean with NO soil at all… just on some wet paper! Shall we test it and find out who's right?"

**Show:** an empty clear jar and a piece of kitchen paper.  **Do:** look mischievous — "Let's prove it."
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Where does a new bean plant come from, and what's tucked inside the seed?" → *listen for:* a seed; a baby plant and a food store.
*(If shaky: remind them of the split bean from Lesson 6.)*

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore/discuss first)
> **Say:** "Before we plant, let's think like scientists. What might a seed need to wake up and grow? I'll show you some cards — you tell me 'yes, it needs that' or 'no, it doesn't'."

**Do:** hold up cards one at a time: **water**, **light/sun**, **warmth (radiator)**, then distractors **sweets**, **music**, and **soil**.
**Ask:** "Does a seed need water?" → *listen for:* yes. "Does it need sweets?" → *listen for:* no! "Does it need *soil* to start sprouting?" → *let them debate — this is the test we're about to run.*

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name the three needs)
> **Say:** "Here are the three things a seed really needs to grow: **WATER** to wake it up, **WARMTH** — a cosy temperature, not freezing cold — and **LIGHT** so it can grow strong and green. That's it: water, warmth, light. Soil is helpful *later* for holding the plant up and feeding it, but a seed can start sprouting with no soil at all — and we're about to prove it!"

**Show / Do:** count the three needs on your fingers — water, warmth, light. Then set up the jar: line it with damp (not soaking) kitchen paper, tuck a bean between the paper and the glass so it can be seen, add a little water at the bottom.
**Emphasize:** "Just *enough* water — damp, not a flood. Too much water and the seed would drown! And we put it on a warm, light windowsill."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name **three** things a seed needs to grow."
- ✅ **Expected:** water, warmth (a suitable temperature), light. Probe: *"What's the *soil* trick we just learned?"* (it's not needed to start sprouting).
- ❌ **If "soil" is one of the three:** the soil misconception. *Why:* we usually plant in soil. → **fix:** "Look at our jar — is there any soil in it? No! Just wet paper. If our bean grows with no soil, was soil one of the *needs*? What were our three?"
- ❌ **If "lots and lots of water" / "the more water the better":** the over-watering misconception. *Why:* water helps, so more must help more. → **fix:** "What happens to *you* if you're under water and can't breathe? A seed is the same — too much water drowns it. It needs *enough* — damp, not flooded. Is our paper soaking wet or just damp?"

### ⏱ 22–28 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child sets up their **own** bean jar (damp paper, bean against the glass, a little water), names their bean on the label, and tells you the three things they'll give it: water, warmth, light. Place the jar on the windowsill.
**Watch for:** jars flooded with water (catch the over-watering misconception live); bean placed where it can't be seen against the glass.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name why — "I think we've forgotten one of the three needs"; 3) go concrete — point at the windowsill: "What's shining on it? (Light.) Is it warm here? (Warmth.) What did we pour in? (Water.)"; 4) ONE hint: "Three needs: w… w… and l…"; 5) re-ask smaller ("Name just *one* thing a seed needs"), then build to three; 6) keep it safe — "You're nearly there. Let's count them on our fingers."

### ⏱ 28–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the three things your bean needs, and the one thing it does NOT need to start growing." → *listen for:* water, warmth, light; not soil (and not a flood of water).
**Process praise:** "You set up a real experiment and gave your bean *just enough* water, not a flood — that careful measuring is exactly what scientists do."
**Revisit later:** The jar *is* the start of Lesson 8 — check it daily. Ask "what are we giving our bean?" each morning.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** **pick the three needs** from the picture cards (water, warmth, light) rather than recalling them; an adult sets up the jar with the child watching.
- **Stretch (challenge):** "Explain why **too much** water is bad for a seed." (It drowns the seed/rots it — roots need some air.) Predict what would happen with no light.

**Extension (if time allows):** Set up a "no light" bean (one jar in a dark cupboard) to compare next week — predict what will happen to it. (This becomes a mini fair test.)
**Assessment evidence:** Child names the three needs (water, warmth, light), correctly says soil isn't needed to sprout, and sets up a bean jar with *damp* (not flooded) paper.

## Lesson 8 — Root first, then shoot (observe over time)
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Plants)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can say which grows first from a seed and draw the stages."

**You will need:** the **bean jar from Lesson 7** (now several days in, ideally showing a root and/or shoot); a **daily observation diary** (a booklet with a box per day) or paper folded into boxes; pencils/crayons; **stage cards** (1: bean; 2: root growing down; 3: shoot growing up; 4: leaves opening) for sequencing; a magnifier. (If the bean hasn't sprouted yet, have a back-up sprouted bean or clear photos of the stages.)
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 7 — a seed needs water, warmth and light; we set up the bean jar to watch.
**Key vocabulary:** **germinate** (a seed starts to grow / wakes up); **root** (grows DOWN first — drinks water, holds on); **shoot** (grows UP toward the light, then opens leaves); **observe over time** (look and record across days).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** The order: **root first (grows DOWN), then shoot (grows UP).** Name **germinate**. Pre-empt "the shoot/green bit comes first" — point at their own jar: the white root pushed down *before* the green shoot. Emphasize the *observing-over-time* skill: a scientist looks every day and **draws what they really see**, not what they expect.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Big question, scientist! When a bean wakes up and starts to grow — what do you think pokes out **first**? A **root** going *down* into the ground… or a **shoot** going *up* into the sky? Make your guess — then we'll look at YOUR bean and find the real answer!"

**Show:** the bean jar.  **Do:** ask the child to commit to a guess — "Do you think root first, or shoot first?" Don't say who's right yet.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What did we give our bean so it'd grow?" → *listen for:* water, warmth, light.

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do — observe first)
> **Say:** "Let's be observers. Pick up your jar very gently and look really closely with the magnifier. What can you see happening to your bean? Has anything cracked out of it? Which way is it pointing — down, or up?"

**Do:** the child examines their bean jar; you help them spot the white root and (if present) the green shoot.
**Ask:** "Which part came out first — and which way is it growing, down or up?" → *listen for:* the white root, growing **down**. "Is there a green bit yet? Which way is *that* going?" → *listen for:* the shoot, growing **up** (or "not yet").

### ⏱ 12–18 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name it)
> **Say:** "Your bean answered the question! The part that comes out **first** is the **root** — and it grows **DOWN**. Why down? It's looking for water to drink and it holds the plant steady. THEN, a day or two later, the **shoot** grows **UP**, reaching toward the light, and opens its first little leaves. When a seed starts growing like this, we say it **germinates**."

**Show / Do:** lay the four stage cards in order while you narrate: bean → root down → shoot up → leaves. Say "germinate" and have the child repeat it.
**Emphasize:** "**Root first, growing DOWN. Then shoot, growing UP.** Down for water, up for light."

### ⏱ 18–23 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which grows **first** from a seed — the **root** or the **shoot**?"
- ✅ **Expected:** the **root** (it grows down first). Probe: *"Why does the root go down?"* (for water / to hold on).
- ❌ **If "the shoot / the green bit first":** the child pictures the leafy plant we usually see. *Why:* above-ground growth is what we normally notice. → **fix:** "Look at your own bean right now — what colour and which way is the *first* bit that came out? Is it the white root pointing down, or a green shoot? What does that tell us came first?"
- ❌ **If "they grow at the same time":** hasn't observed the order. *Why:* not watched closely day to day. → **fix:** "Look back at your diary — on the first day, was there a shoot? Or just the root starting? Which one showed up *before* the other?"

### ⏱ 23–28 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child draws **today's** stage of their bean in the diary box (date it), labelling "root" (and "shoot" if it's there), then puts the four stage cards in the correct order independently.
**Watch for:** drawing a "perfect plant" from imagination instead of what's actually in the jar — redirect to *real* observation. Watch the card order: bean → root → shoot → leaves.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the order; 2) name why — "I think we're drawing what we *expect*, not what we *see*"; 3) go concrete — "Put your eyes right up to the jar. What's actually there *today*?"; 4) ONE hint: "Roots go down to drink; shoots go up to the light"; 5) re-ask smaller ("Which card has *just* the bean? That's number 1…"), then build the sequence; 6) keep it safe — "Real scientists draw exactly what they see. Let's look again."

### ⏱ 28–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the order: what grows first, which way, and what comes next? And what's our special word for a seed starting to grow?" → *listen for:* root first, down; then shoot, up; **germinate**.
**Process praise:** "You watched your bean across the whole week and drew exactly what you saw each day — that's *observing over time*, a real scientist's superpower."
**Revisit later:** Keep the diary going to leaf stage; revisit "root first / germinate" in the Unit 2 review and when comparing the dark-cupboard bean (from the Lesson 7 extension).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** order just **two** stage pictures (bean → root) before adding shoot and leaves; label using a word bank ("root", "shoot").
- **Stretch (challenge):** "Explain *why* the root goes down and the shoot goes up." (Root: water + anchorage; shoot: light to make food.) Compare with the dark-cupboard bean — what's different and why?

**Extension (if time allows):** Measure the root and shoot with a strip of paper or ruler; record the number in the diary and predict tomorrow's length.
**Assessment evidence:** Child states the root grows first (and downward), uses "germinate", correctly orders the germination stages, and keeps a dated observation drawing.

---

**End-of-unit 2 check (oral/quick task):** Name two things that grow from seeds and two from bulbs; name three things a seed needs; say the root grows down first; order the germination stages.

## Lesson 9 — Baby animals grow up
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Animals, including humans)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can match a baby animal to its adult and say it grows into it."

**You will need:** **baby/adult animal picture cards** — pairs: lamb–sheep, calf–cow, chick–hen, puppy–dog, kitten–cat, foal–horse; for stretch: joey–kangaroo, cygnet–swan, caterpillar–butterfly (optional, advanced); a "memory/matching game" layout space; optional photo of the child as a baby vs now.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Year 1 — animals have babies; from Lesson 2 — "make babies" is a life process.
**Key vocabulary:** **offspring / young** (an animal's babies); **adult** (the grown-up animal); **grows into** (the baby turns into the adult over time).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Young animals **grow into** adults that look like their parents — a puppy *is* a baby dog. Pre-empt the misconception "a baby animal is a different kind of animal from the adult." Use the child's own baby photo as the clincher: *you* were a baby and grew into *you* — same person, just grown. Repeat: **"It's the same animal — it just grows up."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Look at this teeny tiny **puppy** — so small and wobbly. Here's my big question: what does this little puppy turn into when it grows up? And is it a *different* animal… or the *same* one, just bigger?"

**Show:** the puppy card (and, if you have it, the child's own baby photo).  **Do:** hold the puppy card small and low, then "grow" your hands big.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "One of our life processes was 'make babies'. So animals have…?" → *listen for:* babies / young.
*(If shaky: "Do dogs have puppies? Do cats have kittens? Yes — those babies are their young.")*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore/match first)
> **Say:** "Let's play matchmaker. Here are baby animals, and here are grown-up animals all mixed up. Help me find each baby's mummy or daddy — the grown-up it will grow INTO."

**Do:** spread the cards; child matches baby→adult pairs (lamb–sheep, calf–cow, chick–hen, puppy–dog).
**Ask:** "What does this **lamb** grow into?" → *listen for:* a sheep. "How can you tell they go together?" → *listen for:* they look alike / same kind of animal.

### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name it)
> **Say:** "You matched them brilliantly! An animal's babies are called its **young**, or its **offspring**. And here's the big idea: the young **grow into** adults that look like their parents. A **puppy** isn't a different animal from a dog — a puppy *is* a baby dog! It grows up into a grown dog. A lamb grows into a sheep. A chick grows into a hen."

**Show / Do:** if you have it, reveal the child's baby photo: "Look — this baby grew into YOU. Same person — just grown up. Animals are the same."
**Emphasize:** "A baby animal isn't a different kind of animal. **It's the same animal — it just grows up.**"

### ⏱ 17–23 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What is a baby **sheep** called, and what does it **grow into**?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "A **lamb**; it grows into a **sheep**." Probe: *"Is the lamb a different animal from the sheep, or the same one growing up?"* (the same).
- ❌ **If "a lamb is a different animal from a sheep":** the core misconception. *Why:* the baby has a different *name*, so it feels like a different creature. → **fix:** "It has a baby name, like *you* were called a baby — but were you a different *person*? The lamb just *grows* into a sheep. Same animal, bigger."
- ❌ **If can't name the baby ("a baby sheep"):** vocabulary gap, not a misconception. *Why:* hasn't learned the special name yet. → **fix:** "Let's find the card — the fluffy little one next to the sheep. Its special name is *lamb*. Say it with me — lamb grows into sheep."

### ⏱ 23–28 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Play a quick **matching game** (cards face down, find the baby–adult pairs). For each pair found, the child says the sentence: "A kitten grows into a cat."
**Watch for:** a child matching by colour/size only and missing the "grows into / same animal" idea — prompt the sentence.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the pair; 2) name why — "I think we're matching by *looks* but forgetting they're the *same animal*"; 3) go concrete — put the baby card *on top of* the adult card: "tiny one, grown one — same kind?"; 4) ONE hint: "What sound does it make / what kind of animal is it — baby and grown-up?"; 5) re-ask smaller ("Is a puppy a baby *dog* or a baby cat?"), then rebuild; 6) keep it safe — "Good thinking. Let's match one easy pair first."

### ⏱ 28–30 min · RECAP

**Process praise:** "You remembered that the puppy and the dog are the *same* animal growing up — that's the tricky part, and you nailed it."
**Revisit later:** Bring back "a puppy is a baby dog" before Lesson 10, and use baby-name surprises (joey, cygnet) as fun retrieval.

**Differentiation**

- **Stretch (challenge):** add a **kangaroo (joey)** or **swan (cygnet)** and explain that even though the names sound very different, the young still grows into that adult.

**Extension (if time allows):** Find out and draw three surprising baby-animal names (joey, cygnet, gosling, kit) with their grown-ups.
**Assessment evidence:** Child matches at least three baby–adult pairs and states that the young "grows into" the adult (the same animal), correcting the "different animal" idea.

## Lesson 10 — What every animal needs
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Animals, including humans)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name the things all animals need to stay alive."

**You will need:** **pet-care picture cards** (bowl of food, bowl of water, fresh air/window, a bed/kennel/cage = shelter) **plus distractors** (a toy, a TV, sweets); a **chosen animal card** (e.g. hamster, goldfish, dog); paper for a quick "day in the life" drawing.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 3 — a habitat gives food, water and shelter; from Lesson 9 — animals (including us) grow.
**Key vocabulary:** **needs** (things you must have to stay alive); **food**, **water**, **air** (to breathe), **shelter** (a safe place to live).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Every animal — a fish, a hamster, **and you** — needs the same **four basics: food, water, air, shelter.** Distinguish *needs* (must have to live) from *wants* (nice to have, like toys). Repeat: **"Food, water, air, shelter — every animal, including us."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Riddle time! What does a little **goldfish**, a fluffy **hamster**, AND *you* — all need, every single day, or you couldn't stay alive? Three very different creatures… but you all need the *same* important things. Let's find them!"

**Show:** the goldfish, hamster and a "child" card together.  **Do:** point to each — "so different… but the same needs."
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "A habitat gives an animal three things — remember them?" → *listen for:* food, water, shelter.
*(If shaky: "What does your home give *you* — food in the kitchen, water from the tap, a safe warm place?")*

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore first)
> **Say:** "Let's plan a perfect day for a pet hamster. I'll show you cards — you decide: does the hamster *need* this to stay alive, or is it just a nice extra?"

**Do:** hold up cards: **food bowl**, **water bottle**, **fresh air**, **a cosy bed/cage (shelter)**, then distractors: **a toy**, **a TV**, **sweets**.
**Ask:** "Does the hamster *need* food to stay alive?" → *listen for:* yes. "Does it *need* a TV?" → *listen for:* no — that's just for fun! "What about air — does it need to breathe?" → *listen for:* yes.

### ⏱ 10–15 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name the four)
> **Say:** "Here they are — the **four** things EVERY animal needs to stay alive: **FOOD** to give it energy, **WATER** to drink, **AIR** to breathe, and **SHELTER** — a safe place to live and sleep. A toy is fun, but it's a *want*, not a *need*. Food, water, air, shelter — those are needs."

**Show / Do:** line up the four need-cards; sweep the distractors to one side. Count the four on your fingers.
**Emphasize:** "And here's the amazing part — a fish, a hamster, a dog, *and YOU* all need these same four. **Food, water, air, shelter — every animal, including us.**"

### ⏱ 15–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name **three** things every animal needs to stay alive."
- ✅ **Expected:** any three of food, water, air, shelter. Probe: *"Is a toy a need or a want? Why?"* (a want — fun, but you can live without it).
- ❌ **If they name a *want* (toy, treats, TV):** mixing wants with needs. *Why:* "need" is used loosely in everyday talk ("I need that toy!"). → **fix:** "Could the animal stay *alive* without a toy? Yes. Could it stay alive without *water*? No. A *need* is something you can't live without. Try again — what can't it live without?"
- ❌ **If they forget *air*:** air is invisible, so it's easy to miss. *Why:* you can't see or hold it. → **fix:** "Hold your breath for a second… you want to breathe, right? What were you breathing? Animals need *air* too — it's a need even though we can't see it."

### ⏱ 20–24 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child picks a **chosen animal card** and draws/lays out its perfect day, including all **four** needs (food, water, air, shelter), then says which are needs and which were just-for-fun extras.
**Watch for:** missing one of the four (usually air or shelter); adding wants as if they were needs.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name why — "I think one of the four needs is missing"; 3) go concrete — point to the need-cards still on the table; 4) ONE hint: "Two are things it eats and drinks; one it breathes; one keeps it safe"; 5) re-ask smaller ("What does it *drink*? What does it *breathe*?"), then build to four; 6) keep it safe — "Nearly there. Let's count them on our fingers together."

### ⏱ 24–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the vet — tell me the four things every animal needs to stay alive. And do *you* need them too?" → *listen for:* food, water, air, shelter; yes!
**Process praise:** "You worked out the difference between a *need* and a *want* — that's clever thinking, not just memorising."
**Revisit later:** Connect to Lesson 11 (staying healthy is *how* humans meet their needs well); quick "name the four needs" retrieval.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** **pick the needs** out of a mixed picture set (needs vs wants already laid out), naming each.
- **Stretch (challenge):** "What would happen to an animal if it was missing **one** need — say, no water?" Explain why each need matters.

**Extension (if time allows):** Design a "perfect home" poster for a chosen pet, labelling where it gets each of the four needs.
**Assessment evidence:** Child names at least three of the four needs (food, water, air, shelter), and distinguishes a need from a want with a reason.

## Lesson 11 — Staying healthy: food, exercise, hygiene
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Animals, including humans)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name ways humans stay healthy."

**You will need:** **food cards** (fruit, veg, bread/pasta, fish/chicken, milk, water, and some treats — cake, sweets, crisps); **activity cards** (running, swimming, cycling, dancing; also sitting all day watching TV); **hygiene cards** (washing hands, brushing teeth, having a bath); a **"stay healthy" plan sheet** (boxes for one food, one exercise, one hygiene habit); optional a balanced-plate outline.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 10 — animals (including us) need food and water; we are animals too.
**Key vocabulary:** **balanced / varied diet** (eating a bit of lots of different foods, with plenty of fruit, veg and water); **exercise** (moving your body to keep it strong); **hygiene** (keeping clean to stop **germs**); **germs** (tiny things that can make you ill).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Three healthy habits — a **balanced, varied diet**, **exercise**, and **hygiene**. The key misconception to fix: **"healthy means NEVER eating treats / zero sugar and fat."** Health comes from **variety and balance**, not from cutting things out completely — lots of fruit, veg and water, with treats *sometimes*. Repeat: **"Balance, not zero."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's a tricky one. Some people think the secret to being healthy is *never, ever* eating a single treat — no cake, no sweets, never! Do you think that's right? Let's investigate — I think the real secret is something different…"

**Show:** a cake card next to an apple card.  **Do:** look sceptical, invite debate.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "We learned all animals need food and water. Are *we* animals who need food and water too?" → *listen for:* yes!
*(If shaky: "We're animals too — we need food, water, air and shelter, just like a dog.")*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore/sort first)
> **Say:** "Let's sort. I'll give you foods and activities — put them where you think they belong: 'helps me stay healthy' or 'only sometimes / not so healthy'."

**Do:** child sorts **food cards** (fruit, veg, water → healthy; cake, sweets → sometimes) and **activity cards** (running, swimming → healthy; sitting all day → not so much), plus **hygiene cards**.
**Ask:** "Where does the apple go? Where do the sweets go?" → *listen for:* apple = healthy; sweets = sometimes. "Should we have sweets *never*, or *sometimes*?" → *let them think — this is the key idea.*

### ⏱ 11–18 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name the three habits + fix the myth)
> **Say:** "Here are the three big ways humans stay healthy. **One: a balanced, varied diet** — that means eating a *bit of lots of different things*, with plenty of fruit, veg and water. **Two: exercise** — moving your body to make it strong: running, swimming, playing. **Three: hygiene** — keeping clean: washing your hands and brushing your teeth to wash away **germs**, the tiny things that make us ill."

**Show / Do:** group the cards into the three habits. Then hold up the cake: "And here's the secret to that tricky question — being healthy does NOT mean *never* a treat. It means **balance**. Lots of good food, and a treat *sometimes* is fine."
**Emphasize:** "Say it with me: **balance, not zero.** A bit of everything, lots of fruit and veg and water."

### ⏱ 18–23 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name **two** things humans do to stay healthy."
- ✅ **Expected:** any two of: eat a balanced/varied diet, exercise, wash hands, brush teeth. Probe: *"Does staying healthy mean *never* having a treat?"* (no — balance, not zero).
- ❌ **If "healthy means no sugar/fat at all" / "never any treats":** the oversimplification. *Why:* "healthy" gets turned into "ban everything nice." → **fix:** "Is a *little* cake at a party going to make you unhealthy? No — it's about *balance*. Lots of good food most of the time, and a treat sometimes. What's our phrase? *Balance, not…?*"
- ❌ **If they name only food (forget exercise/hygiene):** narrow view of health. *Why:* food is the most obvious. → **fix:** "Eating well is one way — but how do we keep our bodies *strong*? (Exercise.) And how do we wash away germs? (Washing/brushing.) Can you name one of those too?"

### ⏱ 23–28 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child fills in a **"stay healthy" plan** for one day: one healthy **food**, one **exercise**, one **hygiene** habit — then explains why each helps. (Stretch: build a balanced plate.)
**Watch for:** a plan that bans all treats (re-teach balance); only-food plans (prompt for exercise + hygiene).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name why — "I think we need a *different kind* of healthy habit"; 3) go concrete — point to the three card-groups (food / move / clean); 4) ONE hint: "One you *eat*, one you *do* with your body, one keeps you *clean*"; 5) re-ask smaller ("Name one thing you *do* to keep your body strong"), then build the plan; 6) keep it safe — "Great start. Let's add one from each group."

### ⏱ 28–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me two ways to stay healthy — and finish our phrase: healthy is *balance, not…?*" → *listen for:* two habits; "…not zero / not never having treats."
**Process praise:** "You busted the myth that healthy means *never* a treat — you understood it's about *balance*. That's grown-up thinking."
**Revisit later:** Revisit "balance, not zero" and the three habits in the Unit 3 end check and the end-of-year health question.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** sort just **two** examples into healthy / less-healthy (apple vs sweets) and name one healthy habit.
- **Stretch (challenge):** plan a **balanced plate** (lots of fruit/veg, some protein and carbs, a little treat) and explain why it's balanced — not zero.

**Extension (if time allows):** Make a "Stay Healthy" mini-poster for the fridge with one food, one exercise and one hygiene tip to teach the family.
**Assessment evidence:** Child names at least two healthy habits across the three areas and explains that health is about **balance, not cutting things out completely**.

---

**End-of-unit 3 check (oral/quick task):** Match three baby animals to adults and say they grow into them; name three things every animal needs; name two ways humans stay healthy and explain "balance, not zero."

## Lesson 12 — Why this material for this job?
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 4 (Uses of everyday materials)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why an object is made of a chosen material."

**You will need:** real objects — a photo or pane of glass, a plastic raincoat, a metal spoon, a rubber band/tyre, a wooden block; property cards (waterproof, transparent, hard, bendy, strong); a "wrong material" prop (a paper umbrella, if you can make one).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** naming materials and their properties (waterproof, hard, bendy) from earlier in the unit / Year 1.
**Key vocabulary:** **material**, **property**, **waterproof**, **transparent**, **suitable** ("right for the job").
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** objects are made of a material because its **property suits the job** — the material is *chosen on purpose*. The reasoning move is "this material is ___, so it's good for ___."

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Why is a window made of glass and not wood? Picture it — a wooden window! What couldn't you do? Today we become material detectives and work out *why* things are made of what they're made of."
**Show:** the glass, then hold up the wooden block where a window would be.  **Do:** mime trying to see through the wood.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Tell me one material and something special about it." → *listen for:* a material + a property (e.g. "plastic — waterproof").

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore first)
> **Say:** "Let's investigate real objects. Pick up the raincoat — what's it made of, and what's special about plastic? Now the spoon — why metal? Tell me what you *notice* about each."
**Do:** child handles each object, describing the property (glass = see-through; plastic = keeps water out; metal = hard and easy to clean; rubber = bendy and grippy).
**Ask:** "What do you notice about *why* each one was chosen?" → *listen for:* "the material is good for that job."

### ⏱ 10–15 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name the idea)
> **Say:** "Here's the science: every object is made of a material **chosen because its property suits the job**. Glass is **transparent** → good for windows (you see through). Plastic is **waterproof** → good for raincoats (keeps rain out). The sentence is: 'This is made of ___ because ___ is ___.'"
**Show / Do:** match each object to its material on a card and add the reason aloud.
**Emphasize:** "Property → job. The material is picked on purpose."

### ⏱ 15–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Why is a raincoat made of plastic and not paper?"
- ✅ **Expected:** plastic is waterproof; paper soaks up water — probe: *"What would happen in the rain with paper?"*
- ❌ **If "because it's nice / strong" (wrong property):** named an unrelated property → **fix:** "What does a raincoat need to do? Keep water *out* — which material does that?"
- ❌ **If can't give a reason:** → **fix:** "Pour a drop on paper vs plastic — which stays dry? That tells you why."

### ⏱ 20–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child matches objects to their best material **with a reason** (spoon→metal because hard; boots→rubber because waterproof and bendy). Then the "what if it were the wrong material?" game (a glass spoon? a paper umbrella?). Once secure, interleave: "Why is a saucepan metal and not plastic?"
**Watch for:** giving a property that doesn't match the job; "because I like it" answers.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — property/job mismatch; 3) go concrete — test the property (drip water, try to bend it); 4) one hint: "What does this object need to *do*?"; 5) re-ask with raincoat; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Finish this for me: 'A window is made of glass because…'." → *listen for:* "glass is transparent / you can see through it."
**Process praise:** "You gave a *reason* every time — 'waterproof so it keeps rain out' — that's real scientific explaining."
**Revisit later:** leads into changing materials' shapes (next lesson) and the fair-test investigation.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match three object–material pairs with the property card beside each.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain why a saucepan is metal (and a handle sometimes plastic); find two objects at home and justify their materials.

**Extension (if time allows):** redesign a silly object made of the wrong material and say what would go wrong.
**Assessment evidence:** child explains why an object is made of its material, naming the matching property.

## Lesson 13 — Changing the shape of materials
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 4 (Uses of everyday materials)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can change a material's shape by squashing, bending, twisting or stretching."

**You will need:** playdough/clay, pipe cleaners, a sponge, rubber bands, a paperclip.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** materials keep their identity (a material is *what something is made of*).
**Key vocabulary:** **squash**, **bend**, **twist**, **stretch**, **shape**, **same material**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** you can change a material's **shape** by squashing, bending, twisting, stretching — but it's **still the same material**. Pre-empt "bending the paperclip turned it into a new material."

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Watch — I bend this paperclip into a wiggle. Quick question: have I turned it into a *new material*? Or is it still metal, just a new shape? Let's find out what we can change… and what we can't."
**Show:** a paperclip, then bend it.  **Do:** hold up the bent and a straight one side by side.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What material is the paperclip made of?" → *listen for:* "metal."

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "There are four ways to change a shape: **squash** (push it flatter — playdough), **bend** (curve it — pipe cleaner), **twist** (turn the ends opposite ways — sponge), **stretch** (pull it longer — rubber band). But — important — the paperclip is **still metal**. I changed its *shape*, not what it's *made of*."
**Show / Do:** demonstrate each of the four actions naming it; stress the material is unchanged.
**Emphasize:** "Shape changes; the **material stays the same**."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn — squash this playdough and tell me the action word. Now bend the pipe cleaner."
**Do:** child performs and names squash, bend, twist, stretch on the objects.
**Ask:** "When you squashed the playdough — is it still playdough?" → *listen for:* "yes, just a different shape."

### ⏱ 12–16 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name a material whose shape you can change by **squashing**."
- ✅ **Expected:** clay / playdough / sponge — probe: *"After squashing, what is it still made of?"*
- ❌ **If "it becomes a new material":** confusing shape with material → **fix (KB):** "Squash it and squash it back — still the same stuff, just a new shape."
- ❌ **If muddles the action words:** → **fix:** "Show me with your hands: squash = push flat; stretch = pull long."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child matches each action to a suitable material and predicts which spring back (rubber band, sponge) vs stay changed (playdough). Once secure, interleave: "Name a material for each of the four actions."
**Watch for:** thinking the material changed; mixing the four action words.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — shape vs material, or vocabulary; 3) do the action together and reverse it; 4) one hint: "Did the *stuff* change, or just the shape?"; 5) re-ask 'squash'; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the four ways to change a shape — and the big rule about the material." → *listen for:* "squash, bend, twist, stretch; it's still the same material."
**Process praise:** "You spotted that a bent paperclip is *still metal* — that's careful scientific thinking."
**Revisit later:** sets up choosing and testing materials in the investigation next lesson.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** do each action and name it as you go; two action words at a time.
- **Stretch (challenge):** name a material for each of the four actions and predict which return to shape.

**Extension (if time allows):** make a playdough model, then squash and rebuild it — same material, new shapes.
**Assessment evidence:** child changes shapes by squashing/bending/twisting/stretching and states the material is unchanged.

## Lesson 14 — Which material is best? (fair-test investigation)
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 4 (Uses of everyday materials)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can run a fair test to find the best material for a job and explain my result."

**You will need:** paper, cotton fabric, plastic (all same size), a small teddy, a dropper, a jug of water, a tray, paper towels for spills, a simple results sheet.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** waterproof vs absorbent; choosing a material for a job (Lesson 12).
**Key vocabulary:** **fair test**, **predict**, **same/change**, **observe**, **conclude**, **waterproof**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** a **fair test** keeps everything the **same except the one thing we change** (the material). The working-scientifically arc: **predict → fair test → observe → conclude**. This is a longer, hands-on investigation lesson — pace it.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Teddy needs an umbrella! Should we make it from paper, cotton or plastic? Which will keep him driest in the rain? Let's not just guess — let's *test* it, fairly, like real scientists."
**Show:** the teddy and the three materials.  **Do:** drip one drop on the back of your hand for drama.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Which material do you *predict* will win, and why?" → *listen for:* a prediction with a reason ("plastic — it's waterproof").

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXPLAIN (I do — set up the fair test)
> **Say:** "To be **fair**, I change only ONE thing — the material. Everything else stays the **same**: same amount of water, same height of dropper, same size cover, same teddy. If I poured more water on the paper, that wouldn't be fair, would it? Watch me set up: dropper this high, three drops each time."
**Show / Do:** lay out the three materials over the teddy/tray; demonstrate the controlled drip; write the prediction on the sheet.
**Emphasize:** "**Same everything, change only the material** — that's what makes it fair."

### ⏱ 12–24 min · EXAMPLE/PRACTICE (we do → you do — run the test)
> **Say:** "Let's test together, then you run the rest. Paper first — three drops from here. Watch teddy: wet or dry? Record it. Now you do cotton, then plastic, keeping it fair."
**Do:** child drips the same water from the same height on each material, observes whether teddy stays dry, and records wet/dry on the sheet.
**Ask (during):** "Are you keeping it fair — same water, same height?" → *listen for:* the child self-checking the controls.

### ⏱ 24–30 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which material won, and why?"
- ✅ **Expected:** plastic — it's waterproof, so it kept teddy dry — probe: *"How do you know it was a fair race?"*
- ❌ **If picks by guess, ignoring the data:** → **fix:** "Look at your results sheet — which one kept teddy driest?"
- ❌ **If can't say why it was fair:** → **fix:** "What did we keep the same for all three? Name two things."

### ⏱ 30–36 min · PRACTICE / consolidate (you do)
**Do:** Child states the conclusion in a full sentence ("Plastic is the best umbrella because it is waterproof and kept teddy dry") and explains **one** thing that made the test fair. Once secure, interleave: "If we tested again, what must stay the same?"
**Watch for:** unfair changes (more water on one); reading results rather than guessing.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — ignoring data or unfairness; 3) re-run one material together watching the controls; 4) one hint: "What does your sheet show?"; 5) re-ask 'paper vs plastic only'; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 36–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me what we did like real scientists today — start with 'First we predicted…'." → *listen for:* "predicted → tested fairly → observed → concluded."
**Process praise:** "You kept the water and height the *same* for every material — that's exactly what makes an experiment fair. Proper scientist work."
**Revisit later:** the fair-test idea is the heart of the Unit 5 wrap-up next lesson.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** test just two materials and say which let water through.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain what made the test fair and suggest a second test (which is strongest?).

**Extension (if time allows):** design teddy a raincoat from the winning material and label why it works.
**Assessment evidence:** child runs a fair test (controls the variables), reads the result, names the best material and explains the fairness.

> **End-of-unit 4 check:** Explain why one object is made of a chosen material; name the four ways to change a material's shape; run a simple fair test and say which material is best and why.

## Unit 5 — Working scientifically (year wrap-up)

**Essential question:** How do scientists compare, gather and record what they find?
**Key vocabulary:** observe, compare, fair test, predict, record, data, gather over time, conclude.

## Lesson 15 — Becoming a data gatherer (year review)
**Science · Year 2 · Unit 5 (Working scientifically)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a simple comparative test and say how to record the results."

**You will need:** two paper towels (or sponges) of different brands, water, a dropper, a recording chart, the year's earlier investigation evidence (bean diary, waterproof results), a "Head Scientist" badge.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the year's enquiries — observing over time (bean), fair testing (umbrella), sorting/hunting (habitats).
**Key vocabulary:** **fair test**, **predict**, **record**, **conclude**, **keep the same / change**, **data**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** a scientist **plans** before testing — decide what to **keep the same**, what to **change**, and **how to record**. This is a review that pulls the whole year's working-scientifically thread together.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Big question for a Head Scientist: which paper towel soaks up the most water? Two brands claim to be best — how would we find out *fairly*? Today you plan and run the whole test yourself."
**Show:** the two paper towels and the water.  **Do:** pin on the Head Scientist badge.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Think back — how did we make the teddy umbrella test fair?" → *listen for:* "kept everything the same except the material."

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do — model planning)
> **Say:** "A scientist **plans first**. I ask: what will I **change**? The towel. What must I **keep the same**? Same amount of water, same size of towel, same way of pouring. And how will I **record**? On a chart — wet/dry, or how many drops it holds. Then **predict, test, record, conclude**."
**Show / Do:** fill in the plan on the chart aloud — change / keep same / how to record.
**Emphasize:** "Plan before you test: change one thing, keep the rest the **same**, and **record** as you go."

### ⏱ 10–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Help me finish the plan — name one thing we must keep the same."
**Do:** child adds a control ("same size towel"), predicts which brand wins, and sets up the chart.
**Ask:** "To compare the two towels fairly, what must stay the same?" → *listen for:* "same amount of water, same size, same method."

### ⏱ 15–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "To compare two paper towels fairly, what must stay the same?"
- ✅ **Expected:** same amount of water, same size of towel, same way of testing — probe: *"What's the one thing we're allowed to change?"* (the towel).
- ❌ **If "make one bigger / use more water on one":** breaking fairness → **fix:** "If one gets more water, the race isn't fair. What must match?"
- ❌ **If unsure how to record:** → **fix:** "We need to remember the results — what could we draw? A chart of wet/dry or drops held."

### ⏱ 18–26 min · PRACTICE (you do — run it)
**Do:** Child runs the comparative test fairly, drips the same water on each towel, and **records** the result on the chart, then states a conclusion. Once secure, interleave: "How could we make the result even more sure?" (test more than once).
**Watch for:** uncontrolled changes; not recording; jumping to a conclusion before the data.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — fairness or recording; 3) run one towel together, filling the chart; 4) one hint: "Same water for both?"; 5) re-ask 'what to keep the same'; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 26–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the steps a scientist follows — start with 'First I plan…'." → *listen for:* "plan (change/keep same/record) → predict → test → record → conclude."
**Process praise:** "You planned what to keep the same *before* testing and wrote down your results — that's exactly how scientists work all year." *(Confirm Head Scientist badge.)*
**Revisit later:** carry these working-scientifically habits straight into Year 3 enquiries.

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** choose what to change/keep with a prompt card; record with a simple wet/dry tick.
- **Stretch (challenge):** design the whole test and the recording chart independently; suggest repeating for reliability.

**Extension (if time allows):** plan a different fair test (which soap makes most bubbles?) and its recording chart.
**Assessment evidence:** child plans a comparative test (names a control and the variable), records results, and states a conclusion in words.

> **End-of-unit 5 check:** Plan a simple comparative test (what to keep same, what to change); record results on a chart; state a conclusion in words.
