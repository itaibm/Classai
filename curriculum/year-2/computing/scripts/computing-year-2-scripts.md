# Year 2 Computing — Tutor Scripts (Lessons 1–18)

Complete tutor scripts for [Year 2 Computing](../computing-year-2.md), built to the [scripting guide](../../../SCRIPTING-GUIDE.md) and grounded in `knowledge-base/subjects/computing-life-skills/computing-ks1-ks2.md` and `online-safety.md`. Unplugged-first wherever it helps. Every beat ⏱ sums to the duration, with verbatim Say / Show / Do / Ask, a real check with wrong-answer fixes, the "if the child is stuck" protocol, differentiation and assessment evidence. Keep the online-safety unit **warm and reassuring** — never imply a child is to blame.

## Lesson 1 — Algorithms grow up: longer and clearer
**Computing · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Clearer algorithms)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a clear 5–6 step algorithm for an everyday task."
**You will need:** numbered instruction strips, picture-step cards, the tutor to "be the computer," soap/tap props optional.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 1 — "algorithm = precise ordered steps."
**Key vocabulary:** **algorithm**, **step**, **sequence**, **precise**, **order**.
**What to emphasize:** a computer needs **every tiny step, in order** — it can't guess. Pre-empt skipping "obvious" steps.
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Last year you wrote 3-step algorithms. But a computer needs *every* tiny step — miss one and it fails. Can you write the *whole* hand-washing algorithm with nothing left out?"
**Do:** mime washing hands but 'fail' because a step is missing (no water!).
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's an algorithm?" → *listen for:* "precise ordered steps / instructions."
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "An **algorithm** is precise steps in order. Watch me build hand-washing: tap on → wet hands → soap → rub → rinse → tap off → dry. Seven steps! If I skip 'soap', my hands aren't clean. A computer does *exactly* what I write — no more, no less."
**Do:** build the 7-step list on a numbered strip; show what breaks if a step is missing.
**Emphasize:** "**Every step, in order** — the computer can't fill gaps."
### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's write 'make cereal' together — what's step 1? Then exactly what next?"
**Do:** child lists 5–6 precise steps; the tutor 'runs' them literally to test.
**Ask:** "Why does a computer need *every* step, even obvious ones?" → *listen for:* "it can't think or guess — it only does what we tell it."
### ⏱ 16–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Why can't we skip the 'obvious' steps in an algorithm?"
- ✅ **Expected:** the computer can't guess — it does exactly what's written. — probe: *"What happens if a step's missing?"*
- ❌ **If skips steps:** assuming the computer fills in → **fix:** "I'll follow it *literally* — oops, you didn't say 'open the box'. Add it."
- ❌ **If steps out of order:** → **fix:** "Can you pour before you open? Put them in order."
### ⏱ 20–26 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child writes a 5–6 step algorithm for their own task on a numbered strip and tests it on the tutor, who follows it *exactly*. Once secure, interleave: spot and add a step the tutor deliberately left out of an algorithm.
**Watch for:** missing/obvious steps; wrong order.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't write it for them; 2) name *why* — missing step/order; 3) act it out literally together; 4) prompt: "what did I have to do *first*?"; 5) rebuild one step; 6) "Every tiny step counts."
### ⏱ 26–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Why must an algorithm have every step in order?" → *listen for:* "the computer only does exactly what we say."
**Process praise:** "Your steps were so precise I could follow them perfectly — that's algorithm thinking."
**Revisit later:** next we break a *big* job into small steps (decompose).
---
**Differentiation** — support: sequence pre-written step cards. / stretch: spot and add a missing step.
**Extension / if the child finishes early:** write an algorithm for the tutor to act out and 'debug'.
**Assessment evidence:** child writes a clear, complete, ordered 5–6 step algorithm.

## Lesson 2 — Decompose it: big job, small steps
**Computing · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Clearer algorithms)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can break a big task into smaller parts (decompose)."
**You will need:** "step tree" sheets, big-task cards, sticky notes.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** algorithms as ordered steps (L1).
**Key vocabulary:** **decompose**, **break down**, **part**, **task**, **step**.
**What to emphasize:** **decompose = break a big task into smaller parts**, then break each part into steps. Pre-empt tackling a giant task in one lump.
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "'Tidy your whole bedroom' is WAY too big for a robot to do in one go. How do we chop it into bites it can actually do?"
**Show:** a big-task card ('tidy your bedroom').
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does an algorithm need?" → *listen for:* "ordered precise steps."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "**Decompose** means break a big task into smaller parts. 'Tidy your bedroom' → part 1: books, part 2: toys, part 3: clothes. Then each part breaks into steps. Watch my **step tree**: big job at the top, parts below, little steps under each."
**Do:** draw a step tree decomposing one big task.
**Emphasize:** "**Decompose** — big job into parts, parts into steps."
### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Decompose 'make a sandwich' with me — what are the 3 big parts?"
**Do:** child splits a big task into 3–4 parts.
**Ask:** "What does it mean to *decompose* a task?" → *listen for:* "break a big task into smaller, easier steps."
### ⏱ 14–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What does 'decompose' mean, and why is it helpful?"
- ✅ **Expected:** break a big task into smaller parts; easier to do/program. — probe: *"What's one part of your big task?"*
- ❌ **If lists one long jumble:** not broken into parts → **fix:** "Group them — which steps belong to the same *part*?"
- ❌ **If parts too big still:** → **fix:** "Can a robot do that part in one step? If not, break it smaller."
### ⏱ 18–26 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child picks a big task and draws a step tree (big job → parts → small steps). Once secure, interleave: decompose two parts each into their own steps.
**Watch for:** parts still too big; no hierarchy.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't decompose for them; 2) name *why* — too big; 3) split one part together; 4) prompt: "what are the big chunks?"; 5) build one branch; 6) "Big job → parts → steps."
### ⏱ 26–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "How does decomposing make a big job easier?" → *listen for:* "lots of small easy steps instead of one giant one."
**Process praise:** "You turned a giant job into a tidy step tree — that's how programmers tackle big problems."
**Revisit later:** Unit 1 check; decomposing helps you plan programs later.
---
**Differentiation** — support: split one given task into 3 parts with prompts. / stretch: decompose two parts into steps.
**Extension / if the child finishes early:** decompose 'get ready for school' into a full step tree.
**Assessment evidence:** child decomposes a big task into smaller parts/steps.

> **End-of-unit check (Unit 1):** Child writes a clear 5–6 step algorithm with nothing missing and decomposes one big task into smaller steps.

## Lesson 3 — Bugs are normal (predict → run → spot → fix)
**Computing · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Become a debugger)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can say what a bug is and name the steps to debug it."
**You will need:** squared paper, a buggy "draw a square" instruction list (only 3 forward+turns), a "bug detective" magnifier prop.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** algorithms as exact steps (L1).
**Key vocabulary:** **bug**, **debug**, **predict**, **run**, **spot**, **fix**.
**What to emphasize:** bugs are **normal**; debug with **predict → run → spot → fix ONE step → run again**. Pre-empt scrapping everything.
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Even the best coders make bugs *every single day* — finding them is the fun part! Fun fact: a real moth once got stuck in a computer and caused the first 'bug'. Today we become bug detectives."
**Do:** hold up the magnifier.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does a computer do — exactly what we say, or what we mean?" → *listen for:* "exactly what we say."
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **bug** is a mistake in the instructions; **debugging** is finding and fixing it. The routine: **predict** what should happen → **run** it → **spot** where it went wrong → **fix one step** → **run again**. Watch: here's 'draw a square' but it only has 3 sides of instructions. I predict a square, run it on squared paper — only 3 sides! I spot the missing side, add one step, run again. Fixed!"
**Do:** demo the buggy square, find the missing side, fix one step.
**Emphasize:** "**Predict → run → spot → fix one step → run again.** Don't scrap it all."
### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Predict with me — what *should* this draw? Now let's run it and spot the bug."
**Do:** child predicts, 'runs' the buggy list, points to the bug.
**Ask:** "What is a bug, and what is debugging?" → *listen for:* "a mistake in the instructions; finding and fixing it."
### ⏱ 16–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name the debugging steps in order."
- ✅ **Expected:** predict → run → spot → fix one → run again. — probe: *"Why only fix *one* step?"*
- ❌ **If "start over / change everything":** → **fix:** "The rest works! Change just the broken step and run again."
- ❌ **If can't define bug:** → **fix:** "It's a *mistake in the instructions* — and debugging fixes it."
### ⏱ 20–26 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child debugs a buggy instruction list on squared paper using the routine. Once secure, interleave: explain why changing everything is a bad strategy.
**Watch for:** scrapping the whole thing; not predicting first.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't fix for them; 2) name *why* — no routine; 3) predict together first; 4) prompt: "where did it stop matching?"; 5) fix one step; 6) "Find the one bug — you keep what works."
### ⏱ 26–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Say the debugging routine." → *listen for:* "predict, run, spot, fix one, run again."
**Process praise:** "You hunted down the bug and fixed just one step — proper detective debugging."
**Revisit later:** next we debug a real floor robot.
---
**Differentiation** — support: bug is hinted ("look at the number of sides"). / stretch: explain why changing everything is bad.
**Extension / if the child finishes early:** plant a bug for the tutor to find.
**Assessment evidence:** child defines a bug and states/uses the debugging routine.

## Lesson 4 — Debug the floor robot
**Computing · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Become a debugger)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can find and fix one wrong step in a floor-robot program."
**You will need:** a Bee-Bot/floor robot, a gridded mat with a "star" target.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the debugging routine (L3).
**Key vocabulary:** **program**, **command**, **bug**, **debug**, **one step**.
**What to emphasize:** fix **only the broken step** — keep what already works. Pre-empt clearing the whole program.
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "My robot was supposed to reach the star, but — watch — it bumped the wall! Can you fix it *without* starting all over?"
**Do:** run the buggy program; the robot misses the star.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's our debugging routine?" → *listen for:* "predict, run, spot, fix one, run again."
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "I predict where it *should* go, run it, and watch where it **diverges** — goes wrong. There! It turned left instead of right. I change *just that one command* — not the whole program — and run again. The rest was fine, so I keep it."
**Do:** identify the one wrong command, change it, re-run to the star.
**Emphasize:** "Fix **only the broken command** — keep the rest."
### ⏱ 11–18 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Predict the path with me. Run… where did it go wrong? Which one command do we change?"
**Do:** child spots the divergence and names the single fix.
**Ask:** "Why fix only the broken step instead of clearing the whole program?" → *listen for:* "the rest works — it's faster and keeps what's right."
### ⏱ 18–24 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "The robot went wrong at one turn. Should you clear the whole program — why or why not?"
- ✅ **Expected:** no — fix just the wrong command; the rest works. — probe: *"How did you find the wrong one?"*
- ❌ **If clears everything:** → **fix:** "Most of it worked! Where exactly did it first go wrong? Change just that."
- ❌ **If can't locate the bug:** → **fix:** "Run it step by step — call out when it stops matching your prediction."
### ⏱ 24–31 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child debugs the robot's program to reach the star, then swaps roles and debugs a "broken" program the tutor wrote. Once secure, interleave: a longer program with the bug unmarked.
**Watch for:** restarting from scratch; guessing fixes.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't fix for them; 2) name *why* — locating the bug; 3) step through together; 4) prompt: "first wrong move?"; 5) change one; 6) "One step fixes it."
### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Why change only the broken command?" → *listen for:* "the rest works."
**Process praise:** "You found the one wrong turn and fixed it — and the robot hit the star. Brilliant debugging."
**Revisit later:** next we debug on screen — same routine.
---
**Differentiation** — support: bug in a 3-step program with a hint. / stretch: longer program, bug unmarked.
**Extension / if the child finishes early:** set a robot challenge with one bug for the tutor.
**Assessment evidence:** child finds and fixes one wrong step in a robot program.

## Lesson 5 — Debug on screen
**Computing · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Become a debugger)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can fix a block program so the sprite reaches its goal."
**You will need:** tablets/computers with a ScratchJr-style tool, pre-loaded buggy projects (sprite over/undershoots a target).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** debugging routine; one-step fixes (L3–4).
**Key vocabulary:** **block**, **sprite**, **run**, **goal**, **debug**.
**What to emphasize:** on-screen debugging uses the **same routine** as the robot — change one block, run again. Pre-empt randomly deleting blocks.
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "This cat keeps zooming *past* the fish! It works *almost* right. What ONE block should we change?"
**Do:** run the buggy project; the sprite overshoots.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Same routine as the robot — what is it?" → *listen for:* "predict, run, spot, fix one, run again."
### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "On screen it's the *same* as the robot. I predict, run, and the cat overshoots. The bug is the 'move' block is too big a number. I change **one block** — make it move fewer steps — and run again. Same routine, just on a screen."
**Do:** identify the wrong block, change it, re-run to land on the fish.
**Emphasize:** "Same routine — **change one block, run again.**"
### ⏱ 10–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Predict where the cat stops. Run… too far? Which block do we shrink?"
**Do:** child adjusts one block to land the sprite.
**Ask:** "Your cat went too far past the fish — what could you change?" → *listen for:* "shorten/fix the move block, then run again."
### ⏱ 15–19 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "The sprite overshoots. What's your one change, and what do you do next?"
- ✅ **Expected:** reduce/fix the move block; run again. — probe: *"Did you change just one thing?"*
- ❌ **If deletes lots of blocks:** → **fix:** "Most of it works — change just the block that's wrong, then run."
- ❌ **If guesses without predicting:** → **fix:** "Predict first: where *should* it stop? Then fix toward that."
### ⏱ 19–26 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child opens a buggy project, predicts, runs, fixes one block, runs again. Once secure, interleave: fix a bug that's a *wrong block*, not just a count.
**Watch for:** random deleting; not re-running.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't fix for them; 2) name *why* — locating/one-change; 3) predict together; 4) prompt: "too far or not far enough?"; 5) change one; 6) "One block, then run."
### ⏱ 26–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Is screen debugging different from robot debugging?" → *listen for:* "no — same routine."
**Process praise:** "You landed the cat on the fish with one neat change — same detective skill, on screen."
**Revisit later:** Unit 2 check; now you'll *build* your own sprite programs.
---
**Differentiation** — support: choose "add one" or "take one away." / stretch: fix a wrong block, not just a count.
**Extension / if the child finishes early:** make a buggy project for the tutor to fix.
**Assessment evidence:** child fixes one block so the sprite reaches its goal.

> **End-of-unit check (Unit 2):** Child states the predict→run→spot→fix→run routine and uses it to fix one bug unplugged, on the robot, and on screen.

## Lesson 6 — Plan it, then build it
**Computing · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Program a sprite)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a short program on paper and build it on screen."
**You will need:** tablets with a ScratchJr-style tool, paper block/arrow cards.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** sequences and the event/start block (Year 1); debugging (Unit 2).
**Key vocabulary:** **plan**, **program**, **block**, **sequence**, **event/start block**.
**What to emphasize:** **plan on paper before building** — fewer bugs to fix later. Pre-empt dragging blocks randomly.
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Real programmers *plan* before they build. Let's draw our program with cards on the table *before* we touch a single block on screen."
**Show:** the arrow/block cards.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What block makes a program start?" → *listen for:* "the green-flag / start / event block."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "My goal: the sprite walks across to the house. First I **plan** with cards: start block → move, move, move. Then I build the *same* sequence on screen, beginning with the start block. Plan first means I get it right before I build."
**Do:** lay a card plan, then build the matching sequence on screen.
**Emphasize:** "**Plan on paper, then build** the same on screen."
### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Plan the route with me using cards — start, then how many moves?"
**Do:** child lays a card plan and starts building it.
**Ask:** "Why plan before building?" → *listen for:* "to get the steps right first, less to fix later."
### ⏱ 14–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Why do programmers plan before building?"
- ✅ **Expected:** to get steps right first / fewer bugs. — probe: *"Does your screen program match your plan?"*
- ❌ **If builds with no plan:** → **fix:** "Lay the cards first — what's the order? Now copy it on screen."
- ❌ **If forgets the start block:** → **fix:** "How will it know when to begin? Add the start/event block first."
### ⏱ 18–26 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child plans with cards then builds the matching sequence on screen and runs it. Once secure, interleave: plan a route with a turn in it.
**Watch for:** mismatch plan vs build; missing start block.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't build for them; 2) name *why* — plan/build mismatch; 3) lay cards together; 4) prompt: "what's first on your plan?"; 5) build one block; 6) "Plan, then copy it."
### ⏱ 26–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What do programmers do before building?" → *listen for:* "plan it."
**Process praise:** "Your screen program matched your paper plan exactly — that's how pros work."
**Revisit later:** next, build a longer program that reaches a goal.
---
**Differentiation** — support: copy a given plan. / stretch: plan a route with a turn.
**Extension / if the child finishes early:** plan and build a two-step journey.
**Assessment evidence:** child plans on paper and builds the matching program on screen.

## Lesson 7 — Make the sprite reach the goal
**Computing · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Program a sprite)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can build and run a sequence that starts on an event and reaches a goal."
**You will need:** tablets with a ScratchJr-style tool, on-screen target objects.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** planning + building (L6); the start/event block.
**Key vocabulary:** **event/start block**, **sequence**, **goal**, **turn**, **run**.
**What to emphasize:** a program **begins on an event block** and runs a **sequence** to a **goal**; predict, run, adjust to land it.
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Can you guide your character all the way to the treasure — AND make it start the moment you tap the flag?"
**Do:** tap a flag; a sprite moves to a target.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does the start block do?" → *listen for:* "tells the program when to begin."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "I build a longer **sequence** — with at least one **turn** — that begins on the **event block** and reaches the target. I predict where it'll stop, run, and adjust the block counts to land it exactly. I can even add a second sprite that also starts on the flag for a whole scene."
**Do:** build a sequence with a turn, run, adjust to hit the target.
**Emphasize:** "Start on the **event**, sequence to the **goal**, adjust to land it."
### ⏱ 9–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Build the route to the treasure with me — start block first, then moves and a turn. Predict where it stops."
**Do:** child builds and runs, adjusting counts.
**Ask:** "What does the start/event block do?" → *listen for:* "tells the program when to begin running."
### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What makes your program *start*, and how did you get the sprite to land on the goal?"
- ✅ **Expected:** the event/start block; predicted and adjusted block counts. — probe: *"What did you change to land it?"*
- ❌ **If no start block:** → **fix:** "Add the event block — without it the program won't run on cue."
- ❌ **If overshoots/undershoots:** → **fix:** "Predict the stop, then add/remove one move and run again."
### ⏱ 22–31 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child builds a sequence (with a turn) from the event block to a target, predicting and adjusting. Once secure, interleave: two sprites, both triggered, reaching two goals.
**Watch for:** missing event block; not predicting.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't build for them; 2) name *why* — start block/counts; 3) build the straight part first; 4) prompt: "where should it stop?"; 5) adjust one block; 6) "Start block, then guide it home."
### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Two things every program needs to reach a goal?" → *listen for:* "a start/event block and the right sequence."
**Process praise:** "You tapped the flag and your scene sprang to life — real programming!"
**Revisit later:** next we make a working program even *better*.
---
**Differentiation** — support: straight route, no turn. / stretch: two sprites, two goals.
**Extension / if the child finishes early:** add a backdrop to your scene.
**Assessment evidence:** child builds and runs an event-triggered sequence that reaches a goal.

## Lesson 8 — Improve your program
**Computing · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Program a sprite)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can make my working program better in one clear way."
**You will need:** tablets with a ScratchJr-style tool (working programs from L7).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** building a working program (L7).
**Key vocabulary:** **improve**, **better**, **sound**, **background**, **make it work → make it better**.
**What to emphasize:** once it **works**, **make it better** in one clear, explained way. Pre-empt changing so much it breaks.
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Your program works — brilliant! But can we make it *better*? Add a sound? A smoother path? A surprise? Good programmers say: make it work, *then* make it better."
**Do:** add a 'pop' sound to a working program.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Does your program reach its goal already?" → *listen for:* "yes."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "I start from a *working* program and choose **one** improvement — a sound block, a background, a smoother route, a sprite that reacts. I make the change, run it, and explain *why* it's better. Just one improvement at a time so I don't break what works."
**Do:** add one improvement; run; explain why it's better.
**Emphasize:** "**Make it work → make it better** — one clear improvement."
### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Pick one improvement with me — a sound, or a background? Add it and run."
**Do:** child adds one improvement and runs it.
**Ask:** "Name one way you made it better and why." → *listen for:* a sensible improvement + reason.
### ⏱ 14–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Your program already works — what's one improvement, and why is it better?"
- ✅ **Expected:** a real improvement with a reason (e.g. "a sound makes it fun"; "fewer blocks = clearer"). — probe: *"Did it still work after?"*
- ❌ **If breaks it changing too much:** → **fix:** "Undo back to working, then make *one* small change."
- ❌ **If no reason:** → **fix:** "Why is that better for whoever plays it?"
### ⏱ 18–26 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child makes one explained improvement to a working program and runs it. Once secure, interleave: combine two improvements that work together.
**Watch for:** breaking the working version; change with no purpose.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't improve it for them; 2) name *why* — too much/no reason; 3) offer a two-option menu; 4) prompt: "what would make it more fun/clear?"; 5) add one; 6) "Keep it working — one good change."
### ⏱ 26–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What's the rule about working vs better?" → *listen for:* "make it work, then make it better."
**Process praise:** "You upgraded a working program *and* could say why it's better — that's a designer's mindset."
**Revisit later:** Unit 3 check; you'll improve digital content next, too.
---
**Differentiation** — support: choose an improvement from a menu of two. / stretch: combine two improvements.
**Extension / if the child finishes early:** show the tutor or family the before and after.
**Assessment evidence:** child improves a working program in one explained way.

> **End-of-unit check (Unit 3):** Child plans, builds and runs a sprite program that starts on an event and reaches a goal, then improves it in one explained way.

## Lesson 9 — Content for a purpose and an audience
**Computing · Year 2 · Unit 4 (Digital content & data)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan digital content by deciding its purpose and who it's for."
**You will need:** a planning sheet, examples of posters/cards.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** creating/saving a picture (Year 1).
**Key vocabulary:** **purpose** (what it's for), **audience** (who it's for), **plan**, **tool**.
**What to emphasize:** plan content by asking **purpose** and **audience** — they change how it looks. Pre-empt diving in with no plan.
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "A poster for a bake sale and a card for grandma both use a computer — but they look *completely* different. Why? Because they have different jobs and different readers."
**Show:** a poster and a card side by side.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What can a computer help us make?" → *listen for:* "pictures, cards, posters, writing."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Two planning questions: **purpose** — what is it *for*? **audience** — who will *see* it? A grandma card is for love (purpose) and for grandma (audience), so it's gentle and personal. > **Say:** "Order the plan with me — what's first? Why do we *keep* it instead of deleting?"
**Do:** child orders the cards and explains 'keep it'.
**Ask:** "Something online scares you — what's the first thing to do, and will you be in trouble?" → *listen for:* "stop, don't reply, keep it, tell a trusted adult; no — never my fault."
### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Say the worry plan in order — and are you ever in trouble for telling?"
- ✅ **Expected:** stop → don't reply → keep it → tell a trusted adult; never in trouble / never your fault. — probe: *"Why keep it, not delete?"*
- ❌ **If 'delete it':** → **fix:** "Keep it — it helps a grown-up understand and help you."
- ❌ **If 'keep it secret':** → **fix:** "Secrets that feel wrong are exactly what to *tell* — you won't be in trouble."
### ⏱ 17–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child names two or three trusted adults on the sheet and orders the worry-plan cards. Once secure, interleave: explain why we *keep* (don't delete) the worrying thing.
**Watch for:** deleting; fear of being blamed.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it gentle; 2) name *why* — order/reassurance; 3) order with picture cards together; 4) prompt: "first you…?"; 5) one step; 6) "It's never your fault — telling is brave and right."
### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Who are your trusted adults, and what's step one of the worry plan?" → *listen for:* named adults + "stop."
**Process praise:** "You know your helper team and the plan by heart — that's exactly the safety net we want."
**Revisit later:** the year celebration brings it all together.
---
**Differentiation** — support: order the plan with picture cards. / stretch: explain why we keep (don't delete) the worrying thing.
**Extension / if the child finishes early:** draw your 'helper team' of trusted adults.
**Assessment evidence:** child names trusted adults and recites the worry plan in order.

## Lesson 18 — Computing experts: a Year 2 celebration
**Computing · Year 2 · Unit 5 (review)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can show what I learned this year about algorithms, debugging, content, data and staying safe."
**You will need:** all year's props — algorithm strips, floor robot + mat, tablets, pictogram cards, safety sorting cards, certificates.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the whole year.
**Key vocabulary:** (all year) **algorithm, debug, program, data, private, age rating, worry plan**.
**What to emphasize:** a confidence-building **celebration carousel** — the child shows their skills, self-checks at stations, and shares a proud moment. Low-stakes and joyful.
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "You can now write algorithms, fix bugs, build programs, handle data *and* stay safe online. That's a LOT! Today we celebrate everything you've learned."
**Show:** the stations and a 'computing expert' certificate.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Which station looks like your favourite?" → *listen for:* a confident choice.
### ⏱ 3–7 min · EXPLAIN (I do — the carousel)
> **Say:** "Five stations: (1) write/sequence a longer algorithm, (2) debug a robot program, (3) build a quick sprite program, (4) read a pictogram (most/least/altogether), (5) sort private/share and age-right/older, name a trusted adult and the worry plan. At each, do the task and check yourself, then move on — I'll be your quizmaster!"
**Do:** demo one station and the self-check.
**Emphasize:** "Show your skill, **self-check**, share a proud moment."
### ⏱ 7–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's do the algorithm station together, then you're off round the carousel."
**Do:** child completes one station with the tutor.
**Ask:** "Name one thing a computer can't do without us." → *listen for:* "think/decide for itself — it only follows our instructions."
### ⏱ 12–16 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Pick a station and show me — then tell me one thing a computer can't do without us."
- ✅ **Expected:** completes a station; "it can't think/decide — it follows the algorithm." — probe: *"How did you check yourself?"*
- ❌ **If stuck on a station:** → **fix:** "Use the skill from that unit — e.g. predict→run→spot→fix for debugging."
- ❌ **If skips self-check:** → **fix:** "Check the answer card — does yours match?"
### ⏱ 16–31 min · PRACTICE (you do — the carousel)
**Do:** Child rotates the five stations, self-checking, with the tutor as quizmaster and game-partner. Once round, they redo a tricky one. Note which skills are secure for handover.
**Watch for:** which strands are secure vs shaky.
> **If the child is stuck at a station:** 1) don't do it for them; 2) name *why*; 3) recall that unit's routine; 4) one hint; 5) easier version; 6) "You're a computing expert — keep going."
### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me one thing you're proud of learning in computing this year." → *listen for:* a genuine pride point.
**Process praise:** "You wrote algorithms, fixed bugs, built programs, handled data *and* kept safe — a true Year 2 computing expert." *(Award the certificate.)*
**Revisit later:** Year 3 computing builds on all of this — Scratch, networks, and deeper data.
---
**Differentiation** — support: do the stations together with the tutor, picking just two. / stretch: 'teach' a favourite station back to the tutor, explaining it.
**Extension / if the child finishes early:** design a new station for the carousel.
**Assessment evidence:** child demonstrates algorithms, debugging, a sprite program, data reading and online-safety knowledge across the stations.

> **End-of-unit check (Unit 5):** Child explains kindness and privacy online (incl. passwords), uses age ratings to judge suitability, names two trusted adults and recites the worry plan.

## End-of-year mastery checks
A Year 2 child finishing this curriculum can: write a clear 5–6 step algorithm and decompose a big task; state and use the predict→run→spot→fix→run routine unplugged, on a robot and on screen; plan, build, run and improve a sprite program; create, save, reopen and improve text-plus-picture content for an audience; collect data into a tally and pictogram and answer most/least/altogether; choose the kind action online (incl. as a bystander); keep personal info and passwords private; use age ratings; and name trusted adults and the worry plan (never their fault).
