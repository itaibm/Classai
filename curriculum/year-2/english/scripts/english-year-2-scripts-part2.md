## Lesson 10 — Word-builders: -ful, -less, -ness, -ly, -ment
**English · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Spelling with suffixes)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can add a meaning-changing suffix to build a new word."

**You will need:** root cards (*care, help, kind, quick, enjoy, hope, pain, sad*); suffix cards (*-ful, -less, -ness, -ly, -ment*); whiteboard; a card showing "*-ful* = ONE *l*".
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** root + suffix; suffixes are endings added to roots (Lessons 5–9).
**Key vocabulary:** *suffix, -ful* (full of), *-less* (without), *-ness* (a "thing"/state), *-ly* (how / in this way), *-ment* (the result).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** These suffixes are **word-builders that change MEANING**, not just spelling. The headline pair: *-ful* (full of) vs *-less* (without) — opposite meanings from one root. The must-not-miss spelling rule: **-ful is spelled with ONE *l*** (even though the word *full* has two). For most, **just add the whole suffix to the whole root**; if the root ends in *y*, change *y→i* first (*happy → happiness*). Pre-empt *carefull* (two *l*s).

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Watch one little word make two opposite words! *Help* plus *-ful* means FULL of help — *helpful*. *Help* plus *-less* means WITHOUT any help — *helpless*. Same root, opposite meanings! These suffixes are word-builders — they change what a word means. Let's build some."

**Show:** *help* with *-ful* and *-less* tails.  **Do:** build *helpful* (thumbs up) and *helpless* (shrug) to act out the meanings.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Remind me — what's a suffix?" → *listen for:* an ending we add to a root word.
*(If shaky: "It's a tail on a word — like the *-ing* in *jumping*. Today's tails change the meaning.")*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Five word-builder suffixes. Watch me build from *care*. *Care* + *-ful* = *careful* — full of care. BUT careful! *-ful* has only ONE *l*, even though the word *full* has two. *Care* + *-less* = *careless* — without care. Now *kind* + *-ness* = *kindness* — *-ness* makes a 'thing' word, the state of being kind. *Quick* + *-ly* = *quickly* — *-ly* tells us HOW. *Enjoy* + *-ment* = *enjoyment* — *-ment* names the result. For all of these, I just add the whole suffix on. One special: if the root ends in *y*, change *y→i* first — *happy → happiness*."

**Show / Do:** build *careful* (point firmly to the single *l*), *careless, kindness, quickly, enjoyment*, writing each root then the suffix in another colour.
**Emphasize:** "*-ful* means full of — and it's spelled with just ONE *l*."

### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Build with me. Take *hope*. Add *-ful* — full of hope? And add *-less* — without hope?"

**Do:** child builds *hopeful, hopeless* (note: *-ful* and *-less* start with consonants, so the silent *e* stays). Then *pain* → *painful, painless*; *sad* → *sadness*.
**Ask:** "Add *-ful* to *care*. How many *l*s?" → *listen for:* *careful*, one *l*.

### ⏱ 14–16 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Add *-ful* to *care*."
- ✅ **Expected:** *careful* — one *l*. Then probe: *"How many *l*s — and why not two?"* (the suffix *-ful* loses an *l*).
- ❌ **If the child writes *carefull*:** copied the word *full* (two *l*s). → **fix:** show the "*-ful* = ONE *l*" card. "The word *full* has two, but the suffix *-ful* drops one. Just one *l*."
- ❌ **If the child changes the root unnecessarily (e.g. *carful*):** dropped a needed letter. → **fix:** "Keep the whole root *care*, then add *-ful*. The root doesn't change here."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child builds one new word with a chosen suffix AND uses it in a spoken/written sentence (e.g. "My dog is *playful*."). Encourage building a *-ful* and a *-less* pair from one root.
**Watch for:** *-full* with two *l*s; forgetting *y→i* if a *y*-ending root is chosen (*happy → happiness*); building a non-word — check it's real.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't build it for them; 2) name *why* — "let's check the suffix spelling / the meaning"; 3) step down — give a root and two suffix cards to match into real words; 4) ONE hint — "*-ful* means full of — say the meaning, then build"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (just build, no sentence); 6) keep it safe — "Word-building is inventing — let's make one real word together."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me what *-ful* and *-less* mean — and the spelling trap for *-ful*." → *listen for:* *-ful* = full of, *-less* = without; *-ful* has one *l*.
**Process praise:** "You built real words AND used one in a sentence — and you remembered *-ful* has just one *l*. That's a word-builder at work."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-Unit 2 check** — child spells *hopping, hoped, happier* AND builds *careful, kindness, happily*. Mastery = correct rule on 4 of 5; *-ful* with one *l*. Revisit these new words as vocabulary in Unit 4 sentences.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match roots to suffix cards to make real words (no spelling from scratch); start with the clear *-ful/-less* pair.
- **Stretch (challenge):** build a "thing" word AND a "how" word from *happy* (*happiness, happily*) — applying *y→i* — and explain each meaning.

**Extension (if time allows):** invent superhero words (*powerful, fearless*) and define each ("full of power", "without fear").
**Assessment evidence:** child builds at least two correct meaning-suffix words including one *-ful* with a single *l*, and uses one in a sentence.

## Lesson 11 — Apostrophes job 1: squashing words (contractions)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Apostrophes & homophones)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a contraction with the apostrophe where letters are missing."

**You will need:** word-pair cards (*do not, I am, it is, can not, she will, we are*); whiteboard; a red pen for crossing out dropped letters.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** capital letters and full stops; writing simple sentences (Year 1).
**Key vocabulary:** *apostrophe* ( ' ), *contraction* (two words squashed into one), *missing letters*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** The apostrophe's **Job 1**: when two words are squashed together, some letters drop out, and the apostrophe **stands exactly where the letters went missing** — like a "little gravestone marking the gap". The move: **find the missing letters → put the apostrophe there.** Pre-empt putting the apostrophe in the wrong place. Memorable image: gravestone for the lost letters.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "When we talk fast, we squash words together. *Do not* becomes *don't*. But look — some letters fall out when we squash! Where did the *o* in *not* go? We mark the gap with a tiny apostrophe — like a little gravestone showing where the letters are buried. Let's bury some letters!"

**Show:** *do not* written out, then *don't* underneath.  **Do:** cross the *o* of *not* in red, then write the apostrophe in its exact place: *don't*.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Do you ever say *don't* or *I'm* when you talk? Say a sentence with *don't*." → *listen for:* natural use, e.g. "I don't like spiders."
*(If unsure: model — "*I am* hungry → *I'm* hungry. People squash words all the time when they speak.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Job one of the apostrophe: squashing words. Watch *do not*. I squash them: *donot* — too long. The *o* in *not* drops out. The apostrophe takes its place: *don't*. The apostrophe sits EXACTLY where the letter went missing. Now *I am*: squash to *Iam*, the *a* drops out → *I'm*. And *it is*: the *i* drops out → *it's*. Find the missing letter, put the apostrophe there."

**Show / Do:** for each, write the two words, cross the dropped letter(s) in red, then write the contraction with the apostrophe right where the gap is.
**Emphasize:** "The apostrophe marks the spot where the letters went missing."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Squash these with me. *Can not* — which letters drop out?" (the *n* and *o* of *not*) "So → *can't*. Now *she will* — the *wi* drops → *she'll*. And *we are* — the *a* drops → *we're*."

**Do:** together cross the dropped letters and place the apostrophe for *can't, she'll, we're*. Point to where each apostrophe sits.
**Ask:** "In *can't*, which letters did we bury?" → *listen for:* the *n-o* of *not* (apostrophe stands in their place).

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Contract *cannot* into one word."
- ✅ **Expected:** *can't*. Then probe: *"Which letters are missing, and is the apostrophe sitting in their spot?"*
- ❌ **If the child writes *ca'nt* or *cant'*:** apostrophe in the wrong place. → **fix:** "Where did the letters drop out? In *not* — the *n-o* go. The apostrophe goes right there: *can't*."
- ❌ **If the child leaves out the apostrophe (*cant*):** forgot the gravestone. → **fix:** "Letters went missing — we must mark the gap. Where do they go? Put the apostrophe there."

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child writes the contraction for four pairs (*do not, I am, it is, we are*), crossing the dropped letters first, then pointing to where the apostrophe sits.
**Watch for:** apostrophe placed at the join rather than where letters dropped; missing apostrophe; spelling the rest wrong.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "let's find which letters fall out"; 3) step down — cross the dropped letters together first; 4) ONE hint — "the apostrophe goes exactly where you crossed out"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (one pair); 6) keep it safe — "Apostrophes hide where letters used to be — let's hunt for the gap."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the apostrophe's first job, and where it sits." → *listen for:* squashing two words into one; it sits where the missing letters were.
**Process praise:** "You found the missing letters every time and put the apostrophe right on the spot — that's exactly how contractions work."
**Revisit later:** Lesson 12 introduces the apostrophe's second job (belonging) — recall this one as the contrast; both return in Lesson 13's "trap".

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** cross out the dropped letters for the child first; child just adds the apostrophe in the gap.
- **Stretch (challenge):** write *they're* and *you're* and say exactly which letters are missing (*they are → they're*: the *a*; *you are → you're*: the *a*).

**Extension (if time allows):** find three contractions in a storybook page and write out the two words they came from.
**Assessment evidence:** child writes at least three contractions with the apostrophe correctly placed where letters were dropped (e.g. *can't, I'm, it's*).

## Lesson 12 — Apostrophes job 2: belonging (possession)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Apostrophes & homophones)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can use *'s* to show that something belongs to someone."

**You will need:** belonging phrase cards ("the tail of the cat", "the bag of Sam", "the bone of the dog", "the desk of the tutor"); sticky labels; whiteboard; real objects around the home to label.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** what a noun is; the apostrophe's Job 1 (contractions, Lesson 11).
**Key vocabulary:** *apostrophe, possession / belonging, 's* (apostrophe-s).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** The apostrophe's **Job 2**: add **'s** to a name/noun to show **something belongs to it** — *the cat's tail = the tail of the cat*. The move: **find who owns it → add 's to the owner.** Keep it concrete by labelling real objects. (Hold the plural trap for Lesson 13, but if the child writes *cats'* or plural confusion, note it.)

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Whose pencil is this? Watch — I can make a word TELL you who it belongs to. I just add a tiny *'s*. *Mia* plus *'s* = *Mia's pencil* — the pencil belonging to Mia! Let's make some belonging labels for things around the room."

**Show:** a pencil and a label.  **Do:** write *Mia's pencil* on a label and stick it on.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Yesterday the apostrophe had a job — squashing words. Contract *do not*." → *listen for:* *don't*.
*(If shaky: quick re-show *do not → don't*; "today the apostrophe has a SECOND, different job.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Job two of the apostrophe: belonging. If something belongs to someone, I add *'s* to the owner. Watch: *the cat's tail* — that means *the tail of the cat*. The *'s* on *cat* says 'this belongs to the cat'. *Sam's bag* = the bag belonging to Sam. The trick: find the OWNER, add *'s* right after their name. *the dog* owns the bone → *the dog's bone*."

**Show / Do:** write *the cat's tail* and beside it *= the tail of the cat*; then *Sam's bag*. Underline the owner each time before adding *'s*.
**Emphasize:** "Find the owner, add *'s* to show it belongs to them."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Turn this around with me: *the bone of the dog*. Who's the owner? The dog. So → *the dog's bone*. Now *the desk of the tutor*?"

**Do:** together rewrite *the dog's bone* and *the tutor's desk*, underlining the owner first, then adding *'s*.
**Ask:** "Show that the bag belongs to Sam." → *listen for:* *Sam's bag*.

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Write it to show the bag belongs to Sam."
- ✅ **Expected:** *Sam's bag*. Then probe: *"Where did the *'s* go, and why there?"* (on Sam, the owner).
- ❌ **If the child writes *Sams bag* (no apostrophe):** forgot the possessive mark. → **fix:** "Does the bag belong to Sam? Then Sam needs *'s* to show it. *Sam's bag*."
- ❌ **If the child writes *bag's Sam* or adds *'s* to the wrong word:** put it on the thing, not the owner. → **fix:** "Who OWNS the bag? Sam. The *'s* goes on the owner — *Sam's bag*."

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child writes possessive *'s* for four phrases (turning "the X of the Y" → "the Y's X") and makes sticky labels for two real items around the home (e.g. *the tutor's chair*).
**Watch for:** missing apostrophe; *'s* on the object instead of the owner; child confusing this with a plural — gently note for Lesson 13.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "let's find the owner first"; 3) step down — give the phrase, child only adds *'s* to the underlined owner; 4) ONE hint — "ask: who does it belong to?"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (one phrase); 6) keep it safe — "Belonging is all about the owner — let's find who owns it."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the apostrophe's second job, and where the *'s* goes." → *listen for:* shows belonging; *'s* goes on the owner.
**Process praise:** "You found the owner every time and gave them their *'s* — your belonging labels are spot on."
**Revisit later:** Lesson 13 — the apostrophe TRAP: NOT for plurals. Recall both jobs (squashing & belonging) as the two times we DO use an apostrophe.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** phrase given with the owner underlined; child only adds *'s*.
- **Stretch (challenge):** rewrite "the lead of the dog" and "the desk of the tutor", then write a sentence using a possessive *'s* ("I borrowed Mia's rubber.").

**Extension (if time allows):** label three more real objects with belonging labels and read them aloud.
**Assessment evidence:** child writes at least three possessive phrases with *'s* correctly on the owner (e.g. *Sam's bag, the dog's bone*).

## Lesson 13 — The apostrophe trap: NOT for plurals
**English · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Apostrophes & homophones)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can tell a plural (no apostrophe) from possession/contraction (apostrophe)."

**You will need:** "wrong sign" cards ("apple's 50p", "two car's", "fresh egg's"); a sorting mat (Apostrophe / No apostrophe); whiteboard; two sentences using *its/it's*.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** apostrophe Job 1 (contractions) and Job 2 (possession) from Lessons 11–12.
**Key vocabulary:** *plural* (more than one), *possession, contraction, its / it's*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Apostrophes do **NOT** make plurals — *two cats*, never *cat's*. We only use an apostrophe for **contraction** or **possession**. The diagnostic question: **"Is something BELONGING, or are letters MISSING? If neither, it's just a plural — no apostrophe."** Teach the *its/it's* test ("say *it is*"). Pre-empt the greengrocer's apostrophe. Frame as "sign inspector".

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "I saw a sign at a shop. It said *apple's 50p*. Hmm… is that right? Do the apples OWN anything? No! Nothing's belonging, no letters missing — so that apostrophe is a MISTAKE. Today you're the sign inspector, catching apostrophes that shouldn't be there. Badge on — let's inspect!"

**Show:** the "apple's 50p" sign card.  **Do:** cross out the wrong apostrophe with a flourish → *apples 50p*.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "When DO we use an apostrophe? You've learned two jobs." → *listen for:* squashing words (contraction) and belonging (possession).
*(If shaky: quick re-show *don't* (squash) and *Sam's bag* (belonging) — "only those two times.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Big rule: an apostrophe is NEVER for a plain plural. *Two cats* — more than one cat — no apostrophe! But *the cat's tail* — belonging — DOES get one. So I ask one question: is something BELONGING, or are letters MISSING? If neither, it's just a plural — leave the apostrophe out. And a special pair: *it's* with an apostrophe means *it is* — test by saying 'it is'. *Its* with NO apostrophe means belonging to it. *The dog wagged its tail* — say 'it is tail'? No! So no apostrophe."

**Show / Do:** two columns — *two cats* (plural, no apostrophe) vs *the cat's tail* (belonging, apostrophe). Then *it's raining* (it is) vs *its tail* (belonging — no apostrophe).
**Emphasize:** "Plurals NEVER take an apostrophe. Only belonging or missing letters do."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Inspect this sign with me: *two car's*. Is anything belonging? Are letters missing? No — it just means more than one car. So…?" (cross out the apostrophe → *cars*) "Now *fresh egg's*?"

**Do:** together fix *cars* and *eggs* (remove the wrong apostrophes). Then test *it's/its*: "*The cat licked it's paw* — say 'it is paw'? No → *its paw*."
**Ask:** "Is *two dogs* written *dog's* or *dogs*?" → *listen for:* *dogs* — no apostrophe (it's a plural).

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "True or false: two dogs is written *dog's*."
- ✅ **Expected:** False — it's *dogs* (plural, no apostrophe). Then probe: *"How do you know it's not belonging?"* (nothing belongs to the dogs; it just means more than one).
- ❌ **If the child says True:** over-applied the apostrophe near *s* (greengrocer's apostrophe). → **fix:** "Do the dogs OWN anything? Are letters missing? No — just more than one. Plurals get no apostrophe."
- ❌ **If the child gets *its/it's* backwards:** assumed possessive always needs an apostrophe. → **fix:** "Use the test — can you say 'it is'? If yes, *it's*. If no, *its* with no apostrophe."

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child decides apostrophe-or-not for six phrases (mix of plurals like *three cats, two buses*; possessives like *the boy's coat*; contractions like *we're here*), sorting onto the mat. Fast finishers fix *its/it's* in two sentences.
**Watch for:** adding apostrophes to plain plurals; removing needed ones from possessives/contractions; *its/it's* slips.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't decide for them; 2) name *why* — "let's ask our one question"; 3) step down — use the yes/no gate "Is something belonging?" then "Are letters missing?"; 4) ONE hint — "if you answered no to both, it's just a plural"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (one phrase); 6) keep it safe — "Even shops get this wrong — that's why we inspect carefully."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the rule a sign inspector lives by — when does a word with *s* NOT get an apostrophe?" → *listen for:* when it's just a plural (more than one); apostrophes only for belonging or missing letters.
**Process praise:** "You caught the fake apostrophes and kept the real ones — a sharp-eyed sign inspector. You even cracked *its* and *it's*."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-Unit 3 check** will ask the child to mark *two dogs* as *dogs*. Bring back the *its/it's* test as retrieval before Lesson 14.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** use the yes/no gate "Is something *belonging*?" as the single decision; only plurals vs possessives (drop contractions/its-it's).
- **Stretch (challenge):** fix *its/it's* in two sentences and explain the test; spot a real greengrocer's apostrophe and correct it.

**Extension (if time allows):** design a "correct" shop sign and a "wrong" one and explain the difference.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly leaves plurals apostrophe-free and keeps apostrophes for possession/contraction in at least four of six phrases; states "plurals never take an apostrophe".

## Lesson 14 — Homophones: there / their / they're
**English · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Apostrophes & homophones)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose *there*, *their* or *they're* by meaning."

**You will need:** homophone cue cards (*there* = place / *their* = belonging / *they're* = they are); a gap-fill sentence sheet (6 sentences); whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** apostrophe contractions — *they're = they are* (Lesson 11).
**Key vocabulary:** *homophone* (words that sound the same but are spelled differently and mean different things), *there, their, they're*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** These three sound identical but mean totally different things — choose by **MEANING**. The memory hooks (from KB): ***there*** has *here* inside it (both about place); ***their*** has *heir* (an owner) inside it (about belonging); ***they're*** is squashed *they are* (apostrophe = missing *a*). The test: **"Can I say 'they are'? Then it's they're."** Pre-empt using *there* for all three.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Three words sound EXACTLY the same — *there, their, they're* — but they mean completely different things. They're the words grown-ups muddle most, even in text messages! Today you're going to become the expert who never gets them wrong. Ready to beat the grown-ups?"

**Show:** the three words written in a row, all sounding the same when read aloud.  **Do:** read all three — "hear that? Identical. But watch the spellings."
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "One of them is a squashed-up pair. *They are* squashes into…?" → *listen for:* *they're*.
*(If shaky: re-show *they are → they're*, apostrophe for the missing *a*.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Pick by MEANING. *There* — a PLACE, like 'over there'. See the secret? *Here* is hiding inside *there* — both are about place. *Their* — BELONGING to them, 'their dog'. It's got *heir* in it — an heir is someone who owns things — so *their* is about owning. *They're* — squashed *they are* — the apostrophe stands for the missing *a*. My best test: can I say 'they are'? If yes, use *they're*. If it's a place, *there*. If it belongs to them, *their*."

**Show / Do:** annotate each: under *there* circle *here*; under *their* circle *heir*; under *they're* show *they + are*. Model on "*They're* going to *their* house over *there*."
**Emphasize:** "Say 'they are' — if it fits, it's *they're*. Place = *there*. Belonging = *their*."

### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Choose with me. '___ my best friends.' Try the test — can I say 'they are my best friends'? Yes! So…?" (*They're*) "Now: 'I like ___ new car.' Does the car belong to them? Yes → ?" (*their*)

**Do:** together fill two sentences, saying the test aloud each time. Write the chosen word.
**Ask:** "Which one means 'belonging to them'?" → *listen for:* *their*.

### ⏱ 14–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which of the three means 'belonging to them'?"
- ✅ **Expected:** *their*. Then probe: *"What's the clue word hiding inside it?"* (*heir* — an owner).
- ❌ **If the child says *there*:** muddled place with belonging. → **fix:** "Does 'over there' mean belonging? No — that's a place. Belonging is *their*, with *heir* inside."
- ❌ **If the child says *they're*:** confused with 'they are'. → **fix:** "Say 'they are' — does 'belonging to them' mean 'they are'? No. *Their* is the belonging one."

### ⏱ 17–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child fills six gaps choosing *there/their/they're*, saying the test aloud for each ("can I say 'they are'? is it a place? does it belong?").
**Watch for:** defaulting to *there* for all; forgetting the apostrophe in *they're*; choosing *their* for a place.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't choose for them; 2) name *why* — "let's run the test"; 3) step down — give two choices not three; use the cue cards; 4) ONE hint — "try saying 'they are' in the gap — does it make sense?"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (one sentence); 6) keep it safe — "Even grown-ups muddle these — the test makes you the expert."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Teach me the three meanings and the best test." → *listen for:* *there* = place (has *here*), *their* = belonging (has *heir*), *they're* = they are; test = "can I say 'they are'?"
**Process praise:** "You ran the test on every gap and chose by meaning — you're now the expert who beats the grown-up texters."
**Revisit later:** Lesson 15 brings *to/too/two* and reviews this set. **End-of-Unit 3 check** uses "___ going to ___ house over ___" (*They're / their / there*).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** two choices instead of three; meaning cue cards on the table; tutor says the test, child decides.
- **Stretch (challenge):** write one sentence using all three correctly (e.g. "*They're* taking *their* dog over *there*.").

**Extension (if time allows):** find an example of each word in a book and explain why that spelling is right.
**Assessment evidence:** child chooses correctly in at least four of six gaps, applying the "say 'they are'" test, and can state the meaning of each.

## Lesson 15 — Homophones: to / too / two & friends (retrieval)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 3 (Apostrophes & homophones)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose the right homophone from sound-alike sets by meaning."

**You will need:** a homophone gap-fill sheet (6 mixed sentences); cue cards (*two* = number / *too* = also or very / *to* = the rest; *here/hear, see/sea*); whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** there/their/they're chosen by meaning (Lesson 14); homophones sound the same but mean different things.
**Key vocabulary:** *homophone, to, too, two, here, hear, see, sea*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Another sound-alike set chosen by **meaning**. The memory hooks (from KB): ***two*** = the number (has the *w*, like *twin/twice*); ***too*** = also / very — the **extra *o* for 'extra'**; ***to*** = the everyday one for everything else. Pre-empt mixing them up by always asking the meaning first. Retrieval of there/their/they're keeps it spaced.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's a tongue-twister of a sentence: '*Two* biscuits is *too* many *to* eat!' Three sound-alikes in ONE sentence — and they all sound like 'too'! By the end you'll untangle them every time. First, a quick warm-up on yesterday's three."

**Show:** the sentence with all three highlighted.  **Do:** read it aloud — "hear how they all sound the same?"
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Yesterday's set — which means 'they are'?" → *listen for:* *they're*.
*(If shaky: quick recall of *there* (place), *their* (belonging), *they're* (they are).)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Three more sound-alikes, all chosen by meaning. *Two* — that's the NUMBER, 2. It's got that *w*, like its cousins *twin* and *twice*. *Too* — means ALSO or VERY: 'me too', 'too hot'. Here's the trick: it has an EXTRA *o* — an extra *o* for 'extra'! *To* — is the everyday one for everything else: 'I'm going to school'. And two more pairs: *here* (this place) / *hear* (with your ears — *ear* is inside it!); *see* (with your eyes) / *sea* (the water)."

**Show / Do:** write *two* (circle the *w*), *too* (circle the extra *o*), *to*. Then *hear* (circle *ear*).
**Emphasize:** "*Too* means also or very — extra *o* for extra!"

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Choose with me. 'I have ___ cats.' How many — is it a number? Yes → ?" (*two*) "Now: 'Can I come ___?' meaning 'also' → ?" (*too* — extra *o* for extra).

**Do:** fill two sentences together, naming the meaning first each time. Add one *here/hear* example: "Did you ___ that?" → *hear* (ear inside).
**Ask:** "Choose for: 'I want to come ___.' meaning 'also'." → *listen for:* *too*.

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "'I want to come ___.' — the word that means 'also'."
- ✅ **Expected:** *too*. Then probe: *"What's the memory trick?"* (extra *o* for 'extra'/also).
- ❌ **If the child writes *to*:** picked the everyday one. → **fix:** "Does it mean 'also' here? Yes — so it needs the extra *o*: *too*."
- ❌ **If the child writes *two*:** picked the number. → **fix:** "Is it a number, 2? No — it means 'also'. That's *too*, the extra-*o* one."

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child completes six mixed homophone gaps (a blend of *to/too/two*, *here/hear*, *see/sea*, and a there/their/they're review item), saying the meaning aloud first.
**Watch for:** defaulting to *to*; missing the "extra *o*" for *too*; mixing *here/hear* or *see/sea*.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't choose for them; 2) name *why* — "let's say the meaning first"; 3) step down — give the meaning prompt cards; offer two choices; 4) ONE hint — "is it a number? does it mean also? if not, it's plain *to*"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (one gap); 6) keep it safe — "These trip up everyone — naming the meaning is your superpower."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the three 'too'-sounding words and how you pick." → *listen for:* *two* = number, *too* = also/very (extra *o*), *to* = everything else; choose by meaning.
**Process praise:** "You named the meaning before every choice — that's exactly how to untangle sound-alikes. The 'extra *o* for extra' trick is yours forever now."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-Unit 3 check** — *can't*, *Sam's bag*, *dogs* (no apostrophe), and *They're/their/there*. Re-pose one *to/too/two* gap as a quick retrieval in Unit 4.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** meaning prompts on each word; two choices per gap; tutor reads the sentence and names the meaning together.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain the "*too = extra o*" trick in their own words and write a sentence using *two, too,* and *to* correctly.

**Extension (if time allows):** spot *here/hear* or *see/sea* in a book and explain which is which.
**Assessment evidence:** child chooses correctly in at least four of six mixed gaps and states the *too = extra o* trick.

## Lesson 16 — Nouns, adjectives and verbs (word jobs)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 4 (Word classes & sentence types)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can spot whether a word is a noun, an adjective or a verb."

**You will need:** word cards (*dog, run, fluffy, school, jump, red, ball, sleep, happy, cat*); a three-column sorting mat (Nouns / Adjectives / Verbs); test-prompt cards ("Can I put *the* before it?" / "Can I *do* it?" / "Does it describe a noun?"); whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** can write/read a simple sentence (Year 1).
**Key vocabulary:** *noun* (a naming word), *adjective* (a describing word), *verb* (a doing/being word).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Every word has a **job**. Give the child three quick **tests** to find the job (word-class labels are abstract — the tests make them concrete): **noun** — "can I put *the* in front?"; **verb** — "can I *do* it / say *I am ___ing*?"; **adjective** — "does it describe a noun?". Pre-empt calling everything a noun. Frame as a "word detective" with tools.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Every single word has a JOB. Some words NAME things — *dog, school* — those are nouns. Some words DESCRIBE — *fluffy, red* — adjectives. Some words DO things — *run, jump* — verbs. Today you're a word detective, and I'm giving you three tools to find out each word's job. Detective badge on!"

**Show:** the three column headings and three word cards (*dog, run, fluffy*).  **Do:** place each in its column as you name the job.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Read me this sentence: *The happy dog ran.*" → *listen for:* fluent read.
*(If shaky: beat-read any tricky word first, then re-read smoothly.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Three detective tools. Tool one, for NOUNS: can I put *the* in front? *The dog* — yes! Dog is a noun. Tool two, for VERBS: can I DO it, or say *I am ___ing*? *I am running* — yes! Run is a verb. Tool three, for ADJECTIVES: does it describe a noun? *Fluffy* — a fluffy dog — yes, it describes! Watch me test *ball*: *the ball* works → noun. *Jump*: *I am jumping* → verb. *Red*: *a red ball*, it describes → adjective."

**Show / Do:** test and sort *dog, run, fluffy, ball, jump, red* aloud, placing each card in its column and saying which tool proved it.
**Emphasize:** "Use the test: *the*? → noun. *I am ___ing*? → verb. Describes a noun? → adjective."

### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Detect with me. Take *school*. Can I put *the* in front? *The school* — yes! So it's a…?" (noun) "Now *sleep* — can I do it? *I am sleeping*? Yes → ?" (verb) "And *happy* — does it describe? *a happy cat* → ?" (adjective)

**Do:** together test and sort *school, sleep, happy* using the prompt cards.
**Ask:** "Which word is the verb in *The happy dog ran*?" → *listen for:* *ran* (you can do it — *I am running*).

### ⏱ 14–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which word is the verb: *The happy dog ran*?"
- ✅ **Expected:** *ran*. Then probe: *"Which test proved it?"* (it's a doing word — *I am running*).
- ❌ **If the child says *dog*:** confused noun with verb. → **fix:** "Can you *do* a dog? No — but you can put *the* before it, so *dog* is a noun. Which word is the DOING word? *ran*."
- ❌ **If the child says *happy*:** confused adjective with verb. → **fix:** "Does *happy* describe the dog? Yes — that's an adjective. The DOING word is *ran*."

### ⏱ 17–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child sorts the remaining word list (*cat, jump, red, sleep, ball, fluffy*…) into nouns / adjectives / verbs, saying the test aloud for each card.
**Watch for:** calling everything a noun; guessing without using a test; muddling adjective and verb.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't sort it; 2) name *why* — "let's run the tests in order"; 3) step down — two columns first (nouns vs verbs), add adjectives after; 4) ONE hint — "try putting *the* in front — does it work?"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (one card); 6) keep it safe — "Detectives use their tools — let's test it together."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me your three detective tools for finding a word's job." → *listen for:* *the* in front → noun; can do it / *I am ___ing* → verb; describes a noun → adjective.
**Process praise:** "You used a test on every word instead of guessing — that's how a real word detective works."
**Revisit later:** Lesson 17 uses nouns + adjectives to build expanded noun phrases. Re-pose "which word is the verb?" as retrieval next session.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** two columns first (nouns vs verbs) before adding adjectives; clear, concrete words only.
- **Stretch (challenge):** find all three word classes in one sentence (e.g. *The fluffy cat sleeps* → adjective/noun/verb) and label each.

**Extension (if time allows):** write one noun, one adjective and one verb of their own and prove each with a test.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly sorts at least six words using the tests and identifies the verb in a given sentence.

## Lesson 17 — Expanded noun phrases (paint a picture)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 4 (Word classes & sentence types)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can expand a noun with adjectives, using a comma between two."

**You will need:** noun cards (*castle, forest, monster, dog*); an adjective word-bank (*dark, spooky, silent, huge, slimy, green, fluffy, noisy*); whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** noun = naming word, adjective = describing word (Lesson 16).
**Key vocabulary:** *noun, adjective, expanded noun phrase* (a noun with describing words added), *comma*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Adding adjectives to a noun makes an **expanded noun phrase** that "paints a clearer picture in the reader's head" — not just *a dog*, but *a small, noisy dog*. The punctuation rule: **a comma between two adjectives**. Pre-empt stringing adjectives with no comma (*a big red fast car*). Build up step by step: noun → one adjective → two adjectives with a comma.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Close your eyes. I say *a dog*. What do you picture? Could be any dog! Now I say *a small, noisy dog*. Ah — now you can really SEE it! Adjectives paint a clearer picture in the reader's head. Today we'll paint pictures with words."

**Show:** the noun card *dog*; then write *a small, noisy dog*.  **Do:** read both and ask the child what changed in their mind's eye.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Quick — is *fluffy* a noun, a verb or an adjective?" → *listen for:* adjective (it describes a noun).
*(If shaky: "Does it describe? *a fluffy dog* — yes → adjective." Recall the test.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "I'm going to grow a noun into a picture. Start with the noun: *castle*. Add ONE adjective in front: *a dark castle* — better! Now add a SECOND adjective — but here's the rule: put a COMMA between the two adjectives: *a dark, spooky castle*. The comma is a tiny pause that keeps the two describing words tidy. Noun → one adjective → two adjectives with a comma between."

**Show / Do:** build the phrase in three stages on the board, adding the comma in a bold colour: *castle → a dark castle → a dark, spooky castle*.
**Emphasize:** "Two adjectives? Put a comma between them."

### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Paint with me. Take *forest*. Choose one adjective from the bank… now a second one — and where does the comma go?"

**Do:** together build *a dark, silent forest* (placing the comma) and *a slimy, green monster*, choosing from the word-bank.
**Ask:** "Add two adjectives to *forest* — and don't forget the comma." → *listen for:* e.g. *a dark, silent forest* with a comma between the two adjectives.

### ⏱ 14–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Make an expanded noun phrase: add two adjectives to *forest*."
- ✅ **Expected:** e.g. *a dark, silent forest* — two adjectives, comma between. Then probe: *"Where did your comma go, and why?"* (between the two adjectives).
- ❌ **If the child writes two adjectives with no comma (*a dark silent forest*):** didn't separate them. → **fix:** "Two adjectives need a tiny pause between them — pop a comma there: *a dark, silent forest*."
- ❌ **If the child uses a non-adjective (e.g. a verb):** word-class slip. → **fix:** "Does that word DESCRIBE the forest? Let's pick a describing word from the bank."

### ⏱ 17–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child expands three nouns (*castle, monster, dog*) into noun phrases, each with two adjectives and a comma. Encourage choosing vivid adjectives from the bank.
**Watch for:** missing comma; only one adjective; using non-adjectives; comma in the wrong place (e.g. after the noun).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "let's check we have two describing words and a comma"; 3) step down — give the noun + one adjective, child adds the second + comma; 4) ONE hint — "choose two describing words, then a comma between them"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (one phrase from the bank); 6) keep it safe — "Painting with words takes practice — let's pick two and pop a comma in."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me how to grow a plain noun into a picture — and the comma rule." → *listen for:* add adjectives to describe it; put a comma between two adjectives.
**Process praise:** "You painted real pictures with your adjectives — and you placed the comma between them every time. The reader can really SEE your monster!"
**Revisit later:** these expanded noun phrases feed the end-of-year stretch (add an expanded noun phrase to a written sentence). Re-pose one as retrieval before Lesson 18.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** adjective word-bank to choose from; tutor writes the noun, child adds two adjectives + comma.
- **Stretch (challenge):** drop the bank (generate own adjectives) and put the noun phrase into a full sentence (e.g. "*A slimy, green monster* lived in the cave.").

**Extension (if time allows):** describe a made-up monster with a noun phrase of two adjectives and draw it to match.
**Assessment evidence:** child writes at least two expanded noun phrases each with two adjectives correctly separated by a comma.

## Lesson 18 — The four sentence types
**English · Year 2 · Unit 4 (Word classes & sentence types)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name the four sentence types and add the correct end mark."

**You will need:** sentence cards (*Stop! / Where are you? / I am tired. / What a mess! / Dogs bark. / Why do dogs bark? / Tidy up. / How fast it runs!*); four type-label cards (Statement / Question / Command / Exclamation); whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** capital letters and full stops; verbs/commands (Lesson 16).
**Key vocabulary:** *statement* (tells — full stop), *question* (asks — ?), *command* (bossy verb), *exclamation* (strong feeling — *What…!/How…!*).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Sentences come in **four types**, each with its own end mark: **statement** tells (.), **question** asks (?), **command** bosses (bossy verb), **exclamation** bursts with feeling and properly starts with **What…/How…** (!). Pre-empt two misconceptions: ending a question with a full stop; and calling anything with *!* an "exclamation sentence" (a command can use *!* too). Read each type aloud "in the right voice".

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Sentences come in four flavours, like ice cream! There's TELLING sentences, ASKING sentences, BOSSING sentences, and BURSTING-with-feeling sentences. Each one has its own special ending. Let's taste all four!"

**Show:** three cards — *Stop! / Where are you? / I am tired.*  **Do:** read each in a different voice (bossy, questioning-up, tired-flat).
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What two things does every sentence start and end with?" → *listen for:* a capital letter at the start, an end mark at the end.
*(If shaky: "A sentence opens with a capital and closes with a mark — today we learn which mark.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Four types. A STATEMENT just tells you something and ends in a full stop: *Dogs bark.* A QUESTION asks something and needs a question mark: *Why do dogs bark?* A COMMAND bosses you about with a bossy verb at the front: *Tidy up. Sit down.* An EXCLAMATION bursts with feeling and properly starts with *What* or *How*, ending in an exclamation mark: *What a mess! How fast it runs!* Watch me sort: *Stop!* — bossy verb, that's a command. *Where are you?* — asks, question. *I am tired.* — tells, statement."

**Show / Do:** place each card under its type label; write the matching end mark beside each.
**Emphasize:** "Tells → full stop. Asks → question mark. Bosses → command (bossy verb). Bursts with *What/How* → exclamation."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Sort with me. *Dogs bark.* — does it tell, ask, boss or burst?" (tells → statement, full stop) "Now *Why do dogs bark?*" (asks → question, ?)

**Do:** together sort two more cards under labels and add end marks, reading each in its voice.
**Ask:** "What type is *Close the window.*?" → *listen for:* a command (bossy verb *close* at the front).

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What type is *Close the window.*?"
- ✅ **Expected:** a command (starts with a bossy verb). Then probe: *"How do you know — what's the first word doing?"* (bossing you to do something).
- ❌ **If the child says 'statement':** confused telling with bossing. → **fix:** "Is it telling you a fact, or telling YOU to do something? It's bossing you — *close!* — that's a command."
- ❌ **If the child says 'exclamation' (because it feels strong):** confused mark with type. → **fix:** "Does it start with *What* or *How*? No. A true exclamation does. This one bosses — it's a command."

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child sorts eight sentence cards into the four types and adds the correct end mark to each.
**Watch for:** full stop on a question; calling any *!* sentence an exclamation; missing the bossy-verb cue for commands.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't sort it; 2) name *why* — "let's ask: does it tell, ask, boss or burst?"; 3) step down — use the four type-cards as anchors and read aloud in the matching voice; 4) ONE hint — "read it — does your voice go UP? then it's a question"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (one card); 6) keep it safe — "Four flavours take practice — let's taste this one again."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Name the four sentence types and the mark each one ends with." → *listen for:* statement (.), question (?), command (bossy verb, . or !), exclamation (*What/How*…!).
**Process praise:** "You read each one in the right voice and matched its mark — even spotting that a strong-feeling sentence isn't always an *exclamation* type. Sharp work."
**Revisit later:** Lesson 19 zooms in on questions and exclamations (the special marks). Re-pose "what type is…?" as retrieval next session.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** four type-cards as anchors on the table; sort with tutor reading each aloud in voice first.
- **Stretch (challenge):** write one sentence of each of the four types with correct end marks.

**Extension (if time allows):** turn one statement into all four types where possible and read each in its voice.
**Assessment evidence:** child sorts at least six of eight sentences into the correct type with the right end mark, including identifying a command by its bossy verb.
