## Lesson 1 — Clap it into beats: reading long words by syllable
**English · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Fluent reading)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can read a long word by chopping it into syllables (beats)."

**You will need:** long-word cards (*rabbit, sunset, playground, elephant, picnic, dinosaur, jumper, basket*) printed big; a small drum, tambourine, or just the table to tap; a pencil.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** blending sounds in short words (CVC like *cat*) from Year 1 Phase 5.
**Key vocabulary:** *syllable* (a beat in a word), *blend* (push sounds together), *decode* (sound a word out).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A long word is not one scary lump — it is a string of small **beats** you already know how to read. The move that must land: **clap → read each beat → push together**. Pre-empt the misconception that you must read a long word "in one go" (working-memory overload). Repeat the magic phrase: *"Clap it into beats first."*

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "I've got a word here that looks SO long it might be a bit scary. Ready? *Fantastic!* Whoa. But watch this — I'm going to knock that big word down, one beat at a time, like toppling a tower of blocks. Want to see the trick?"

**Show:** the card *fantastic* held up big.  **Do:** clap three times slowly as you say *fan – tas – tic*, knocking an imaginary tower down with each clap.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "First, warm up your reading. Read this little word for me: *cat*." → *listen for:* /c/ /a/ /t/ → "cat", blended smoothly.
*(If blending is shaky: sound it with the child finger by finger — /c/ /a/ /t/ — then "push them together: cat." Do one more, *sun*, before moving on.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A long word is made of little beats. We call each beat a *syllable*. Watch me read *rabbit*. Step one — I clap the beats: *rab* (clap) *bit* (clap). Two beats! Step two — I read each beat on its own: /r/ /a/ /b/… *rab*. /b/ /i/ /t/… *bit*. Step three — I push them together, faster: *rab-bit*… *rabbit!* Three steps every time: **clap, read each beat, push together.**"

**Show / Do:** on *rabbit*, draw a small dot under *rab* and under *bit* as you clap, so the child sees two beats. Sound each beat, then sweep your finger under the whole word as you blend.
**Emphasize:** "Clap it into beats first. We never read a long word in one big gulp — we read it beat by beat."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Now you clap WITH me. Here's *sunset*. Clap the beats with me… *sun* (clap) *set* (clap). How many beats?"

**Do:** child claps along; tutor writes a dot under each beat. Together read *sun*, then *set*, then push together → "sunset". Repeat with *playground* (*play-ground*, 2) and *elephant* (*el-e-phant*, 3 — three claps, three dots).
**Ask:** "Your turn to lead — clap *elephant* and tell me how many beats." → *listen for:* three claps, "*el-e-phant*", three beats.

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "How many syllables are in *elephant*, and then read it for me."
- ✅ **Expected:** 3 beats — *el-e-phant* → "elephant". Then probe: *"How did you knock it down? Show me the beats."*
- ❌ **If the child tries to read it in one go and stalls:** working-memory overload — too many sounds at once. → **fix:** "Clap it into beats first." Clap it together, dot each beat, then read one beat at a time.
- ❌ **If the child claps the wrong number (e.g. 2):** beats run together. → **fix:** put a hand under the chin — the jaw drops once per beat. Say it slowly: *el…e…phant* — feel three drops.

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** hand the child four cards — *picnic, dinosaur, jumper, basket*. For each: clap the beats, dot them, read each beat, then push together and read the whole word.
**Watch for:** child skipping the clap and guessing; remind "clap first". Watch for reading a beat correctly but forgetting to blend the whole word — prompt "now push them together."
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't say the word; 2) name *why* — "this one's got lots of beats, let's chop it"; 3) drop back to the concrete stage — clap it together and dot each beat; 4) give ONE hint — read just the first beat with them; 5) re-ask the smaller version — "read just *pic*… now just *nic*"; 6) keep it safe — "Mistakes show us what to practise. Let's take it one beat at a time."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Teach it back to me — if a word looks really long and scary, what are the three magic steps?" → *listen for:* clap the beats, read each beat, push together.
**Process praise:** "You didn't give up on the big words — you clapped them into beats and knocked them down one at a time. That's exactly what strong readers do."
**Revisit later:** bring back *elephant* and *picnic* as a 30-second warm-up at the start of Lesson 2, and again in Lesson 4's review.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** tutor claps the beats first; child echoes the claps, then reads each beat. Use 2-beat words only (*rabbit, sunset, picnic, jumper, basket*) for a guaranteed win before any 3-beat word.
- **Stretch (challenge):** chop a 4-beat word — *caterpillar* (*cat-er-pil-lar*) — dotting and reading all four beats.

**Extension (if time allows):** hunt for the longest word on a page of a favourite book and "beat-read" it to the tutor.
**Assessment evidence:** child independently claps and reads at least two long words (one 3-beat) using clap → read beats → blend, with no "one-gulp" attempts.

## Lesson 2 — Dinosaur names & place names (beat-reading challenge)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Fluent reading)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can read very long, unfamiliar words by syllable-chopping."

**You will need:** dinosaur cards (*T-rex, triceratops, stegosaurus, diplodocus*) and place-name cards (*Manchester, London, Australia*); a pencil for dotting beats; the *elephant* and *picnic* cards from Lesson 1.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** clap → read each beat → push together (Lesson 1).
**Key vocabulary:** *syllable* (beat), *decode* (sound out), *blend*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** The exact same trick works even for words you have NEVER seen, like dinosaur names — chop into beats, read beat by beat. The new tool: **mark the beats with little dots yourself before you read**, so your eyes know where to stop. Pre-empt the "read it all at once and give up" overload.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Big challenge today. Could you read a word as long as a dinosaur is tall? I think you can. Listen — *Ste-go-sau-rus.* That's a real dinosaur AND a really long word. By the end of today you'll read it like an expert. Let's prove it."

**Show:** the *stegosaurus* card.  **Do:** say it slowly, tapping the table four times — *ste · go · sau · rus*.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Quick warm-up from last time — read *elephant* by its beats." → *listen for:* *el-e-phant* → "elephant".
*(If shaky: clap it together, dot the three beats, read one at a time.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "When a word is REALLY long, I help my eyes by marking the beats first. Watch *triceratops*. I clap as I go and put a little dot after each beat: *tri* (dot) *cer* (dot) *a* (dot) *tops* (dot). Now my eyes know exactly where to stop. I read each beat — *tri… cer… a… tops* — then I push them together: *tri-cer-a-tops… triceratops!* The dots are like little stepping stones across a big puddle."

**Show / Do:** write *triceratops* large; clap and place a dot under each syllable break; sweep finger under each beat as you read, then under the whole word to blend.
**Emphasize:** "Dot the beats, then read the stepping stones one at a time."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's do *Manchester* together — that's a place. Clap with me and we'll dot it: *Man* (dot) *chest* (dot) *er* (dot). Read each beat… now push together."

**Do:** child claps; tutor and child place the dots; read each beat, then blend → "Manchester". Repeat with *T-rex* (*T-rex*, 2) and *diplodocus* (*di-plod-o-cus*).
**Ask:** "You dot this one: *triceratops*. Mark the beats, then read it." → *listen for:* four dots, reads *tri-cer-a-tops* → "triceratops".

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Read *stegosaurus* by its beats — dot them first."
- ✅ **Expected:** *ste-go-sau-rus* → "stegosaurus", read beat by beat. Then probe: *"Show me your dots — where did each beat stop?"*
- ❌ **If the child reads it as one long blur and stops:** overload from too many sounds. → **fix:** "Chop it into beats first" — dot it together, then read one stepping stone at a time.
- ❌ **If the child reads beats correctly but can't blend the whole word:** lost the thread over four beats. → **fix:** read the first two beats together (*ste-go*), then the next two (*sau-rus*), then join the two halves.

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child chooses three cards from the dinosaur/place pile, dots the beats on each, and reads each aloud beat by beat then blended.
**Watch for:** dots placed mid-beat (splitting a beat wrongly) — model the clap again; child guessing from the first letter instead of reading every beat.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't read it for them; 2) name *why* — "this is a brand-new word, so we chop it"; 3) step down to the concrete — clap it together and place the dots; 4) ONE hint — cover all but the first beat with your finger; 5) re-ask smaller — "just read this one beat"; uncover the next; 6) keep it safe — "Even grown-ups chop dinosaur names. Let's take it one beat at a time."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "You just read words most grown-ups stumble on! Tell me the trick for a brand-new, super-long word." → *listen for:* dot the beats, read each beat, push together.
**Process praise:** "You marked your own stepping stones and read every single beat — you didn't guess. That's expert reading."
**Revisit later:** keep one dinosaur card to re-read at the start of Lesson 3, and include *stegosaurus* in the Lesson 4 review.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** beats already dotted on the card; child just reads beat by beat then blends. Start with 2-beat *T-rex*.
- **Stretch (challenge):** give a long word with **no** dots and ask the child to mark the beats themselves before reading (e.g. *triceratops*, *Australia*).

**Extension (if time allows):** find the "world's longest dinosaur name" the child can read, dot it, and perform it proudly.
**Assessment evidence:** child independently dots and reads at least one unfamiliar 3+ beat word (e.g. *stegosaurus*) beat by beat, then blends it.

## Lesson 3 — Reading on sight & reading with expression (fluency)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Fluent reading)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can read a sentence smoothly, with expression, not robot-by-word."

**You will need:** a short decodable passage (3–4 sentences, e.g. *"The dog ran fast. It saw a cat! Where did the cat go? It hid in the box."*); a familiar storybook page; a card listing 3 sentences for the child.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** decoding/blending and beat-reading long words (Lessons 1–2).
**Key vocabulary:** *on sight / sight word* (a word your eyes just know instantly), *fluency* (reading smoothly), *expression* (reading with feeling).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Decoding is step one; **reading smoothly with feeling** is the goal. Two big ideas: (1) **sight words** — your eyes know them instantly, no sounding out; (2) **re-read for fluency** — read once to decode, then again to make it sound like talking. Pre-empt the robotic, word-by-word reading (still decoding everything). The repeated move: *"Read it once to crack it, then read it again like a storyteller."*

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "I'm going to read this sentence two ways. Listen and tell me which one sounds like a real reader. Way one…" (read flat, robotic, one… word… at… a… time) "…The. dog. ran. fast. Way two…" (read smooth and lively) "…*The dog ran fast!* Which one was the real reader?"

**Show:** the first sentence of the passage on the board.  **Do:** read it both ways, exaggerating the robot.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Before we read smoothly, warm up your decoding — beat-read this for me: *playground*." → *listen for:* *play-ground* → "playground".
*(If shaky: clap and dot the two beats, read each, blend.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Some words your eyes just KNOW — you don't sound them out at all. We call those *sight words*: *the, was, said, you.* The more you read, the more words become sight words, and that's what makes reading smooth. That smoothness has a name — *fluency*. And good readers add *expression* — feeling — so it sounds like talking, not a robot. My secret: I read a tricky sentence ONCE to crack the words, then I read it AGAIN to make it sound right. Watch." (Read the passage once, decoding; then re-read with expression, dipping your voice at the full stop, lifting at the question, popping the exclamation.)

**Show / Do:** point to *the* and *to* — "sight words, my eyes just know them." Then run your finger smoothly under the re-read, pausing at each full stop.
**Emphasize:** "Read it once to crack it, then read it again like a storyteller."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's do this passage together. First read — we just crack the words, beat-reading any tricky one. Ready?" (Read together once.) "Now the magic — second read, smooth and with feeling. Watch the punctuation: full stop, take a tiny breath; question mark, voice goes UP; exclamation, get excited!"

**Do:** read the passage together twice — decode pass, then fluency pass. Tutor models the voice changes; child copies.
**Ask:** "On the second read, what did your voice do at the question mark *Where did the cat go?*" → *listen for:* "it went up" / made it sound like asking.

### ⏱ 13–16 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What does it mean to read a word *on sight*?"
- ✅ **Expected:** read it instantly, straight away, without sounding it out. Then probe: *"Tell me a word your eyes just know."*
- ❌ **If the child says 'you look at it' (vaguely):** hasn't grasped *instant*. → **fix:** "Yes you look — but do you have to sound it out, or do you know it straight away?" Model with *the*: "I don't go /th/ /e/ — I just know it. That's on sight."
- ❌ **If the child reads everything robotically when asked to demonstrate:** still decoding every word. → **fix:** re-read the same sentence a second time — "now you've cracked it, read it smooth like you're telling me a story."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** give the child the 3-sentence card. For each sentence: first read to decode, then a second read for smoothness and expression. Encourage performing one sentence "in character" (a giant, a baby).
**Watch for:** child reading once and moving on (no fluency pass) — prompt "now the storyteller read"; ignoring punctuation — point to the question mark and lift your own voice as a model.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice — can't smooth it out):** 1) don't read the whole thing for them; 2) name *why* — "the words are still new, so they feel bumpy"; 3) step down — choose the shortest sentence; 4) ONE hint — model just that sentence with expression, then have the child echo it; 5) re-ask smaller — read it together, then the child alone; 6) keep it safe — "Smooth reading comes from re-reading. Let's read it one more time together."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me your two-step secret for making reading sound great." → *listen for:* read once to crack the words, read again smoothly with expression.
**Process praise:** "You re-read every sentence to make it smooth, and you made your voice go up at the questions — that's a real storyteller's reading."
**Revisit later:** start Lesson 4 by re-reading one of these sentences "in character"; revisit *on sight* meaning in the Lesson 4 check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** very short, fully decodable sentences (3–4 words); tutor models each sentence first, child echoes, then reads alone.
- **Stretch (challenge):** read a whole paragraph aloud "in character" (a king, a tiny mouse), changing voice for different punctuation and speakers.

**Extension (if time allows):** choose a page of a favourite storybook and perform it aloud with full expression to an audience (toy/parent).
**Assessment evidence:** child reads a 3-sentence passage on a fluency pass — smoothly, attending to at least the full stop and the question mark — and can explain "on sight" as reading instantly.

## Lesson 4 — Fluency review: beat the robot (retrieval)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 1 (Fluent reading)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can syllable-chop new words and read a passage fluently."

**You will need:** four long-word cards (*picnic, elephant, stegosaurus, playground*); a short passage (4–5 sentences with a full stop, a question mark, and an exclamation mark); a timer (optional); pencil for dots.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** clap/dot → beat-read long words (L1–2); read once to decode then again for fluency (L3).
**Key vocabulary:** *syllable* (beat), *fluency*, *expression*, *on sight*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** This is a **retrieval** lesson — the child does the doing. Two skills together: beat-read any long word, then read a passage like a storyteller. Keep the "Robot vs Storyteller" framing — robot first, then beat it. Catch any return to one-gulp reading (chop it) or robotic reading (re-read for fluency).

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "It's a face-off: Robot versus Storyteller! Here's how it works — you read it once the robot way, all flat and bumpy… then you BEAT the robot with a smooth, lively read. You always win the second time. Ready, champion?"

**Show:** the passage, covered for now; the four word cards face down.  **Do:** do a quick robot-vs-storyteller demo on one sentence.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Warm up — read *playground* by its beats." → *listen for:* *play-ground* (2) → "playground".
*(If shaky: clap, dot, read each beat, blend.)*

### ⏱ 3–6 min · EXPLAIN (I do — quick refresh)
> **Say:** "Two tricks we're using today. Trick one — long words: clap or dot the beats, read each, push together. Trick two — smooth reading: read once to crack the words, then read AGAIN like a storyteller, watching the punctuation. That second read is how you beat the robot."

**Show / Do:** quickly model dotting *stegosaurus* and reading it; then model a robot-then-storyteller read of the first passage sentence.
**Emphasize:** "Chop the big words; re-read for the smooth win."

### ⏱ 6–9 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's warm up together. Beat-read this card with me." (Hold up *elephant*; clap, dot, read.) "Now one sentence of our passage — robot read first… now our storyteller read together."

**Do:** beat-read *elephant* together; then read the opening sentence twice (decode → fluency), modelling voice for the punctuation.
**Ask:** "On our storyteller read, what should my voice do at the exclamation mark?" → *listen for:* get louder/excited.

### ⏱ 9–11 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Read *playground* and tell me its beats."
- ✅ **Expected:** *play-ground* (2 beats) → "playground". Then probe: *"How did you split it into two beats?"*
- ❌ **If the child tries to read it whole and stalls:** one-gulp overload. → **fix:** "Clap it into beats first" — clap *play-ground*, then read each beat.
- ❌ **If the child says one beat (runs it together):** beats merged. → **fix:** hand under chin — feel the jaw drop twice — *play… ground.*

### ⏱ 11–17 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** independently the child (1) beat-reads the four word cards — *picnic, elephant, stegosaurus, playground* — dotting as needed; then (2) reads the passage twice: a decode read, then a fluency read with expression. Optional: time the second read and try to beat it for smoothness next time.
**Watch for:** skipping the second (fluency) read; reading long words by guessing the first letter; ignoring the question/exclamation marks.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't supply the word; 2) name *why* — "long word, let's chop it" or "still bumpy, let's re-read"; 3) step down — dot the beats / choose the shortest sentence; 4) ONE hint — read the first beat, or model the sentence to echo; 5) re-ask the smaller version, then build back up; 6) keep it safe — "Mistakes show us what to practise. One beat / one more read."

### ⏱ 17–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "You beat the robot! Teach me both winning moves — one for long words, one for smooth reading." → *listen for:* chop into beats; read once to crack it then again for fluency.
**Process praise:** "You chopped every long word into beats and you re-read for the smooth win — your storyteller read beat the robot every time."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-Unit 1 check** — have the child beat-read *picnic, elephant, stegosaurus* and read a short passage fluently with some expression. Mastery = long words read by beats; passage smooth, not word-by-word. Bring beat-reading back as a warm-up in Unit 2.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** fewer words (two cards), a shorter 2–3 sentence passage; tutor does the decode read with the child first.
- **Stretch (challenge):** time the second (fluency) read and aim to beat it for smoothness; add a 4-beat word like *caterpillar*.

**Extension (if time allows):** record a "storyteller read" of the passage and listen back, marking one thing done well.
**Assessment evidence:** child beat-reads three unfamiliar long words and reads a short passage on a fluency pass with some expression — the End-of-Unit 1 standard.

## Lesson 5 — Just add it on: -ing, -ed, -er, -est
**English · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Spelling with suffixes)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can add a suffix to a word that needs no spelling change."

**You will need:** root-word cards (*jump, play, help, paint, kick, fast*); paper "tail" cards for suffixes (*-ing, -ed, -er, -est*) you can physically stick on; whiteboard and pen.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** what a root word is; adding *-s* to make a plural (Year 1).
**Key vocabulary:** *root word* (the main word before any ending), *suffix* (a little ending/tail added to a word).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A **suffix is a tail you add to a root word** to change it. Today's rule is the easy one: for these roots, you **just add it on — no spelling change**. This builds the habit before the trickier rules (drop-e, double, y→i) come in Lessons 6–8. Repeated image: *"Grow a tail onto the root."* Keep the root word visible and unchanged so the child sees nothing inside it changes.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "I've got a word: *jump*. Watch — I'm going to grow a little tail on the end of it. *Jump*… plus *-ing*… becomes *jumping!* That little tail is called a *suffix*, and the main word, *jump*, is the *root*. Let's grow some tails!"

**Show:** the root card *jump* and the tail card *-ing*.  **Do:** physically slide the *-ing* tail onto the end of *jump*.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "You already add endings — how do I make *cat* into more than one cat?" → *listen for:* add *s* → *cats*.
*(If shaky: "One cat… two… *cats* — we added an *s*. Endings change words. Today's ending is a bigger one called a suffix.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "The main word is the *root*. The ending we add is the *suffix*. For these easy words, I just stick the suffix straight on — nothing inside the root changes. Watch: *jump* + *-ing* = *jumping*. *Jump* + *-ed* = *jumped*. See? The *jump* stays exactly the same, I just grow the tail. Let's try *play*: *play* + *-ing* = *playing*; *play* + *-ed* = *played*. Easy — just add it on."

**Show / Do:** write *jump*, then add *ing* in a different colour so the root stays clearly visible; repeat for *jumped, playing, played*.
**Emphasize:** "Just add it on — the root word doesn't change at all."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn to help. Take *help*. Grow it an *-ing* tail… what do we get? Now an *-ed* tail?"

**Do:** child reads/builds *helping, helped*; tutor writes. Repeat with *paint* → *painting, painted* and *kick* → *kicking, kicked*.
**Ask:** "Add *-ing* to *play*." → *listen for:* *playing* (root unchanged).

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Add *-ing* to *play* and read it back."
- ✅ **Expected:** *playing*. Then probe: *"Did the word *play* change inside? No — we just added the tail."*
- ❌ **If the child changes the root (e.g. *plaing*, *playling*):** over-thinking, dropping or adding letters. → **fix:** "For this easy rule we change nothing inside the root. Write *play*, then just add *-ing*." Keep the root card visible.
- ❌ **If the child writes a different suffix (e.g. *played*):** mixed up the tails. → **fix:** "Which tail did I ask for — *-ing* or *-ed*?" Point to the *-ing* tail card.

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** give four easy roots (*jump, play, help, kick*). Child adds *-ing* AND *-ed* to each and reads them back. (Fast finishers add *-er/-est* to *fast*.)
**Watch for:** child altering the root unnecessarily; forgetting to read the new word; mixing the tails.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it for them; 2) name *why* — "the root is changing — but this rule changes nothing"; 3) step down — give the root already written, child only adds the tail; 4) ONE hint — "write the root first, exactly as it is, then the tail"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (one suffix only); 6) keep it safe — "Tails are easy — the trick is leaving the root alone."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me — what's a *root* and what's a *suffix*? And for today's easy words, what do we do to the root?" → *listen for:* root = main word; suffix = ending/tail; we just add it on, root doesn't change.
**Process praise:** "You grew tails on every word and kept the root word perfectly safe — that's careful spelling."
**Revisit later:** keep these in a warm-up for Lesson 6 ("just-add words"), then contrast with drop-the-*e*. Bring back in Lesson 9's suffix sort.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** root word pre-written; child only adds the paper tail and reads it. Use *-ing* only first, then *-ed*.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add *-er* and *-est* to *fast* (*faster, fastest*) and use one in a spoken sentence ("I am faster than…").

**Extension (if time allows):** find three more "just-add" roots around the room or in a book and grow tails on them.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly adds *-ing* and *-ed* to at least three roots with the root unchanged, and can name *root* and *suffix*.

## Lesson 6 — Drop the e (hope → hoping)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Spelling with suffixes)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can drop a silent *e* before adding a vowel suffix."

**You will need:** *e*-ending root cards (*hope, bake, smile, nice, ride, race*); suffix cards (*-ing, -ed, -er, -est*); whiteboard; a red pen for crossing out the *e*.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** root + suffix, "just add it on" (Lesson 5); silent *e* makes the vowel say its long name (Year 1 split digraph).
**Key vocabulary:** *root word, suffix, silent e, drop the e, vowel suffix* (an ending starting with a vowel like *-ing, -ed, -er, -est*).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** When a root ends in a **silent *e***, that *e* must **hop off** before a vowel suffix moves in. The model move: **cross the *e*, then add the tail.** Pre-empt the misconception *bakeing/niceer* (kept the silent *e*). Repeated phrase: *"Drop the e before a vowel suffix."*

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Watch this go wrong, then we'll fix it. *Hope* plus *-ing*… if I just stick it on I get *h-o-p-e-i-n-g*… *hopeing.* Try reading that — clunky, isn't it? There's no room for both the *e* and the *-ing*. So the silent *e* has to HOP off first. Let's kick it off!"

**Show:** write *hope* + *-ing* = *hopeing* and read it deliberately clunkily.  **Do:** cross out the *e* with a red pen so it "hops off", then add *ing* → *hoping*.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Yesterday we added tails the easy way. Add *-ing* to *jump*." → *listen for:* *jumping* (no change).
*(If shaky: re-show *jump → jumping* — "that root had no silent *e*, so nothing hopped off. Today's roots DO end in *e*.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Here's the rule. If the root ends in a silent *e*, and my suffix starts with a vowel — like *-ing, -ed, -er, -est* — I drop the *e* first. Watch *hope*: cross off the *e* (it hops away), then add *-ing* → *hoping*. Now *bake*: cross the *e*, add *-ing* → *baking*. And with *-ed*: *bake*, cross the *e*, add *-ed* → *baked*. The silent *e* always hops off to make room."

**Show / Do:** for each word, write the root, cross the *e* in red, then add the suffix in another colour. Line up *hope → hoping*, *bake → baking*, *baked*.
**Emphasize:** "Drop the e before a vowel suffix. Cross it off, then add the tail."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "You be the *e*-kicker. Take *smile* and add *-ing*. First — what do we do to the *e*?"

**Do:** child says "cross it off / drop it"; tutor crosses; child adds *-ing* → *smiling*. Repeat with *nice* + *-er* → *nicer* and *ride* + *-ing* → *riding*.
**Ask:** "Add *-ed* to *hope*. What happens to the *e* first?" → *listen for:* drop the *e* → *hoped*.

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Add *-ed* to *hope*."
- ✅ **Expected:** *hoped* (drop the *e*). Then probe: *"Why did the *e* have to go?"* (to make room for the vowel suffix).
- ❌ **If the child writes *hopeed*:** kept the silent *e*. → **fix:** "Read it — *hope-ed*, clunky. Drop the *e* before a vowel suffix." Cross it off together.
- ❌ **If the child writes *hopped* (doubles the p):** that's the wrong rule (doubling comes in Lesson 7). → **fix:** "Does *hope* have a silent *e* on the end? Yes — so we DROP the *e*, we don't double. *hope → hoped*." (Note this distinction; it returns in L7.)

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child adds *-ing* and *-ed* to four *e*-ending roots (*hope, bake, smile, race*), crossing the *e* off first each time, and reads each back.
**Watch for:** keeping the *e* (*bakeing*); dropping the *e* but then misspelling the rest; child applying drop-e to a non-*e* word — only these roots end in *e*.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "the silent *e* is still there — it has to hop off"; 3) step down — tutor crosses the *e*, child adds the tail; 4) ONE hint — "find the silent *e* on the end and cross it first"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (just *-ing* on *bake*); 6) keep it safe — "Spotting the silent *e* is the whole trick — let's find it together."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Teach me the rule for words that end in a silent *e* when we add *-ing* or *-ed*." → *listen for:* drop the *e* first, then add the suffix.
**Process praise:** "You spotted the silent *e* every time and kicked it off before adding the tail — that's exactly the rule."
**Revisit later:** warm-up for Lesson 7 contrasting *hope→hoping* (drop e) with *hop→hopping* (double); feature in the Lesson 9 sort.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** tutor crosses the *e*; child adds the suffix. Do *-ing* on all four first, then *-ed*.
- **Stretch (challenge):** spell *nicer* and *largest* (drop the *e* before *-er/-est*) and explain why the *e* goes.

**Extension (if time allows):** find two more silent-*e* words (e.g. *like, shine*) and add *-ing* correctly.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly drops the *e* before a vowel suffix in at least three of four words (e.g. *hoping, baked, smiling*).

## Lesson 7 — Double the consonant (run → running)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Spelling with suffixes)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can double the final consonant of a short-vowel word before a vowel suffix."

**You will need:** CVC root cards (*run, hop, big, sit, swim*); suffix cards (*-ing, -ed, -er*); a rule prompt card reading "short vowel + one consonant → double it"; whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** root + suffix; drop-the-*e* (Lesson 6); short vs long vowel sounds (Year 1).
**Key vocabulary:** *consonant, vowel, short vowel sound, double the consonant, CVC* (consonant-vowel-consonant word like *run*).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A short word with **one vowel + one consonant** at the end **doubles that last consonant** before a vowel suffix — to **protect the short vowel sound**. The killer contrast: *hoping* vs *hopping* — one letter changes everything. Pre-empt *runing/hoping-for-hopping* (didn't double, so the vowel reads long). Repeated test: *"Short vowel, one consonant on the end? Double it."*

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Two words, ONE letter apart, but totally different meanings. *Hoping* — that's wishing for something. *Hopping* — that's jumping like a bunny! The only difference is a double *p*. That little double letter is a superhero — it protects the short sound. Let's learn its trick."

**Show:** write *hoping* and *hopping* one above the other.  **Do:** read each — *ho-ping* (long o) vs *hop-ping* (short o) — bounce like a bunny on *hopping*.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Quick — what's the short sound for *o*? Say the word *hop*." → *listen for:* /o/ as in *hop* (short).
*(If shaky: say *hot, dog, hop* — "feel that short, sharp /o/? That's the sound we're protecting today.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Here's the rule. Look at the end of the root. Is it ONE short vowel and then ONE consonant — like *r-u-n*? If yes, I double that last consonant before a vowel suffix. Watch *run*: one vowel *u*, one consonant *n* → double the *n* → *running*. Why? If I wrote *runing*, the *u* would go long, like *roo-ning*. Doubling protects the short /u/. Now *hop*: one vowel, one consonant → double the *p* → *hopping*. Compare *hope* — that one ends in silent *e*, so we DROP, not double. *hop → hopping*, but *hope → hoping*."

**Show / Do:** under *run* mark the vowel and the single end consonant; double the *n*; add *-ing*. Put *hopping* next to *hoping* and read both to feel the difference.
**Emphasize:** "Short vowel + one consonant → double it, to protect the short sound."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "You decide with me. *Big* plus *-er*. Does it end in one short vowel and one consonant? Say it… so what do we do to the *g*?"

**Do:** child says "double it" → *bigger*. Repeat with *sit* + *-ing* → *sitting* and *swim* + *-ing* → *swimming*. Each time, point to the one vowel + one consonant before doubling.
**Ask:** "Add *-ing* to *hop*." → *listen for:* *hopping* (double the *p*).

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Add *-ing* to *hop*."
- ✅ **Expected:** *hopping* (double the *p*). Then probe: *"Why did we double the *p*?"* (to keep the short /o/, so it isn't *hoping*).
- ❌ **If the child writes *hoping*:** didn't double, so the vowel reads long. → **fix:** "Read it back — *ho-ping*? That means wishing! We want bunny-*hopping*. One vowel, one consonant → double the *p*." Use the rule card.
- ❌ **If the child drops a letter or writes *hopeing*:** confused with drop-e. → **fix:** "Does *hop* have a silent *e*? No — so we don't drop, we double. *hop → hopping*."

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child adds *-ing* and *-ed* to four CVC roots (*run, hop, sit, big* — for *big* use *-er*), doubling the last letter, and reads each back.
**Watch for:** forgetting to double (*runing*); doubling a word that shouldn't (e.g. a long-vowel or *e*-ending word) — keep to CVC roots; doubling the wrong letter.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "the short sound isn't protected — we need to double"; 3) step down — use the rule card and tap the one vowel + one consonant together; 4) ONE hint — "what's the last consonant? Make a twin of it"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (just double, then add the tail); 6) keep it safe — "The doubling trick takes practice — let's spot the short vowel together."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the test — when do we double the last letter before adding *-ing*?" → *listen for:* when the root is a short vowel + one consonant (to protect the short sound).
**Process praise:** "You tested every word for the short vowel and doubled to protect the sound — and you kept *hoping* and *hopping* apart. Brilliant."
**Revisit later:** Lesson 9 sort will mix doublers with drop-e and y→i. Re-pose *hoping vs hopping* as a quick retrieval next session.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** "short vowel + one consonant → double it" prompt card always in front; do *-ing* only at first.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain *why* *hoping* and *hopping* differ (silent *e* makes the long sound; doubling protects the short sound), and spell *biggest, swimmer*.

**Extension (if time allows):** find two more CVC words (e.g. *clap, stop*) and double correctly (*clapping, stopping*).
**Assessment evidence:** child doubles the final consonant correctly in at least three CVC words (e.g. *running, hopping, sitting*) and can contrast *hoping/hopping*.

## Lesson 8 — y → i (happy → happier)
**English · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Spelling with suffixes)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can change a final *y* to *i* before *-er/-est/-ed* (but keep *y* before *-ing*)."

**You will need:** *y*-ending root cards (*happy, funny, sunny, try, cry, dry, sad*); suffix cards (*-er, -est, -ed, -ing*); whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** root + suffix; the earlier suffix rules (just-add, drop-e, double).
**Key vocabulary:** *root word, suffix, y → i* (change the *y* to *i*), *exception* (a special case that breaks the rule).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** When a root ends in **y**, change the **y to i** before *-er, -est, -ed*. The one exception that MUST be flagged: keep the **y before -ing** (because we never want *ii*: *crying*, not *criing*). Pre-empt *happyer/cryed* (kept the *y*). Repeated image: the *y* "transforms" into an *i*.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "*Happy* plus *-er* — is it *happyer*? Read it… nope, that's not right. Watch the magic: the *y* TRANSFORMS into an *i* — *happi-er* — *happier!* But watch this twist: with *-ing*, the *y* stays put — *crying*, not *criing*. Two tricks in one lesson. Ready?"

**Show:** write *happy* → *happier* with the *y* visibly changing to *i* (write *y*, cross it, write *i*).  **Do:** mime a transformation ("the *y* turns into an *i*!").
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "We've learned three suffix tricks. What do we do to *hope* before *-ing*?" → *listen for:* drop the *e* → *hoping*.
*(If shaky: quick recap — just-add (*jump*), drop-e (*hope*), double (*hop*); "today is a fourth trick for words ending in *y*.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Rule: if the root ends in *y*, change the *y* to *i* before *-er, -est, -ed*. Watch *happy*: change *y* to *i*, add *-er* → *happier*; add *-est* → *happiest*. Now *cry*: change *y* to *i*, add *-ed* → *cried*. BUT — big exception — keep the *y* before *-ing*, because two *i*'s look wrong: *cry* + *-ing* = *crying* (NOT *criing*). So: *y→i* for *-er/-est/-ed*; keep *y* for *-ing*."

**Show / Do:** build *happy → happier → happiest* and *cry → cried* (changing y→i), then *cry → crying* (keeping the y) right beside it for contrast.
**Emphasize:** "Change *y* to *i* — except keep the *y* before *-ing*."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "You make the change. *Funny* plus *-er*. What happens to the *y*?"

**Do:** child changes *y→i* → *funnier*. Repeat with *sunny* + *-est* → *sunniest* and *try* + *-ed* → *tried*. Then the twist: *try* + *-ing* → *trying* (keep the *y*).
**Ask:** "Add *-est* to *happy*." → *listen for:* *happiest* (*y→i*).

### ⏱ 12–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Add *-est* to *happy*."
- ✅ **Expected:** *happiest* (*y → i*). Then probe: *"What did the *y* do?"* (turned into an *i*).
- ❌ **If the child writes *happyest*:** kept the *y*. → **fix:** "Read it — *happy-est*? The rule says change the *y* to *i* before *-est*. Cross the *y*, write *i*." 
- ❌ **If the child changes *y→i* before *-ing* later (e.g. *criing*):** over-applied the rule. → **fix:** "Two *i*'s in a row look wrong. Before *-ing* the *y* STAYS — *crying*."

### ⏱ 14–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child spells *happiest, sadder*(decoy — *sad* is a doubler, watch for it), *dried*, and *flying* (the *-ing* exception — keep the *y*). Read each back.
**Watch for:** keeping the *y* before *-er/-est/-ed*; wrongly changing *y→i* before *-ing*; applying the *y* rule to *sad* (no *y* — it's a doubler → *sadder*).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "the *y* needs to change" or "before *-ing* the *y* stays"; 3) step down — give the pair side by side to copy the change; 4) ONE hint — "is the suffix *-ing*? If not, change *y* to *i*"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (just one word); 6) keep it safe — "The *-ing* twist is tricky for everyone — let's check the suffix first."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Teach me the *y* rule — and don't forget the twist." → *listen for:* change *y* to *i* before *-er/-est/-ed*; keep the *y* before *-ing*.
**Process praise:** "You transformed the *y* into an *i* every time — and you remembered the tricky *-ing* twist that catches lots of people out."
**Revisit later:** include *happier/happiest* and *crying* in the Lesson 9 sort; bring back the *-ing* exception as a quick check next session.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** pairs given side by side (*happy → happi__*) so the child copies the change and adds the suffix. Avoid the *-ing* exception until the main rule is secure.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain *why* *crying* keeps the *y* (we don't want *ii*) and spell *trying, drier, busiest*.

**Extension (if time allows):** find two more *y*-ending words (e.g. *lucky, dirty*) and make them into "more/most" words (*luckier, dirtiest*).
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly changes *y→i* in at least three words (e.g. *happiest, cried, sadder*) and keeps the *y* in *flying/crying*.

## Lesson 9 — Suffix sort (which rule?) — retrieval
**English · Year 2 · Unit 2 (Spelling with suffixes)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose the right suffix rule for any root word."

**You will need:** a four-column sorting mat headed **Just add · Drop the e · Double · y→i**; root-word cards (*jump, hope, run, happy, bake, sit, play, cry*); suffix cards (*-ing, -ed*); whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** all four suffix rules from Lessons 5–8.
**Key vocabulary:** *root word, suffix, just add, drop the e, double the consonant, y → i*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** This is **retrieval** — the child decides WHICH rule each word needs, then applies it. The thinking routine: **look at the end of the root → choose the rule → spell it.** Catch the cross-rule confusions: *hope* (drop-e) vs *hop* (double); *play* (just-add — the *y* has a vowel before it) vs *cry* (y→i). Frame as the "Spelling Sorting Office".

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Welcome to the Spelling Sorting Office! Every word that arrives has to go in the right rule-bag: *Just add*, *Drop the e*, *Double*, or *y to i*. You're the sorting-office worker — your job is to read the end of each word and pop it in the correct bag. Mis-file one and the spelling goes wrong! Ready to sort?"

**Show:** the four-column mat with its headings; the stack of root cards.  **Do:** hold up *jump* and post it into "Just add" as a demo.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Quick recall — name me the four rules we've learned." → *listen for:* just add, drop the e, double the consonant, y→i.
*(If shaky: point to each column heading and give one example: *jump, hope, run, happy*.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Here's how I sort. I look at the END of the root and ask three quick questions. One — does it end in a silent *e*? Then DROP the e (*hope*). Two — is it a short vowel and one consonant, like *run*? Then DOUBLE. Three — does it end in *y*? Then *y → i*. If none of those, it just goes in JUST ADD (*jump, play*). Watch me sort *bake*: ends in silent *e* → Drop the e bag → *baking*."

**Show / Do:** think aloud and post *bake* (drop-e), *sit* (double), *cry* (y→i), *play* (just-add). For each, write the *-ing* form underneath.
**Emphasize:** "Look at the END of the word — that tells you the rule."

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Sort these with me. *Hope* — what's on the end? So which bag? Now spell *hope* + *-ing*. Next, *hop* — careful, this one's sneaky! What's different about it?"

**Do:** together sort *hope* (drop-e → *hoping*) then *hop* (double → *hopping*), drawing out the silent-*e*-vs-no-*e* difference. Then *happy* (y→i → *happier*) vs *play* (just-add → *playing*).
**Ask:** "Which rule for *run* + *-ing*?" → *listen for:* double the consonant → *running*.

### ⏱ 13–16 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which rule does *run* need to add *-ing*, and what do you get?"
- ✅ **Expected:** double the consonant → *running*. Then probe: *"How did you know — what's on the end of *run*?"* (one short vowel + one consonant).
- ❌ **If the child says "drop the e" / writes *runing*:** mis-sorted; no *e* to drop and didn't double. → **fix:** "Is there a silent *e* on *run*? No. Is it a short vowel and one consonant? Yes → double." Move the card to the right bag.
- ❌ **If the child writes *runned*-style errors or confuses with *play*:** rule mix-up. → **fix:** re-read the end of the word and walk the three questions in order.

### ⏱ 16–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child independently sorts all eight cards — *jump, hope, run, happy, bake, sit, play, cry* — into the four columns, then writes each with *-ing* or *-ed*.
**Watch for:** *hope* vs *hop* mix-ups; putting *play* in the y→i bag (it's just-add — vowel before the *y*); *cry* in just-add; forgetting to apply the rule when spelling.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't sort it for them; 2) name *why* — "let's check the end of this word again"; 3) step down — narrow to three columns or pre-group roughly; 4) ONE hint — ask just one of the three questions ("does it end in a silent *e*?"); 5) re-ask a smaller version (sort two cards only); 6) keep it safe — "Sorting takes a careful eye — read the end and we'll check together."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "You're a top sorting-office worker. Tell me the three questions you ask to choose the right bag." → *listen for:* silent *e*? → drop; short vowel + one consonant? → double; ends in *y*? → y→i; else → just add.
**Process praise:** "You read the end of every word and chose the right rule — even the sneaky *hope*-and-*hop* pair. That's careful, expert spelling."
**Revisit later:** keep a couple of these for a warm-up before Lesson 10; this routine feeds the **End-of-Unit 2 check** (spell *hopping, hoped, happier*).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** use three columns only (drop *y→i* until secure) and pre-group the cards roughly so the child confirms rather than sorts from scratch.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add a "trick" word that fits none of the four (e.g. *see → seeing* — already a vowel pair) and explain why.

**Extension (if time allows):** invent two new word cards, sort them, and explain the rule to the tutor.
**Assessment evidence:** child sorts at least six of eight words into the correct rule column and spells the suffixed forms accurately (esp. *hoping/hopping*).
