# Year 3 Geography (ages 8–9) — Scope & Sequence

## Year overview

Year 3 geography takes children **around the world through its climates and biomes** and into two contrasting region studies — **the Amazon rainforest in South America** and **the Mediterranean region of Italy in Europe** — then follows food along global **trade routes from Ghana (Africa)** to their own kitchen. It opens by taking Year 2's **map skills** (eight compass points, four-figure grid references, symbols, keys and scale) outdoors and into real atlases and digital maps — sixteen compass points, atlas indexes, satellite layers and comparing maps of different scales — and closes with **fieldwork** in the local area, adaptable to any town or country.

**Physical and human geography together.** Every unit links what the land and climate are like (physical) to how people live, work, trade and change places (human), and asks about **sustainability** — deforestation, water use, fair trade, and caring for local places.

**What this year assumes (from Year 2, ages 7–8):** continents and oceans; latitude, longitude, the Equator, hemispheres and time zones; the country's regions; rivers and the water cycle's role in rivers. **What Year 4 (ages 9–10) relies on:** confident four-figure grid references, atlas and digital-map skills, the climate-zone and biome framework, comparing regions fairly, and the fieldwork cycle — used in Year 4's units on coasts, the Colorado River region of North America, Antarctica, mountains at altitude, and resources and energy.

**Horizontal links:** the water cycle is met in Year 2 (rivers) and taught fully in Science (Unit 6, Term 2) — geography's Term 1 water unit recalls the basics and focuses on water supply and use; the Roman Mediterranean links to History; *Journey to the River Sea* and *The Wild Robot* on the English shelf link to the rainforest and biomes.

Lessons are **40 minutes**, one a week, with at least three lessons outdoors.

## Unit 1 — Map skills: finding our way

**Essential question:** How do maps help us find, describe and plan routes to places near and far?

**Key vocabulary:** map, plan, key, symbol, scale, compass, north, north-east, east, south-east, south, south-west, west, north-west, grid, grid reference, easting, northing, route, aerial photograph, atlas, index, north-north-east (and the other in-between points), sixteen-point compass, magnetic compass, satellite image, layer, large-scale, small-scale

### Lesson 1 — Sixteen compass points: giving precise directions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use the sixteen points of the compass and a magnetic compass to describe directions more precisely." (UK NC KS2 Ge (eight points of a compass; use maps))
- **Hook:** "A ship is told to sail 'north-east', but the harbour really lies halfway between north-east and north. After a long voyage, will it arrive?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of the eight points (Year 2) on a chalk compass rose. New: the sixteen points — the directions halfway between each pair (NNE, ENE, ESE, SSE, SSW, WSW, WNW, NNW), named with the nearest of N, E, S or W first (so NNE is closer to north, ENE closer to east); a magnetic compass needle points to magnetic north, so line the map's north arrow up with it before reading directions (set maps and pace counts are practised in PE orienteering). Pairs then give and follow leg-by-leg directions between control points in the school grounds using the sixteen points.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why would a pilot or sailor say 'north-north-east' rather than just 'north-east'?" → it is more precise; over a long distance even a small difference in direction takes you a long way from where you meant to go
- **Differentiation:** support: a compass-rose card with the sixteen points colour-coded (main, half-way, in-between) / stretch: write a five-leg treasure route for a partner using at least three in-between points
- **Materials:** magnetic compasses, school maps, control markers, chalk
- **Joy:** "Compass-rose twister": call a direction and everyone jumps to it

### Lesson 2 — Atlas skills: finding any place in the world
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use an atlas's contents and index to find any place, and choose the right kind of map for a question." (UK NC KS2 Ge (use maps, atlases and globes; four-figure grid references))
- **Hook:** "There are thousands of places in this atlas. How can you find Manaus in under a minute?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of four-figure grid references (Year 2) on a local map. New: atlas skills — the contents page, the index (page number plus a letter–number grid square, e.g. '48 C3'), and different map types (physical, political, climate, population). Races to find places from the index; then pairs decide which map type answers questions such as 'Which is the highest mountain in Africa?' or 'Which country borders Ghana to the west?'.
- **Check for understanding:** "You want to know whether Italy is mountainous. Would you use the political map or the physical map of Europe? Explain." → the physical map, because it shows height with colours and marks mountain ranges; the political map mainly shows countries, borders and cities
- **Differentiation:** support: an index card with the page and grid square already highlighted / stretch: find the same city on two maps of different scales and explain what each shows better
- **Materials:** class sets of the same school atlas, index-race cards, local grid maps
- **Joy:** "Atlas race": teams find five mystery places fastest

### Lesson 3 — Digital maps and satellite images
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a digital map's layers, zoom and measuring tool to find and compare information about a place." (UK NC KS2 Ge (use digital/computer mapping))
- **Hook:** "The same place can look completely different depending on which button you press. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of symbols, keys and aerial photos (Year 2). New: on tablets, children switch between map, satellite and terrain layers for the school and one faraway place (e.g. Manaus or Rome); zoom in and out to see what appears and disappears; use the measuring tool to find a straight-line distance; and record one thing each layer shows best.
- **Check for understanding:** "You need to know whether a path goes uphill. Which layer would you use, and why not the satellite view?" → the terrain layer, because it shows the shape and height of the land; a satellite photo shows what is on the surface but not clearly how high or steep it is
- **Differentiation:** support: a step-by-step card with screenshots of each button / stretch: compare a satellite image from years ago with today's (using a historical-imagery tool) and describe one change
- **Materials:** tablets or computers with a free digital map, printed screenshots for backup, recording sheets
- **Joy:** "Satellite spy": identify mystery places from above

### Lesson 4 — Large-scale and small-scale maps
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare maps of different scales, choose the right one for a job, and measure a winding route with a scale bar." (UK NC KS2 Ge (use maps; scale))
- **Hook:** "Why can't you find your school on a map of the whole world — or the whole world on a map of your street?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval: using a scale bar to measure a straight distance (Year 2). New: lay out four maps of the same place at different scales (street plan, town, country, world); discuss how detail disappears as the area grows. Measure a winding walking route with string along the scale bar and compare it with the straight-line distance.
- **Check for understanding:** "You are planning a walk to the park. Would you use the country map or the street map? Explain using the word 'detail'." → the street map — it is a large-scale map covering a small area, so it shows individual streets and paths in detail; the country map covers a huge area and leaves those details out
- **Differentiation:** support: scale bar with ruler marks and two maps only / stretch: calculate the time to walk a route at a given speed
- **Materials:** maps, string, rulers
- **Joy:** "Route race": plan the shortest route

### Lesson 5 — Unit review: plan an expedition
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use compass directions, grid references, symbols and scale together to plan a route." (UK NC KS2 Ge (use maps, atlases and digital mapping))
- **Hook:** "Plan a safe walking route to a local landmark for the class trip."
- **Key activity:** Groups plan a route on a local map, describe it with grid references and compass directions, measure its length using scale, and identify features on the way using the key; swap plans and check.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give the grid reference of your destination and describe the first part of your route using compass directions." → a correct four-figure reference and accurate compass-direction description
- **Differentiation:** support: route template with checkboxes / stretch: plan an alternative route that avoids a busy road and compare distances
- **Materials:** local maps, rulers, compasses
- **Joy:** follow the best route on a real walk if possible

**End-of-unit check:** Name and use the sixteen compass points to give precise directions; find three places with an atlas index; choose the right map type and scale for a task; give four-figure grid references and measure a winding route with a scale bar.

## Unit 2 — Water: from rain to tap

**Essential question:** Where does our water come from, and why does access to clean water differ around the world?

**Key vocabulary:** water supply, reservoir, well, treatment, pipe, drought, sanitation, water use, conservation, rainfall

### Lesson 6 — Where does our water come from?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can trace the journey of water from rain to our taps." (UK NC KS2 Ge (human geography: distribution of natural resources))
- **Hook:** "Turn on a tap. Where was that water a week ago?"
- **Key activity:** Recall the water cycle from Year 2's rivers unit (Science teaches it fully in Term 2); follow water through catchment, reservoir or aquifer, treatment works and pipes to school; locate the local reservoir or source on a map.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must water be treated before it reaches our taps?" → to remove dirt and germs so it's safe to drink
- **Differentiation:** support: a flow diagram to complete / stretch: explain what happens to water after it goes down the drain
- **Materials:** maps, flow diagrams
- **Joy:** filter muddy water through sand and gravel (not for drinking)

### Lesson 7 — Water around the world
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare access to clean water in different places and explain the physical and human reasons for the differences." (UK NC KS2 Ge (human and physical geography; distribution of resources))
- **Hook:** "Millions of children around the world spend hours every day fetching water. Why — when others just turn a tap?"
- **Key activity:** Groups study case-study cards (a village in northern Kenya, a city in India, a town in the Netherlands, the child's own town) with rainfall data and infrastructure facts; mark them on a world map; sort reasons into physical (low or seasonal rainfall, drought) and human (no pipes or treatment, cost, conflict). Children carry a bucket holding a safe amount (3–5 litres) for 50 m, then calculate how many trips it would take to carry the 20 litres or more a family may need each day.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one physical and one human reason why a place might lack clean water." → physical: very little or unreliable rain; human: no money for pipes, pumps or treatment works
- **Differentiation:** support: picture case-study cards with key facts highlighted / stretch: explain how building a village well or rainwater tank changes daily life, especially for girls who often fetch water
- **Materials:** case-study cards, world map, rainfall charts, buckets
- **Joy:** the playground bucket challenge

### Lesson 8 — How much water do we use?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can measure and estimate household water use and plan realistic ways to reduce it." (UK NC KS2 Ge (sustainability); links to maths (capacity))
- **Hook:** "How many litres does one shower use? A toilet flush? Brushing your teeth with the tap running?"
- **Key activity:** Measure a tap running for one minute into a bucket with a measuring jug; use published estimates for showers, flushes and washing machines; children keep a one-day tally at home and calculate a class total. Discuss 'virtual water' in food (e.g. growing cotton or beef).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is one change that would save the most water in your home, and how do you know?" → e.g. shorter showers or turning off the tap while brushing, supported by the litres measured or calculated
- **Differentiation:** support: a tally chart with pictures and pre-filled litres per activity / stretch: calculate how much water the class would save in a year from one pledge
- **Materials:** buckets, measuring jugs, stopwatch, tally charts
- **Joy:** "Water detectives" pledge poster

### Lesson 9 — Unit review: water for everyone
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain where our water comes from and propose fair, sustainable ways to share it." (UK NC KS2 Ge)
- **Hook:** "A town is running short of water in a drought. What should it do?"
- **Key activity:** Groups act as a town council with roles (farmer, factory, family, nature reserve) and a limited water 'budget' in cubes; they allocate water and justify choices; then answer mastery questions about the supply journey and world differences.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain one way the town could save water and one way it could increase its supply." → save: fix leaks, limit garden watering; increase: rainwater harvesting, a new reservoir or recycling wastewater
- **Differentiation:** support: role cards with suggested arguments / stretch: explain the trade-off between farmers' and nature's water needs
- **Materials:** role cards, cubes as water budget
- **Joy:** "Drought council" role-play

**End-of-unit check:** Trace water from rain to tap; give physical and human reasons for unequal water access; propose two sustainable ways to save or increase water supply.

## Unit 3 — Climate zones and biomes

**Essential question:** Why is the world hot in some places and cold in others — and how does climate shape the living world?

**Key vocabulary:** weather, climate, climate zone, tropical, dry (arid), temperate, polar, mountain, Equator, Tropic of Cancer, Tropic of Capricorn, latitude, biome, rainforest, desert, savannah, grassland, temperate forest, taiga, tundra, adaptation, climate graph

### Lesson 10 — Weather or climate?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the difference between weather and climate using data." (UK NC KS2 Ge (climate zones))
- **Hook:** "Today it's raining in Cairo. Does that mean Cairo has a wet climate?"
- **Key activity:** Use a week of local weather observations (the class records them for a week before the lesson, or uses a weather website) as 'weather' and 30-year averages as 'climate'. Sort statements into weather or climate. Read a first climate graph (bars for rainfall, line for temperature).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can't one rainy day tell us Cairo's climate?" → weather is the day-to-day conditions; climate is the average pattern over many years — Cairo's climate is hot and dry overall
- **Differentiation:** support: statement cards with 'today / usually' clue words / stretch: read a climate graph and describe the wettest and hottest months
- **Materials:** climate graphs, weather data, sorting cards
- **Joy:** "Weather or climate?" quick-fire game with thumbs up/down

### Lesson 11 — Why latitude matters
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why places near the Equator are hotter than places near the poles." (UK NC KS2 Ge (latitude; climate zones))
- **Hook:** "If we travel south from the Arctic to the Equator along one line of longitude, will it get warmer all the way?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of Year 2's torch-and-globe demonstration (direct rays near the Equator, spread-out rays near the poles). New: a temperature transect along about 30° E. Pairs plot average yearly temperatures on a bar chart ordered by latitude — Murmansk (69° N, about 0 °C), St Petersburg (60° N, about 6 °C), Kyiv (50° N, about 8 °C), Istanbul (41° N, about 15 °C), Cairo (30° N, about 22 °C), Khartoum (16° N, about 30 °C) and Kampala (0°, about 22 °C) — describe the pattern and hunt for the surprise. Then colour a blank world map with climate zones (tropical, dry, temperate, polar, mountain).
- **Check for understanding:** "Temperatures rise from Murmansk to Khartoum, but Kampala, almost on the Equator, is cooler than Khartoum. Explain both parts of the pattern." → nearer the Equator the Sun's rays are more direct, so it is generally warmer; Kampala is about 1,200 m above sea level, and higher places are cooler
- **Differentiation:** support: a bar chart with the axes and first three bars drawn, and a map with zones outlined to colour / stretch: explain why mountains near the Equator, such as Kilimanjaro, can have snow
- **Materials:** globes, torches, blank world maps, atlases, city temperature cards, squared paper
- **Joy:** "Surprise city" — spot the city that breaks the pattern

### Lesson 12 — What is a biome?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the world's main biomes and locate them on a map." (UK NC KS2 Ge (biomes and vegetation belts))
- **Hook:** "If you flew from the North Pole to the Equator, how would the land below change?"
- **Key activity:** Photo cards of biomes (tundra, taiga, temperate forest, grassland, desert, savannah, tropical rainforest); match to a world map and to climate zones; notice the 'bands' either side of the Equator.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are deserts often found near the Tropics rather than at the Equator?" → the air there is usually dry and sinking, so little rain falls
- **Differentiation:** support: picture-to-map matching with a partly completed map / stretch: explain why the same biome appears on different continents
- **Materials:** photo cards, world maps, atlases
- **Joy:** "Flight from pole to Equator": a floor map children 'fly' over

### Lesson 13 — Adaptation: built for the biome
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how plants and animals are adapted to their biome." (UK NC KS2 Ge; links to Science)
- **Hook:** "Why does a camel have wide feet and a polar bear black skin under white fur?"
- **Key activity:** Adaptation cards; match animals and plants to biomes with reasons; design an imaginary creature for a biome.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain one adaptation that helps a cactus survive in a desert." → e.g. thick stems store water, or spines instead of leaves reduce water loss
- **Differentiation:** support: card pairs with pictures / stretch: design a creature for two biomes and justify each adaptation
- **Materials:** adaptation cards, drawing materials
- **Joy:** "Creature designer" gallery

### Lesson 14 — Biome case study: the hot desert
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the physical features of a hot desert and explain how people live and make a living there." (UK NC KS2 Ge (physical and human geography))
- **Hook:** "Can people live where it hardly ever rains? In the Sahara, they have for thousands of years."
- **Key activity:** Read a climate graph for a Saharan town (very low rainfall, hot days, cool nights); explore photos of sand seas, rocky plateaus and oases; learn how an oasis forms where groundwater reaches the surface. Case studies of ways of life: Tuareg herders and traders, oasis farmers growing date palms, modern mining and solar power.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give two ways people make a living in the Sahara and explain how each depends on the desert's geography." → e.g. oasis farming depends on underground water reaching the surface; herding camels and goats depends on animals adapted to heat and little water
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards with one fact each and a partly labelled oasis diagram / stretch: explain why desert nights are cold even though the days are very hot (clear skies let heat escape)
- **Materials:** climate graphs, photos, maps of North Africa
- **Joy:** "Desert survival kit" challenge: choose 5 items and justify them

### Lesson 15 — Biome case study: the Arctic tundra
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the tundra and polar regions and explain how climate change is affecting people and wildlife there." (UK NC KS2 Ge (sustainability; human and physical))
- **Hook:** "Why is the Arctic warming faster than almost anywhere else on Earth?"
- **Key activity:** Climate graph for an Arctic town (very cold, short summer); permafrost, low plants, no trees; Inuit communities in Canada and Greenland — traditional hunting on sea ice and modern life. Look at simple sea-ice data showing summer ice shrinking. Demonstrate why white ice reflects heat and dark sea absorbs it (black and white paper in the sun with thermometers).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is happening to Arctic sea ice, and why does it matter to people and animals?" → summer sea ice is shrinking as the planet warms; polar bears hunt from it and Inuit communities travel and hunt on it
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards and a simplified bar chart of ice extent / stretch: explain the 'feedback': less white ice means more dark sea absorbing heat, which melts more ice
- **Materials:** climate graphs, photos, black and white card, thermometers
- **Joy:** the black-and-white card heat test in the sun

### Lesson 16 — Unit review: biome explorers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare two biomes using climate graphs, adaptations and human activity." (UK NC KS2 Ge)
- **Hook:** "A travel company needs expert guides. Can you compare two biomes well enough to prepare travellers?"
- **Key activity:** Pairs choose two contrasting biomes; compare them using climate graphs (temperature and rainfall), a map location and latitude, two adaptations of plants or animals, and how people live there; make a two-page 'explorer's guide'.
- **Check for understanding:** "Use the climate graphs to compare the two biomes. What explains the biggest difference?" → compares temperature and rainfall with values and links the difference to latitude or distance from the sea
- **Differentiation:** support: graphs pre-annotated with the highest and lowest values / stretch: explain why two places at the same latitude can have different biomes (e.g. altitude, ocean currents, distance from the sea)
- **Materials:** climate graphs, atlases, guide templates
- **Joy:** "Explorer's guide" pitched to a travel-company 'panel'

**End-of-unit check:** Explain the difference between weather and climate; explain why the Equator is hotter than the poles; locate five biomes; describe two adaptations with reasons.

## Unit 4 — Region study: the Amazon rainforest and South America

**Essential question:** Why is the Amazon so important to the whole planet, and who should decide its future?

**Key vocabulary:** South America, Amazon, rainforest, canopy, emergent, understorey, forest floor, biodiversity, tributary, Indigenous, Manaus, deforestation, cattle ranching, soya, sustainable, conservation

### Lesson 17 — South America and the Amazon basin
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can locate South America's countries, physical features and the Amazon basin on maps." (UK NC KS2 Ge (locational knowledge: South America))
- **Hook:** "The Amazon rainforest covers parts of nine countries. Can you find them all?"
- **Key activity:** Atlas work: name and locate South America's countries and capitals, the Andes, the Amazon River and its tributaries, the Equator. Colour the Amazon basin across Brazil, Peru, Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Bolivia, Guyana, Suriname and French Guiana (part of France).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is so much of the Amazon rainforest near the Equator?" → tropical rainforests grow where it is hot and wet all year — conditions found near the Equator
- **Differentiation:** support: a map with country outlines numbered and a word bank / stretch: use grid references to locate Manaus and explain why a big city grew in the middle of the forest (river port)
- **Materials:** atlases, blank maps of South America
- **Joy:** "Nine-country challenge" map race

### Lesson 18 — Layers of the rainforest
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the layers of the rainforest and how living things are adapted to each." (UK NC KS2 Ge (physical geography: vegetation))
- **Hook:** "Only a tiny fraction of sunlight reaches the rainforest floor. What lives down there?"
- **Key activity:** Build a class rainforest in the corner (emergent, canopy, understorey, forest floor) with children's drawings of plants and animals placed in the correct layer; read climate data for Manaus (hot, wet year-round).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do many rainforest plants have huge leaves low down but small leaves at the top?" → low down there is little light, so large leaves catch more of it; at the top there is plenty of light and more wind
- **Differentiation:** support: a layer diagram with example animals to place / stretch: explain why the soil is often thin and poor even though the forest is so rich (nutrients are held in the plants)
- **Materials:** paper, paint, climate graph, layer diagram
- **Joy:** the class 'rainforest corner' with a sound-track of forest sounds

### Lesson 19 — People of the Amazon
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how Indigenous peoples and city dwellers live in the Amazon and depend on the forest." (UK NC KS2 Ge (human geography))
- **Hook:** "Hundreds of Indigenous peoples live in the Amazon — and there is also a city of about two million people. How do their lives compare?"
- **Key activity:** Case studies: an Indigenous community (e.g. the Yanomami) — farming small forest gardens, hunting, fishing, deep knowledge of plants; and Manaus — a river port and industrial city. Compare with a Venn diagram; discuss respect and rights to land.
- **Check for understanding:** "How do Indigenous communities use the forest without destroying it?" → they clear small areas, move gardens so the forest regrows, and take only what they need
- **Differentiation:** support: picture-supported case-study cards / stretch: explain why Indigenous land rights matter for protecting the forest
- **Materials:** case-study cards, photos, Venn diagrams
- **Joy:** "Market in Manaus": explore real products from the forest (açaí, Brazil nuts, rubber)

### Lesson 20 — Why is the rainforest disappearing?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the causes of deforestation in the Amazon using maps and photographs." (UK NC KS2 Ge (human impact; land use))
- **Hook:** "Satellite photos show a 'fishbone' pattern cutting into the forest. What is making it?"
- **Key activity:** Compare satellite images of the same area over time (roads with side-clearings — the 'fishbone'); sort causes: cattle ranching, soya farming, logging, mining, roads and dams. Discuss who benefits and who loses.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does building a road into the forest often lead to much more deforestation?" → roads let loggers, farmers and ranchers reach new areas, so clearing spreads out from them
- **Differentiation:** support: cause cards with pictures to sort / stretch: explain why people far away (e.g. buying beef or soya) are connected to deforestation
- **Materials:** satellite images (e.g. from NASA Earth Observatory), cause cards
- **Joy:** "Fishbone detectives": spot deforestation on satellite images

### Lesson 21 — The Amazon and the planet
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why the Amazon matters to the whole planet, not just South America." (UK NC KS2 Ge (sustainability))
- **Hook:** "The Amazon makes some of its own rain. What happens if the trees go?"
- **Key activity:** Explore how trees release water vapour that forms clouds ('flying rivers' carry moisture across South America); trees store carbon; the Amazon is home to a huge share of the world's species. Simple demonstration: a plastic bag tied over a leafy branch collects water released by the leaves.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give two reasons why the Amazon matters to people who live far away from it." → it stores carbon that would otherwise warm the planet, and it helps make rain and holds a vast number of species
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards with one reason each / stretch: explain the link between Science's water cycle and the rainforest making its own rain
- **Materials:** plastic bags, string, leafy branch, diagrams
- **Joy:** the 'tree sweat' bag experiment

### Lesson 22 — Debate: who should decide the Amazon's future?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present and respond to different viewpoints on the Amazon's future." (UK NC KS2 Ge; links to English (discussion))
- **Hook:** "A new road is planned. A rancher, an Indigenous leader, a scientist, a city worker and a government minister all have a view."
- **Key activity:** Groups take a role with an evidence card, prepare arguments, and hold a public meeting; the class then votes and discusses fair and sustainable compromises.
- **Check for understanding:** "Summarise the strongest argument from a viewpoint different from your own." → accurately restates another role's main point and its evidence
- **Differentiation:** support: role cards with sentence starters / stretch: propose a compromise that meets the needs of at least three roles
- **Materials:** role cards, evidence cards, name badges
- **Joy:** "Town-hall meeting" in Manaus

### Lesson 23 — Unit review: rainforest guide
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show mastery of the Amazon region study through a guide that links physical and human geography." (UK NC KS2 Ge)
- **Hook:** "Write the guidebook page every visitor to the Amazon should read."
- **Key activity:** Each child makes a one-page guide: location with a map and grid reference, the layers and climate, the people, the threats and one sustainable solution.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain how the rainforest's climate, its people and deforestation are connected." → links the hot, wet climate to the forest's richness, people's dependence on it, and how clearing threatens both
- **Differentiation:** support: a guide template with sections and word bank / stretch: include a comparison with a forest in your own country
- **Materials:** templates, atlases
- **Joy:** guides displayed in the class 'travel agency'

**End-of-unit check:** Locate the Amazon and at least five countries it covers; describe the rainforest's layers and climate; explain two causes of deforestation and two reasons the Amazon matters to the planet; summarise a viewpoint different from your own.

## Unit 5 — Region study: the Mediterranean and Italy

**Essential question:** How does a Mediterranean climate shape the land, the food and the way people live — and how does it compare with the Amazon and with home?

**Key vocabulary:** Europe, Mediterranean, Italy, peninsula, Alps, Apennines, Po Valley, island, Mediterranean climate, olive, vine, citrus, tourism, coast, compare, similarity, difference

### Lesson 24 — Italy on the map
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can locate Italy and its main physical and human features and describe its position in Europe." (UK NC KS2 Ge (locational knowledge: Europe))
- **Hook:** "Italy is shaped like a boot kicking a ball. Which island is the ball?"
- **Key activity:** Atlas work: the Italian peninsula, Sicily and Sardinia, the Alps (north), the Apennines (the 'spine'), the Po Valley, the Mediterranean, Adriatic and Tyrrhenian seas, and cities (Rome, Milan, Naples, Venice). Use four-figure grid references on a map of Italy and compare Italy's latitude with the Amazon's.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do you think Italy's biggest farming area is the Po Valley rather than the Apennines?" → the Po Valley is flat, with fertile soil and river water; the Apennines are steep and rocky
- **Differentiation:** support: a map with features numbered and a word bank / stretch: explain how Italy's long coastline and many islands affect its climate and economy
- **Materials:** atlases, maps of Italy, grid-reference cards
- **Joy:** "Boot and ball" map puzzle

### Lesson 25 — The Mediterranean climate
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe and explain the Mediterranean climate using a climate graph." (UK NC KS2 Ge (climate zones))
- **Hook:** "Why does Italy have hot, dry summers but rainy winters — the opposite of many places?"
- **Key activity:** Read a climate graph for Rome or Palermo; compare with Manaus and with the child's own town; identify the pattern (hot dry summers, mild wet winters); locate other places with this climate (California, central Chile, South Africa's Western Cape, south-west Australia).
- **Check for understanding:** "Use the climate graph to describe Rome's summer and winter. How is it different from Manaus?" → Rome: hot, dry summer and mild, wetter winter; Manaus: hot and wet all year
- **Differentiation:** support: graph with the summer months highlighted / stretch: explain why the Mediterranean climate appears on the west coasts of several continents at similar latitudes
- **Materials:** climate graphs, world map
- **Joy:** "Climate twins" matching game

### Lesson 26 — Farming and food
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the Mediterranean climate shapes farming and food." (UK NC KS2 Ge (land use; economic activity))
- **Hook:** "Olives, grapes, lemons and tomatoes — why do they grow so well around the Mediterranean?"
- **Key activity:** Taste-test (check allergies) olives, tomatoes and citrus; link crops to the climate (deep roots and waxy leaves for dry summers); map where crops are grown; follow olive oil from tree to bottle.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain why olive trees suit a Mediterranean climate." → they have deep roots and small, tough leaves that lose little water, so they survive hot, dry summers
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards linking crop features to climate / stretch: compare Mediterranean farming with cattle ranching in the Amazon — which is more suited to its climate?
- **Materials:** food samples, crop picture cards, maps
- **Joy:** Mediterranean tasting table

### Lesson 27 — Tourism and the coast
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the benefits and problems of tourism in a Mediterranean region." (UK NC KS2 Ge (human geography; sustainability))
- **Hook:** "Venice welcomes millions of visitors a year. Is that good news for people who live there?"
- **Key activity:** Case study of Venice (and a quieter coastal village); sort impacts into benefits (jobs, money) and problems (crowding, rising house prices, pollution from cruise ships); discuss sustainable tourism.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one benefit and one problem that tourism brings to Venice." → benefit: jobs and income for shops, hotels and guides; problem: crowding and higher prices that push residents out
- **Differentiation:** support: impact cards with pictures / stretch: design a rule that would make tourism fairer and explain its trade-off
- **Materials:** photos, impact cards
- **Joy:** "Tourist board" poster for sustainable visiting

### Lesson 28 — Compare: Italy, the Amazon and home
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare three places using physical and human features and explain similarities and differences." (UK NC KS2 Ge (understand similarities and differences through human and physical geography))
- **Hook:** "Three places, three climates, three ways of life. What connects them?"
- **Key activity:** Comparison grid: location and latitude, climate, landscape, farming, jobs, threats and sustainability; groups present one similarity and one difference and explain the cause.
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose one difference between the Amazon and the Mediterranean region and explain its cause." → e.g. the Amazon has rain all year and the Mediterranean has dry summers because of their different latitudes and climate zones
- **Differentiation:** support: comparison grid with some boxes filled / stretch: find a surprising similarity (e.g. both face pressure from farming and tourism/roads) and explain it
- **Materials:** comparison grids, atlases, climate graphs
- **Joy:** "Three-place postcard" set

**End-of-unit check:** Locate Italy's main physical features; describe the Mediterranean climate from a graph; explain one link between climate and farming; compare Italy, the Amazon and your home area with reasons.

## Unit 6 — Trade: from cocoa farm to chocolate bar

**Essential question:** How do the things we buy connect us to people around the world — and is that trade fair?

**Key vocabulary:** trade, import, export, supply chain, producer, consumer, cocoa, Ghana, West Africa, price, profit, Fairtrade, cooperative, fair, sustainable

### Lesson 29 — Where does our food come from?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can trace where everyday foods come from and show trade links on a world map." (UK NC KS2 Ge (economic activity including trade links))
- **Hook:** "Your breakfast has travelled farther than most people do on holiday. Let's prove it."
- **Key activity:** Children read labels on food packaging (bananas, rice, tea, chocolate, coffee, tinned fish) brought from home or supplied; plot countries of origin on a world map with string to the school; tally imports by continent.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we import bananas and cocoa instead of growing them here?" → they need a hot, wet tropical climate that most other countries don't have (links to climate zones)
- **Differentiation:** support: labels with the country highlighted / stretch: calculate or estimate the distance of the furthest food and discuss its environmental cost
- **Materials:** food packaging, world map, string, pins
- **Joy:** "Food miles" string map

### Lesson 30 — Cocoa in Ghana
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how cocoa is grown in Ghana and the lives of cocoa farmers." (UK NC KS2 Ge (place knowledge: a region in Africa))
- **Hook:** "Most of the world's cocoa is grown in West Africa, and Ghana is one of the biggest producers. Many cocoa farmers rarely, if ever, eat the chocolate made from their beans. Why might that be?"
- **Key activity:** Locate Ghana; climate graph (tropical); cocoa-growing steps (pods, beans, fermenting, drying, selling); photos and a short approved video of a cocoa farm; discuss farmers' incomes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does cocoa grow well in Ghana?" → Ghana is near the Equator with a hot, wet climate that cocoa trees need, usually in the shade of taller trees
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture sequence of cocoa growing / stretch: explain why it's unfair that many farmers earn very little even though chocolate is expensive
- **Materials:** maps, cocoa pods (or photos), climate graph
- **Joy:** handle cocoa beans and smell raw cocoa nibs

### Lesson 31 — The chocolate supply chain
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain who gets what share of the money from a chocolate bar." (UK NC KS2 Ge; links to maths (fractions of amounts))
- **Hook:** "If a chocolate bar costs 100 cents, how much do you think the cocoa farmer gets?"
- **Key activity:** Supply-chain role-play (farmer, buyer, shipper, factory, shop, government); share out 100 counters using published approximate shares; discuss who has most and least power.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which part of the supply chain gets the smallest share, and why might that be?" → often the farmer — many small farmers sell to powerful buyers and have little choice about price
- **Differentiation:** support: role cards with numbers given / stretch: calculate each share as a fraction and a decimal of a dollar
- **Materials:** counters, role cards
- **Joy:** supply-chain role-play

### Lesson 32 — Fair trade and making a difference
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Fairtrade and cooperatives aim to make trade fairer, and plan an action." (UK NC KS2 Ge; global citizenship)
- **Hook:** "Can shoppers far away make life better for farmers in Ghana?"
- **Key activity:** Learn how Fairtrade sets a minimum price and a premium for communities (e.g. schools, clean water); case study of a cocoa cooperative (e.g. Kuapa Kokoo in Ghana, farmer-owned); find the Fairtrade mark on products; plan a class action (a fair-trade stall, a letter to the school kitchen).
- **Check for understanding:** "How does the Fairtrade premium help a whole community, not just one farmer?" → the extra money is spent on shared projects like schools, wells or health clinics
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards explaining minimum price and premium / stretch: evaluate whether buying Fairtrade alone is enough, with reasons
- **Materials:** products with and without the Fairtrade mark, case-study cards
- **Joy:** class fair-trade tasting stall

**End-of-unit check:** Explain why we import foods like cocoa; describe how cocoa is grown in Ghana; explain who gets the money from a chocolate bar; explain how Fairtrade aims to make trade fairer.

## Unit 7 — Fieldwork: investigating our local area

**Essential question:** How can we find out what our local area is like and how it could be improved?

**Key vocabulary:** fieldwork, enquiry question, hypothesis, data, tally, survey, traffic count, noise, litter, sketch map, photograph, conclusion, improve

### Lesson 33 — Planning a fieldwork enquiry
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a local fieldwork enquiry with a question, a prediction and methods." (UK NC KS2 Ge (fieldwork: observe, measure, record))
- **Hook:** "Is our school street a pleasant, safe place to walk? How could we find out, not just guess?"
- **Key activity:** The fieldwork cycle: question → predict → collect data → present → conclude → act. Groups choose a question (traffic, noise, litter, green spaces) and plan methods (tally, noise scale or app, litter count, photos, questionnaire), sites and timings; risk assessment with the teacher.
- **Check for understanding:** "What data will you collect, and why is it the right data for your question?" → names a measurable method directly linked to the question (e.g. a 10-minute traffic count to answer 'Is the street busy?')
- **Differentiation:** support: a planning template with method options to tick / stretch: plan a comparison between two sites and explain how to make it fair (same time, same length of count)
- **Materials:** planning templates, local maps, clipboards
- **Joy:** "Fieldwork team" badges and roles

### Lesson 34 — Collecting data in the field
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can collect accurate data outdoors using tallies, measurements, sketch maps and photographs." (UK NC KS2 Ge (fieldwork))
- **Hook:** "Out into the real world — scientists of places!"
- **Key activity:** Supervised walk to sites; groups collect data (traffic tallies, noise ratings or sound-meter readings, litter counts, environmental quality scores), draw a sketch map and take photos; note anything surprising. Safety: adults at every group, high-visibility vests.
- **Check for understanding:** "What surprised you while collecting data, and could it affect your results?" → names a surprise (e.g. a bus at school time) and how it might skew results
- **Differentiation:** support: tally sheets with pictures / stretch: record time and weather and explain why they matter
- **Materials:** clipboards, tally sheets, sound-meter app, cameras, high-visibility vests
- **Joy:** the fieldwork walk itself

### Lesson 35 — Presenting findings and making a difference
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present fieldwork data, draw a conclusion and propose an improvement to our local area." (UK NC KS2 Ge (present findings; sustainability))
- **Hook:** "Your data can change things. Who needs to hear it?"
- **Key activity:** Groups make bar charts and annotated maps/photos; compare results with predictions; write a conclusion and a proposal (e.g. a new crossing, bins, planting) in a letter to the local council or head teacher.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is your conclusion, and what evidence supports your proposal?" → a conclusion that answers the question with data, and a proposal justified by the data
- **Differentiation:** support: graph axes and letter template provided / stretch: evaluate the reliability of your data and suggest what you'd do differently
- **Materials:** squared paper, maps, photos, letter paper
- **Joy:** letters sent to the council or head, with any reply shared

**End-of-unit check:** Plan a fieldwork enquiry with a question and methods; collect and present data accurately; write a conclusion and an evidence-based proposal for improving the local area.

## Books & resources

- ***Journey to the River Sea* by Eva Ibbotson** — a novel set in Manaus and the Amazon in 1910; a rich stretch read for the Amazon unit.
- ***The Great Kapok Tree* by Lynne Cherry** — a picture book in which rainforest animals persuade a man not to cut down a tree; the perfect hook for the deforestation debate.
- ***Atlas of Adventures* by Rachel Williams and Lucy Letherland** — a beautifully illustrated world atlas of places and experiences; sparks curiosity about every continent.
- ***Maps* by Aleksandra Mizielińska and Daniel Mizieliński** — hand-drawn maps of countries packed with detail on landscape, food and culture; ideal for place knowledge.
- ***If the World Were a Village* by David J. Smith** — shrinks the world to 100 people to show inequality in water, food and wealth; supports the water and trade units.
- ***Wangari's Trees of Peace* by Jeanette Winter** — shared with Science; an African environmental hero for the sustainability thread.
- **A good school atlas** (e.g. *Oxford School Atlas* or a current national equivalent) and **globes** — used in almost every lesson.
- **Digital mapping** — Google Earth / satellite views and national mapping agencies' free online maps (e.g. Ordnance Survey's free resources in the UK) for aerial photographs and grid references.
- **NASA Earth Observatory** — free satellite images showing change over time (deforestation, sea ice).
- **Fairtrade Foundation / Fairtrade International** education resources — accurate information on cocoa farming and the Fairtrade premium.
- **Royal Geographical Society and Geographical Association** — primary fieldwork guides and resources.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Map skills** — uses sixteen compass points and a magnetic compass to give precise directions; uses an atlas index, digital map layers, four-figure grid references, keys and scale; chooses maps of the right scale. *See it:* the compass-rose trail and the expedition route plan.
2. **Weather vs climate** — explains the difference and reads a climate graph. *See it:* describes and compares two climate graphs.
3. **Climate zones and biomes** — explains why latitude affects temperature and locates the main biomes with examples of adaptations. *See it:* a labelled world biome map.
4. **Water** — traces water from rain to tap and explains unequal access with physical and human reasons.
5. **The Amazon** — locates it, describes its layers, climate and people, and explains the causes and effects of deforestation.
6. **The Mediterranean** — describes its climate and farming and compares Italy with the Amazon and home. *See it:* the three-place comparison.
7. **Trade** — explains why foods are imported and how Fairtrade aims to make trade fairer.
8. **Fieldwork** — plans an enquiry, collects and presents data, and draws an evidence-based conclusion. *See it:* the local-area proposal.

## Teacher guidance

**Maps every lesson.** Keep an atlas or globe open in every lesson and ask children to locate each place they meet using continents, lines of latitude and grid references.

**Common misconceptions to watch for.**
- *Weather and climate are the same* — climate is the long-term average pattern.
- *It's hotter at the Equator because it's closer to the Sun* — it's because the Sun's rays hit more directly.
- *All deserts are hot and sandy* — Antarctica is a cold desert; many hot deserts are rocky.
- *Rainforest soil must be very fertile* — it's often thin; nutrients are held in the living plants.
- *Africa is a country* — it's a continent of 54 countries; always name the country (Ghana, Kenya).
- *Indigenous peoples live 'in the past'* — they are modern communities with rights, knowledge and choices.
- *Fair trade means charity* — it's about fairer prices and partnership in trade.
- *Grid references: northing first* — always along the corridor (easting), then up the stairs (northing).

**Adapting for your country.** The local area unit and all 'compare with home' tasks use your own town. Where Italy or the Amazon is 'home', compare with another contrasting region instead.

**Differentiation.** Support = more concrete: physical maps and globes, picture cards, partially completed diagrams, adult-supported fieldwork roles. Stretch = deeper geographical reasoning: explain causes and trade-offs, compare three places, evaluate data reliability, propose sustainable solutions.

**Vertical connections.** Builds on Year 2 (latitude and longitude, hemispheres, time zones, eight compass points, four-figure grid references, symbols, scale, rivers, mountains and volcanoes, settlements). Year 3 retrieves those map skills briefly and adds sixteen compass points, atlas indexes, digital layers and map scale. Year 4 uses them for coasts, the Colorado River region of North America, Antarctica, mountains at altitude, and resources and energy; Year 5 adds six-figure grid references, contours and GIS.
