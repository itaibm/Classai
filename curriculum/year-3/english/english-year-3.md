# Year 3 English (ages 8–9) — Scope & Sequence

## Year overview

Year 3 is the year children move from *learning to read* to *reading to learn and to love*. Phonics is complete (Year 1–2 finished synthetic phonics and Phase 6 spelling); now the work is fluency, stamina, vocabulary and understanding of whole books, and writing that is organised in **paragraphs** for real readers. Everything is taught **through the class texts**: grammar and spelling are taught in service of the writing the book inspires, never as isolated test drills.

**The class-text spine (read in full, aloud and together):**
- **Term 1 — *The Iron Man* by Ted Hughes** (a short modern classic with poetic, rhythmic prose — ideal for learning how writers build setting and suspense) and **Greek myths** from *D'Aulaires' Book of Greek Myths* by Ingri and Edgar Parin d'Aulaire (the foundation stories of Western literature, read alongside the Ancient Greece history unit). Poetry: **performance poetry** — Allan Ahlberg, Roger McGough, Michael Rosen, Joseph Coelho.
- **Term 2 — *The Boy at the Back of the Class* by Onjali Q. Raúf** (a funny, moving story of a refugee boy from Syria and the friends who help him — builds empathy and discussion) and ***The Arrival* by Shaun Tan** (a wordless graphic novel of migration — a masterclass in inference and a stimulus for narrative). Non-fiction: explanation texts and a **research report** on Ancient Rome or the Kingdom of Kush (linked to history). Poetry: **haiku, tanka and imagery** — Matsuo Bashō, Grace Nichols, Benjamin Zephaniah.
- **Term 3 — *Where the Mountain Meets the Moon* by Grace Lin** (a Chinese-folklore quest novel full of stories-within-stories, read alongside the Ancient China history unit) and ***The Wild Robot* by Peter Brown** (a robot survives on a wild island — science, nature and what it means to belong). Poetry: **narrative and classic poems to learn by heart** — T. S. Eliot, Charles Causley, Robert Louis Stevenson, Edward Lear.

**Writing genres this year:** descriptive narrative (a new chapter), myth, letter in role, journey narrative from pictures, explanation, non-chronological report from research, playscript, survival diary/log, poetry (performance, haiku, narrative), book review and a real letter. **Grammar in service of writing:** paragraphs; fronted adverbials with commas; expanded noun phrases with prepositional phrases; pronouns and nouns for cohesion; inverted commas for direct speech; conjunctions and adverbs for time and cause; Standard English verb forms; present perfect; plural possessive apostrophes. **Word study:** prefixes (in-, il-, im-, ir-, sub-, inter-, super-, anti-, auto-), suffixes (-ation, -ous, -ly, -tion/-sion/-ssion/-cian), Greek-origin spellings (ch as /k/, ph), French-origin spellings (ch as /sh/, -que, -gue), /s/ spelled sc, ei/eigh/ey, homophones, and the Year 3–4 statutory word list, practised through the week's reading. **Handwriting** is joined, legible and increasingly fast; **speaking and listening** runs through every unit (reading aloud, discussion with reasons, hot-seating, storytelling, poetry performance, a formal presentation).

**What this year assumes (from Year 2, ages 7–8):** secure decoding (all phonics phases), reading age-appropriate chapter books with growing independence, writing in simple paragraphs, using prefixes and suffixes, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, and speech marks in their simplest form, with joined handwriting started. **What Year 4 (ages 9–10) relies on:** reading longer novels independently, summarising and inferring with evidence, planning and paragraphing multi-part writing, confident direct speech, fronted adverbials and cohesion — the base for Year 4's persuasive writing, relative clauses, modal verbs and parenthesis.

Lessons are **40 minutes** (poetry and speaking lessons 35), plus **20 minutes of independent reading every day** outside these lessons, and a weekly library visit. Every week includes class reading aloud, one grammar-for-writing or word-study focus, and one sustained writing session.

## Unit 1 — Readers and writers: launching the year

**Essential question:** What kind of reader and writer am I — and how can I become an even better one?

**Key vocabulary:** reader, genre, blurb, recommendation, fluency, expression, dictionary, thesaurus, alphabetical order, synonym, paragraph, topic sentence, joined handwriting

### Lesson 1 — My reading life
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe my reading tastes and choose a book that fits me." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (range of books))
- **Hook:** "If your favourite book were a food, what would it be — and why?"
- **Key activity:** Teacher shares their own reading history with real books from childhood; children complete a 'reader profile' (genres, favourite authors, where they read). Book-tasting: tables laid like a restaurant with 'menus' of diverse books; children sample first pages, write one-line reactions, and choose their first independent read.
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose one book you tasted and explain, using a detail from its first page, why you will or won't read on." → names a specific detail (character, hook, language) that attracted or put them off
- **Differentiation:** support: teacher-guided choice from a short, well-matched selection with audio versions available / stretch: compare two openings and explain which hooks the reader faster and how
- **Materials:** a wide class library (picture books, novels, non-fiction, poetry, graphic novels from many cultures), menu cards
- **Joy:** "Book-tasting café" with tablecloths and a reading-menu

### Lesson 2 — Reading aloud with fluency
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read a passage aloud with accuracy, pace and expression that shows meaning." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc; SL)
- **Hook:** "Listen to me read this sentence three ways — which one sounds like the author meant it?"
- **Key activity:** I do: model prosody with a short extract (pauses at punctuation, stress on key words, voice for dialogue). We do: echo reading and choral reading. You do: pairs rehearse a paragraph, mark pauses and emphasis in pencil, then perform.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why should your voice change at a question mark or an exclamation mark? Show me." → punctuation signals meaning — a question rises, an exclamation is stronger; demonstrated with the text
- **Differentiation:** support: shorter passage with pauses pre-marked; echo-read first / stretch: read a passage with two characters' voices and explain the choices
- **Materials:** copies of a short passage, pencils, audio recorder
- **Joy:** "Radio play": record the class reading for a podcast

### Lesson 3 — Word detectives: dictionaries and thesauruses
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find words quickly in a dictionary using the first two or three letters and choose precise synonyms." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (use dictionaries); Wg)
- **Hook:** "Is 'big' the best word for a whale, a mountain and a problem?"
- **Key activity:** Race to find words by 2nd and 3rd letters; read definitions and choose the meaning that fits a sentence. Thesaurus: replace 'big' in three sentences with precise words (enormous, towering, serious), discussing shades of meaning.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why isn't 'gigantic' a good replacement for 'big' in 'a big mistake'?" → synonyms have different shades of meaning; 'serious' fits a mistake, 'gigantic' suggests physical size
- **Differentiation:** support: dictionary with the alphabet printed down the edge; first-letter races before second-letter / stretch: build a 'shades of meaning' scale for words meaning 'walk' (amble … march … sprint)
- **Materials:** children's dictionaries and thesauruses, paint-chip cards for word scales
- **Joy:** "Dictionary dash" team race

### Lesson 4 — Handwriting: fluent joins
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write with consistent joined handwriting at a steady speed." (UK NC Y3–4 Wh)
- **Hook:** "Can you write a sentence that's both beautiful and fast?"
- **Key activity:** Warm-up: finger and shoulder exercises. Teacher models the four join families (diagonal, horizontal, to anticlockwise letters, break letters) on a board with lined guides. Children practise joins in words from the year's spelling list, then a 2-minute copying sprint of a favourite poem line, counting words.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which letters don't join from, and why?" → capital letters and letters that finish on the left or bottom (such as b, g, j, p, q, x, y, z in many styles) often don't join — show from own handwriting scheme
- **Differentiation:** support: wider-lined paper, slower pace, focus on one join family / stretch: copy a short poem in best joined handwriting and illustrate it for display
- **Materials:** handwriting books with lines, pencils/pens, poem cards
- **Joy:** "Poem postcards" handwritten for another class

### Lesson 5 — Paragraphs: one idea at a time
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what a paragraph is for and group sentences into paragraphs with a topic sentence." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (organise paragraphs around a theme))
- **Hook:** "This page is one enormous block of writing. Why is it so hard to read?"
- **Key activity:** Cut up a short non-fiction text about a real animal (e.g. snow leopards) into sentences; groups sort into paragraphs by theme (habitat, food, threats) and choose a topic sentence for each. Teacher models the 'new time, new place, new speaker, new topic' rule for narrative too.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did you put 'They hunt blue sheep and ibex' in the same paragraph as 'Snow leopards are carnivores'?" → both sentences are about the same topic — food
- **Differentiation:** support: fewer sentences with colour-coded topics / stretch: write a topic sentence for a paragraph that has none
- **Materials:** cut-up text strips, envelopes, highlighters
- **Joy:** "Sentence jigsaw" race to rebuild the text

### Lesson 6 — Baseline writing: tell me about a place
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a short description of a place I know, organised in paragraphs." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc)
- **Hook:** "Close your eyes. You're in the place you feel happiest. What can you hear?"
- **Key activity:** Guided visualisation; children list sense details; teacher models a two-paragraph description (what you see first, then what you hear and feel). Children write independently for 20 minutes; this is kept as the September writing sample for the portfolio.
- **Check for understanding:** "Point to your two paragraphs — what is each one about?" → each paragraph has one focus (e.g. sights, then sounds and feelings)
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence starters and a sense-grid planner / stretch: include one sentence that shows a feeling without naming it
- **Materials:** planners, lined paper, portfolio folders
- **Joy:** writing is sealed in a 'time capsule' to compare in July

**End-of-unit check:** Read a paragraph aloud with expression; use a dictionary to find the right meaning; sort sentences into paragraphs with topic sentences; the baseline description is kept for the portfolio.

## Unit 2 — Class novel: The Iron Man (reading)

**Essential question:** How does a writer make us afraid of something — and then change our minds?

**Key vocabulary:** prediction, inference, evidence, suspense, repetition, simile, metaphor, personification, character, motive, summary, theme, fear, trust

### Lesson 7 — The opening: prediction and questions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make predictions and ask questions from a story opening, using evidence." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (predict; ask questions))
- **Hook:** "Nobody knows. Nobody knows. Nobody knows. — Why does the author keep saying that?"
- **Key activity:** Read the famous opening of Chapter 1 aloud with the lights dimmed. Children list questions ('Where had he come from?') and predictions, each with a clue from the text. Discuss the effect of the repeated 'Nobody knows'.
- **Check for understanding:** "What do you predict the Iron Man will do next, and which words make you think so?" → a prediction supported by a quoted detail (e.g. he is huge and falls — he may break)
- **Differentiation:** support: question stems ('I wonder why…') and picture cues / stretch: explain how the repetition creates mystery and suspense
- **Materials:** copies of *The Iron Man* (Ted Hughes), sticky notes
- **Joy:** lights off, torch reading of the opening

### Lesson 8 — Word study: prefixes in-, il-, im-, ir-
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can spell and explain words with the negative prefixes in-, il-, im- and ir-." (UK NC Y3–4 Wt (prefixes))
- **Hook:** "The Iron Man seems invincible — but what does the 'in' at the start of that word do?"
- **Key activity:** Sort word cards (incorrect, illegal, impossible, irregular, immature, illogical, irresponsible, inactive). Discover the pattern: il- before l, ir- before r, im- before m or p, in- elsewhere (with exceptions). Children build words and write them in sentences about the story.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it 'impossible' and not 'inpossible'?" → before a root starting with p (or m), the prefix is spelled im-
- **Differentiation:** support: sort by the root's first letter using a colour-coded chart / stretch: find an exception to the rule (e.g. 'inedible' follows the rule; 'unlikely' uses a different prefix) and explain why some words take un- instead
- **Materials:** word cards, sorting mats, dictionaries
- **Joy:** "Prefix transformer": flip-cards that turn words into their opposites

### Lesson 9 — Chapter 1: summarising
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can summarise a chapter in a few sentences using the most important events." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (summarise main ideas))
- **Hook:** "Could you tell the whole of Chapter 1 in a text message of 30 words?"
- **Key activity:** Finish Chapter 1 (the fall, the hand and eye searching, reassembly). Children list every event, then cross out details that aren't essential, then write a 30-word summary. Teacher models 'somebody–wanted–but–so–then'.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which event did you leave out of your summary, and why wasn't it essential?" → names a detail and explains the story still makes sense without it
- **Differentiation:** support: event cards to order and choose from / stretch: write two summaries: 30 words and 10 words, and compare what is lost
- **Materials:** novel, event cards, word-count grids
- **Joy:** "Tweet the chapter" on a paper phone

### Lesson 10 — The language of the Iron Man
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify similes and personification and explain their effect." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (discuss words and phrases that capture imagination))
- **Hook:** "How do you describe something nobody has ever seen?"
- **Key activity:** Hunt for comparisons in the description of the Iron Man; sort into simile, metaphor, personification. Discuss why Hughes compares a machine to living things and everyday objects.
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose one comparison and explain what it makes you picture and feel." → names the comparison and explains the image and effect (size, strength, strangeness)
- **Differentiation:** support: a set of 4 pre-highlighted phrases to sort / stretch: write a new simile for the Iron Man's eyes that fits the mood
- **Materials:** novel extracts, highlighters, sorting grid
- **Joy:** "Image gallery": draw the picture each comparison makes

### Lesson 11 — Chapter 2: Hogarth and the farmers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can infer characters' feelings and motives from their actions and words." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (infer feelings, thoughts and motives))
- **Hook:** "The farmers want to trap the Iron Man. Are they villains or just scared?"
- **Key activity:** Read Chapter 2. Role on the wall: outline of Hogarth and of the farmers; inside, feelings/motives; outside, evidence from actions/words. Discussion with reasons.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do the farmers decide to trap the Iron Man? Use evidence." → he is eating their machinery and they fear for their farms — quoted or paraphrased evidence
- **Differentiation:** support: feelings word bank and pictures / stretch: argue from the Iron Man's point of view about what's fair
- **Materials:** novel, large paper outlines, sticky notes
- **Joy:** "Conscience alley": Hogarth walks between voices telling him what to do

### Lesson 12 — Grammar for writing: fronted adverbials
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use fronted adverbials followed by a comma to show when, where and how." (UK NC Y3–4 Wg (fronted adverbials))
- **Hook:** "'Slowly, the Iron Man rose from the pit.' Why does the sentence begin that way?"
- **Key activity:** Find fronted adverbials in the text; sort into when / where / how; move adverbials from the end to the front and add the comma. Children write three sentences about the Iron Man using each type.
- **Check for understanding:** "Rewrite 'The Iron Man walked along the cliff in the moonlight' with a fronted adverbial. Where does the comma go, and why?" → 'In the moonlight, the Iron Man walked along the cliff.' — the comma marks the end of the adverbial before the main clause
- **Differentiation:** support: adverbial cards to physically move to the front / stretch: vary adverbials across a paragraph so no two start the same way
- **Materials:** sentence strips, scissors, adverbial word bank
- **Joy:** "Sentence shuffle": human sentences where children holding words re-order themselves

### Lesson 13 — Chapter 3: what should we do with him?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can discuss a dilemma in the story and give reasons for my opinion." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc; SL (discussion))
- **Hook:** "The Iron Man needs to eat metal. The farmers need their machines. Is there a fair answer?"
- **Key activity:** Read Chapter 3 (the scrap-metal yard solution). Before the solution is revealed, groups debate options with 'I think… because…' and 'I disagree because…'. Compare with Hogarth's solution.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the scrap-metal yard a clever solution? Explain from both sides." → it gives the Iron Man food he can eat and removes the threat to the farmers — both sides get what they need
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence stems for discussion / stretch: suggest a different solution and weigh its pros and cons
- **Materials:** novel, discussion stems
- **Joy:** "Town-hall meeting" role-play

### Lesson 14 — Word study: the suffix -ous
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can spell and use adjectives ending in -ous, including the tricky ones." (UK NC Y3–4 Wt (-ous suffix))
- **Hook:** "The Iron Man is enormous, dangerous and mysterious. What do these words share?"
- **Key activity:** Sort words: root + ous (poisonous, dangerous, mountainous), drop -e (famous, from fame), y → i (various, glorious), -our → -or (humorous), and words with no obvious root (enormous, jealous, tremendous). Children use them in descriptions.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it 'humorous', not 'humourous'? How is 'famous' made?" → the -our of humour changes to -or before -ous; famous = fame with the final e dropped before -ous
- **Differentiation:** support: one group of rule words at a time with a reference card / stretch: collect -ous words from the text and other books and classify them
- **Materials:** word cards, dictionaries, sorting mats
- **Joy:** "Adjective auction": bid for the best -ous word for a picture

### Lesson 15 — Chapter 4: the space-being
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare two characters and how the author presents them." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc)
- **Hook:** "Something enormous lands on Australia. Is it a bat? An angel? A dragon?"
- **Key activity:** Read Chapter 4. Venn diagram comparing the Iron Man and the space-being (size, arrival, what they want, how people react). Discuss how people's fear repeats.
- **Check for understanding:** "How is the world's reaction to the space-being like the farmers' reaction to the Iron Man?" → both times people are afraid of something huge and unknown and want to fight it
- **Differentiation:** support: Venn diagram with sentence starters / stretch: explain why Hughes might have repeated this pattern (the theme of fear)
- **Materials:** novel, Venn diagrams
- **Joy:** draw the 'space-bat-angel-dragon' from textual clues only

### Lesson 16 — Chapter 5: the challenge
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a writer builds suspense towards a climax." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc)
- **Hook:** "Two giants, one test: who can survive the fire and the sun?"
- **Key activity:** Read Chapter 5 aloud with a 'tension graph' — children plot the rise in suspense page by page and mark the words that make it rise (short sentences, repetition, delay).
- **Check for understanding:** "Point to a place where the tension rises. Which words cause it?" → identifies a passage and explains the technique (e.g. short sentences, a countdown, unknown outcome)
- **Differentiation:** support: tension graph with page numbers marked / stretch: compare the climax with the opening chapter's techniques
- **Materials:** novel, tension-graph sheets
- **Joy:** "Tension meter": a paper dial the class turns as the chapter is read

### Lesson 17 — Theme: fear and peace
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify a theme and explain it with evidence from across the book." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (identify themes))
- **Hook:** "Ted Hughes first wrote this story for his own children. What big idea did he want them to carry with them?"
- **Key activity:** Groups gather evidence cards from each chapter; decide on a theme sentence ('Fear makes us fight what we don't understand') and support it with three quotations.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is one theme of The Iron Man? Give two pieces of evidence." → a theme such as fear of the unknown, or peace over war, supported by two events or quotes
- **Differentiation:** support: theme options given, children choose and find one piece of evidence / stretch: compare the theme with another story they know
- **Materials:** novel, evidence cards
- **Joy:** "Theme tree": branches of evidence on a class display

### Lesson 18 — Word study: Year 3–4 word list (1)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can learn to spell ten words from the statutory list using meaning and memory strategies." (UK NC Y3–4 Wt (word list))
- **Hook:** "Why is 'separate' spelled with 'a rat' in the middle?"
- **Key activity:** Teach 10 list words (e.g. separate, believe, disappear, earth, heart, island, occasion, special, strength, surprise). Strategies: look for the tricky part, mnemonics, words within words, syllables, look-say-cover-write-check.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which part of 'island' is tricky, and what strategy helps you remember it?" → the silent s — e.g. 'an island is land surrounded by water'
- **Differentiation:** support: five words with picture mnemonics / stretch: create mnemonics for three words and teach them to the class
- **Materials:** word cards, whiteboards
- **Joy:** "Spelling magician": invent the funniest mnemonic

### Lesson 19 — Retelling the story orally
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can retell the whole story in my own words with the main events in order." (UK NC Y3–4 SL; Rc)
- **Hook:** "Could you tell the whole story to someone who has never read it — in 3 minutes?"
- **Key activity:** Story map with five boxes (one per chapter); rehearse in pairs with connectives (first, later, meanwhile, finally); tell to a partner who asks one question.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which events must be in your retelling, and why?" → names the key events per chapter and explains they change the story
- **Differentiation:** support: story map with pictures / stretch: retell from the Iron Man's point of view
- **Materials:** story maps, a timer
- **Joy:** "Storyteller's chair" performance

### Lesson 20 — Book response and review
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a book review giving my opinion with reasons and evidence." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc)
- **Hook:** "Would you recommend The Iron Man to a 7-year-old? To your grandmother?"
- **Key activity:** Read model reviews; identify features (summary without spoilers, opinion, reasons, recommendation). Write a review for the class library.
- **Check for understanding:** "What's the difference between a summary and a review?" → a summary retells; a review judges the book and gives reasons
- **Differentiation:** support: review frame with prompts / stretch: write for two different audiences
- **Materials:** review templates, star ratings
- **Joy:** reviews posted in the library for other classes

### Lesson 21 — Reading assessment: The Iron Man
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can answer retrieval, inference and language questions about the novel." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc)
- **Hook:** "Show what you know about the Iron Man."
- **Key activity:** Low-stakes comprehension on an unseen extract with questions of each type; discuss answers afterwards.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain how the author makes the Iron Man seem powerful in this extract, with evidence." → identifies two techniques with quoted words
- **Differentiation:** support: questions read aloud / stretch: an extended answer comparing two parts
- **Materials:** comprehension booklet
- **Joy:** class discussion 'our favourite line'

**End-of-unit check:** Summarise The Iron Man in five sentences; explain one theme with evidence; identify and explain a simile; write three sentences with different fronted adverbials; spell ten list words.

## Unit 3 — Writing: a new Iron Man chapter

**Essential question:** How do writers build a vivid setting and a believable character, paragraph by paragraph?

**Key vocabulary:** setting, atmosphere, senses, expanded noun phrase, preposition, prepositional phrase, fronted adverbial, show don't tell, paragraph, plan, draft, edit, proofread, publish

### Lesson 22 — Reading as a writer: settings
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a writer builds a setting using the senses and precise words." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (read similar writing to learn structure and language))
- **Hook:** "Close your eyes while I read the cliff-top scene. What can you hear, smell and feel?"
- **Key activity:** Re-read a setting passage from *The Iron Man*; children highlight sight, sound, touch and smell words in different colours; teacher models turning notes into an annotated 'writer's toolkit'.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which sense does Hughes use most in this passage, and what effect does it have?" → identifies a sense with examples and explains the mood it creates (e.g. sound — eerie, lonely)
- **Differentiation:** support: passage pre-split into sentences with sense icons / stretch: find where Hughes chose a surprising verb and explain why it works
- **Materials:** extracts, four highlighters, toolkit sheets
- **Joy:** "Sound booth": create the soundscape of the cliff with voices and objects

### Lesson 23 — Grammar for writing: expanded noun phrases
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can expand noun phrases with adjectives and prepositional phrases to add precise detail." (UK NC Y3–4 Wg (noun phrases expanded by modifying adjectives, nouns and prepositional phrases))
- **Hook:** "'The robot' — or 'the rusty, towering robot with eyes like headlamps'? Which one can you see?"
- **Key activity:** Build noun phrases on a 'noun-phrase ladder': determiner + adjective(s) + noun + prepositional phrase. Teacher models; children expand five nouns from the story; then cut unnecessary adjectives ('less is more').
- **Check for understanding:** "Expand 'the cliff' with one adjective and a prepositional phrase. Which part tells us where?" → e.g. 'the crumbling cliff above the grey sea' — 'above the grey sea' is the prepositional phrase
- **Differentiation:** support: word banks of adjectives and prepositions / stretch: write one noun phrase for the same noun that makes it frightening and one that makes it peaceful
- **Materials:** noun-phrase ladder cards, word banks
- **Joy:** "Noun-phrase gallery": captions for dramatic photographs

### Lesson 24 — Show, don't tell: character feelings
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show a character's feelings through actions, body language and dialogue." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (character))
- **Hook:** "Hogarth was scared — boring! How can we make the reader feel his fear instead?"
- **Key activity:** Freeze-frames of emotions; children describe what they see (trembling hands, a whisper). Teacher rewrites 'He was scared' three ways. Children rewrite four 'telling' sentences.
- **Check for understanding:** "Rewrite 'Hogarth was excited' to show it. What clues did you give the reader?" → e.g. 'Hogarth's heart thudded as he raced down the path, grinning.' — actions and body language show excitement
- **Differentiation:** support: feelings-and-actions chart for reference / stretch: show two conflicting feelings at once (brave but frightened)
- **Materials:** emotion picture cards, charts
- **Joy:** "Freeze-frame theatre"

### Lesson 25 — Word study: prefixes sub-, inter-, super-, anti-, auto-
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can spell and explain words with the prefixes sub-, inter-, super-, anti- and auto-." (UK NC Y3–4 Wt (prefixes))
- **Hook:** "A submarine goes under the sea. A superhero is above ordinary. What does each prefix mean?"
- **Key activity:** Meaning chart: sub (under), inter (between), super (above/beyond), anti (against), auto (self). Build words (subheading, international, supermarket, antiseptic, autograph) and invent 'new' words for a sci-fi Iron Man world, defining them.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does 'interplanetary' mean? Explain using the prefix." → between planets — inter means between
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards for each prefix meaning / stretch: invent and define three words for a science-fiction glossary
- **Materials:** prefix cards, dictionaries
- **Joy:** "Invent-a-word" sci-fi dictionary

### Lesson 26 — Planning a new chapter
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a new chapter in paragraphs with a clear setting, problem and resolution." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (plan writing))
- **Hook:** "The Iron Man is needed again — what new danger could bring him back?"
- **Key activity:** Class brainstorm of problems (a tsunami, a lost child, a machine gone wrong); children use a 5-paragraph planner (setting, problem, build-up, climax, resolution) with key vocabulary for each.
- **Check for understanding:** "What will each of your paragraphs be about? Tell me in one sentence each." → five clear paragraph focuses in sequence
- **Differentiation:** support: a 3-paragraph planner with picture prompts / stretch: include a flashback or a change of viewpoint
- **Materials:** planning frames, story maps
- **Joy:** "Pitch to the publisher": 30-second chapter pitch

### Lesson 27 — Drafting: setting and build-up
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draft the opening paragraphs using the toolkit." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (draft and write))
- **Hook:** "Your first sentence should grab the reader like a hand on the shoulder."
- **Key activity:** Teacher models writing the opening paragraph live, thinking aloud about word choice. Children draft paragraphs 1–2, referring to the toolkit.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me a sentence where you used the senses. What does it make the reader feel?" → points to a specific sentence and names the intended effect
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence starters for each paragraph / stretch: open with dialogue or a short dramatic sentence
- **Materials:** planners, toolkit, drafting books
- **Joy:** "Opening line gallery": share first sentences

### Lesson 28 — Drafting: climax and resolution
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draft the climax and ending, using short sentences for tension." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc)
- **Hook:** "How do writers make your heart beat faster?"
- **Key activity:** Mini-lesson: compare two versions of the same climax — one in long sentences, one mixing long and very short sentences — and discuss pace. Teacher models drafting a climax paragraph aloud; children draft paragraphs 3–5 from their plan, then read the climax to a partner who marks where their heart beat faster.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where did you use short sentences, and why?" → at the climax, to speed up the pace and build tension
- **Differentiation:** support: a bank of short climax sentences and a partly written climax to continue / stretch: end with an echo of the book’s repeated opening ("Nobody knows…") that gives it new meaning
- **Materials:** drafting books, planners, toolkit
- **Joy:** "Heartbeat reading" to a partner

### Lesson 29 — Editing for effect
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can improve my draft by changing words and sentences for effect." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (evaluate and edit))
- **Hook:** "Every author you love rewrote their work — first drafts are meant to be messy."
- **Key activity:** Pairs read drafts aloud; use an editing checklist (senses, noun phrases, show-don't-tell, fronted adverbials); children make three improvements in coloured pen.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me one change you made. Why is it better?" → explains the improvement's effect
- **Differentiation:** support: checklist with three items / stretch: restructure one paragraph
- **Materials:** editing pens, checklists
- **Joy:** "Author conference" with a partner

### Lesson 30 — Proofreading like a publisher
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can proofread my writing for spelling, capital letters and punctuation and correct errors independently." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (proofread for spelling and punctuation errors))
- **Hook:** "The printer is waiting — but the teacher's paragraph has ten planted mistakes. Can you catch them all before it goes to print?"
- **Key activity:** I do: proofread the planted paragraph aloud, one sweep per error type (capitals and full stops; commas after fronted adverbials; spellings from this term's word study). We do: a second paragraph together. You do: three sweeps of their own chapter with a coloured pen, using a dictionary for any word with a 'wobbly' underline.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it better to read your work several times, looking for one kind of error each time?" → focusing on one error type at a time makes mistakes easier to spot than trying to find everything at once
- **Differentiation:** support: a two-sweep checklist (full stops/capitals, then tricky words already underlined by the teacher) / stretch: swap with a partner and proofread each other's work, explaining each correction politely
- **Materials:** planted-error paragraph, coloured pens, dictionaries, proofreading checklist
- **Joy:** "Mistake hunt": teams race to find planted errors, with a golden ticket for the last hidden one

### Lesson 31 — Publishing the anthology
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can publish my chapter in fluent joined handwriting with a layout that helps the reader." (UK NC Y3–4 Wh; Wc)
- **Hook:** "Our class is becoming a publishing house — each chapter goes into a real anthology for the library."
- **Key activity:** Children write up their edited chapter in best joined handwriting (or type it if handwriting is a barrier), add a title, one illustration in the style of the book's original line drawings, and a short 'About the author' paragraph. Pages are bound into the class anthology.
- **Check for understanding:** "What makes your chapter ready for a reader you've never met?" → it has been edited for effect, proofread, is legible and set out in paragraphs with a title
- **Differentiation:** support: publish the two strongest paragraphs only, with a scribe or typing option / stretch: write a blurb for the whole anthology that makes a reader want to open it
- **Materials:** good paper, fine-liner pens, a binder or stapler, cover card
- **Joy:** the anthology is catalogued and shelved in the school library

### Lesson 32 — Author reading
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read my chapter aloud with expression to an audience and respond to questions." (UK NC Y3–4 SL (speak audibly and fluently; participate in presentations))
- **Hook:** "Real authors do readings. Today, you're the author on tour."
- **Key activity:** Children rehearse a 2-minute extract with a partner, marking pauses and emphasis. In small 'bookshop' groups, each author reads and answers two audience questions ('Why did you choose that ending?'). Audience gives 'two stars and a wish'.
- **Check for understanding:** "What question did your audience ask, and how did your answer explain a choice you made as a writer?" → explains a deliberate choice (e.g. short sentences at the climax to build tension)
- **Differentiation:** support: read a shorter extract, rehearsed with the teacher first / stretch: answer questions in role as the Iron Man or Hogarth
- **Materials:** published anthology, a 'bookshop' corner, a microphone prop
- **Joy:** "Author tour": a pretend bookshop with signing table and bookmarks

### Lesson 33 — Reflecting on my writing
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare my new writing with my September sample and set a writing target." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (evaluate own writing))
- **Hook:** "Open your time capsule: how has your writing grown since September?"
- **Key activity:** Children lay the September baseline beside their published chapter and find evidence of growth (paragraphs, noun phrases, fronted adverbials, show-don't-tell). They choose one target from a menu for Term 2 and write it inside their writing book cover. Teacher conferences with each child briefly.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me one thing you can do now that you couldn't in September, with evidence from both pieces." → points to a specific improvement in the new piece and its absence in the baseline
- **Differentiation:** support: a growth checklist with tick-boxes and examples / stretch: explain which target will make the biggest difference to a reader and why
- **Materials:** baseline samples, published chapters, target menus
- **Joy:** "Growth stars": a sticker for every piece of growth evidence found

**End-of-unit check:** Publish a five-paragraph chapter that includes a sense-rich setting, at least two expanded noun phrases with prepositional phrases, three fronted adverbials with commas, and a character's feelings shown through action; read an extract aloud with expression.

## Unit 4 — Greek myths: reading, telling and writing myths

**Essential question:** Why did people tell myths, and what makes a myth still worth telling 3,000 years later?

**Key vocabulary:** myth, legend, god, goddess, hero, quest, monster, Olympus, fate, hubris, moral, oral tradition, narrator, sequence, dialogue, inverted commas

### Lesson 34 — What is a myth?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the features of a myth and how myths differ from legends and fairy tales." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (myths and legends; identify conventions))
- **Hook:** "Why does the sun cross the sky? Why are there seasons? Before science, people told stories."
- **Key activity:** Read the myth of Persephone and Demeter (explaining the seasons). Build a features chart: gods and humans, supernatural events, explains nature or teaches a lesson, first told aloud. Compare with a fairy tale and a legend (e.g. Robin Hood). Place Greece on a map and timeline (link to history).
- **Check for understanding:** "How does the Persephone myth explain the seasons?" → when Persephone is in the underworld, her mother Demeter grieves and nothing grows (winter); when she returns, spring comes
- **Differentiation:** support: features checklist with picture icons / stretch: find a myth from another culture that explains the same thing and compare
- **Materials:** *D'Aulaires' Book of Greek Myths*, world map, timeline, features chart
- **Joy:** "Mount Olympus" display where each god gets a portrait and symbol

### Lesson 35 — Word study: Greek roots and ch as /k/
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can spell words of Greek origin where ch says /k/ and ph says /f/, and use Greek roots to work out meanings." (UK NC Y3–4 Wt (words with /k/ spelled ch))
- **Hook:** "The Greeks gave us 'chorus', 'echo' and 'character'. And a nymph called Echo gave us a word!"
- **Key activity:** Tell the myth of Echo in 3 minutes. Sort words with ch as /k/ (chorus, echo, character, scheme, chemist, stomach, school) and ph (photograph, alphabet, telephone). Roots: tele (far), phone (sound), graph (write), auto (self). Build and define words.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'telephone' spelled with ph? What do its two parts mean?" → it comes from Greek: tele (far) + phone (sound/voice), and Greek φ became ph in English
- **Differentiation:** support: picture-root cards to combine / stretch: create a Greek-root 'word tree' with at least six branches
- **Materials:** root cards, dictionaries, word-tree template
- **Joy:** "Echo game": the class echoes words back and spells them

### Lesson 36 — Pandora and the jar
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can infer character motives and discuss a myth's message." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (infer; discuss))
- **Hook:** "You're given a box and told never to open it. What do you do?"
- **Key activity:** Read the myth of Pandora. Discuss: why did the gods give her curiosity and the forbidden jar? What was left inside (hope)? Philosophy-for-children style enquiry: 'Is curiosity good or bad?'
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it important that hope stayed in the jar? What might the Greeks have been saying?" → even after troubles escape into the world, people still have hope — a message about getting through hard times
- **Differentiation:** support: picture-sequence of the story and discussion stems / stretch: argue both sides of 'Pandora is to blame' with evidence
- **Materials:** myth text, a decorated 'forbidden box' prop
- **Joy:** the 'forbidden box' opened at the end reveals a note of hope for each child

### Lesson 37 — Theseus and the Minotaur: story structure
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can map the structure of a hero's quest and identify the problem, climax and resolution." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (identify how structure contributes to meaning))
- **Hook:** "A monster in a maze, a ball of thread, and a sail that was the wrong colour."
- **Key activity:** Read the myth; story mountain (opening, build-up, problem, climax, resolution); discuss the tragic ending (the black sail). Children build a maze with string to retell.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which event is the climax, and why is the ending both happy and sad?" → the fight with the Minotaur; Theseus wins but forgets to change the sail and his father Aegeus dies of grief
- **Differentiation:** support: story mountain with event pictures to place / stretch: explain how Ariadne is as much a hero as Theseus
- **Materials:** myth text, story-mountain sheets, string and chairs for a maze
- **Joy:** "The labyrinth": a string maze in the hall to walk through while retelling

### Lesson 38 — Daedalus and Icarus: character and warning
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a character's choices lead to consequences and what warning the myth gives." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc)
- **Hook:** "If you had wings made of feathers and wax, how high would you fly?"
- **Key activity:** Read the myth; freeze-frames of key moments; thought-tracking Icarus and Daedalus at the moment of flight. Discuss the idea of 'hubris' in child-friendly terms (pride that ignores a warning).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did Icarus fall, and what warning might the myth be giving?" → he flew too close to the sun, the wax melted; the warning is to listen to wise advice and not let excitement make you reckless
- **Differentiation:** support: freeze-frame prompts and a feelings bank / stretch: compare Icarus with a modern story where someone ignores a warning
- **Materials:** myth text, feathers, a picture of Bruegel's *Landscape with the Fall of Icarus*
- **Joy:** spot tiny Icarus in Bruegel's painting

### Lesson 39 — Arachne and Athena: dialogue
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can punctuate direct speech with inverted commas and a reporting clause." (UK NC Y3–4 Wg (inverted commas for direct speech))
- **Hook:** "A weaver boasts she's better than a goddess. What would they say to each other?"
- **Key activity:** Read the myth of Arachne. Model speech punctuation: opening inverted commas, spoken words, punctuation inside, closing inverted commas, reporting clause. Children write the argument between Arachne and Athena as a dialogue, starting a new line for each new speaker.
- **Check for understanding:** "Punctuate: I am the best weaver in the world said Arachne. Explain where each mark goes." → "I am the best weaver in the world," said Arachne. — words spoken go inside inverted commas, with a comma before the closing marks
- **Differentiation:** support: speech bubbles converted into sentences with a template / stretch: vary reporting verbs and put the reporting clause first or in the middle
- **Materials:** myth text, speech-bubble templates, weaving loom or picture
- **Joy:** act out the contest with fabric scraps

### Lesson 40 — Perseus and Medusa: oral storytelling
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can retell a myth orally with expression, gesture and storytelling phrases." (UK NC Y3–4 SL (speak audibly and fluently; storytelling))
- **Hook:** "Before books, Greek myths were told out loud by storytellers. Can you hold an audience?"
- **Key activity:** Read Perseus and Medusa; learn a story map with actions (Talk for Writing style); rehearse in threes with storytelling phrases ('Long ago, when the gods still walked the earth…').
- **Check for understanding:** "What did you do with your voice and body to keep the audience listening?" → describes a deliberate technique (pause before the reveal, gestures, varying volume)
- **Differentiation:** support: story map with pictures and actions / stretch: tell the myth from Medusa's point of view
- **Materials:** story maps, a cloak or prop
- **Joy:** "Storyteller's circle" by torchlight

### Lesson 41 — King Midas: morals
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify the moral of a myth and relate it to my own life." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (themes))
- **Hook:** "Everything you touch turns to gold. Wonderful … or terrible?"
- **Key activity:** Read the myth of Midas and the golden touch; discuss what he learned; children write the moral in one sentence and a modern example.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the moral of the Midas myth? Give an example from real life." → greed can make us lose what really matters — e.g. choosing money over time with family
- **Differentiation:** support: moral options to choose from / stretch: compare Midas's moral with the moral of another culture's story
- **Materials:** myth text, gold paper
- **Joy:** "Golden touch": children list what they'd never want turned to gold

### Lesson 42 — Myths from around the world
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare a Greek myth with a myth from another culture." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (compare texts))
- **Hook:** "Every culture has a story about how the world began or why the sun moves."
- **Key activity:** Groups read a Greek myth and a myth from another tradition (e.g. Anansi from West Africa, Māui slowing the sun from Polynesia, Sedna from the Inuit). Compare purposes, characters and settings on a grid.
- **Check for understanding:** "How are the Greek and Polynesian sun myths similar, and how are they different?" → both explain nature with heroes and gods; the settings, characters and how the problem is solved differ

- **Materials:** myth texts from several cultures, comparison grids, world map
- **Joy:** "Myth map": pin each myth to its place of origin

### Lesson 43 — Planning my own myth
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan an original myth with a hero, a god, a quest and a nature explanation or moral." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (plan))
- **Hook:** "Invent a myth: why do zebras have stripes? Why does the moon change shape?"

- **Check for understanding:** "What does your myth explain or teach, and where in your plan does that happen?" → names the explanation or moral and shows the resolution paragraph that delivers it
- **Differentiation:** support: a shorter planner with character and monster cards / stretch: include a twist where the god is not what they seem
- **Materials:** planners, character cards
- **Joy:** "Monster forge": design and name your monster

### Lesson 44 — Drafting my myth
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draft a myth in paragraphs with dialogue and descriptive detail." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (draft))
- **Hook:** "Your hero is about to meet the monster. What happens?"
- **Key activity:** Teacher models drafting the call-to-adventure paragraph with dialogue. Children draft their myth, using inverted commas for at least two speech sentences and expanded noun phrases for the monster.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me a line of dialogue. How does it move the story on or reveal character?" → quotes their dialogue and explains what it shows (e.g. the hero's fear, the god's warning)
- **Differentiation:** support: a partly written opening to continue / stretch: open in the middle of the action and fill in background later
- **Materials:** planners, drafting books
- **Joy:** "Mid-draft cliffhanger": read your last sentence to a partner who guesses what's next

### Lesson 45 — Editing and publishing myths
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can edit for effect and accuracy and publish my myth." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (evaluate, edit, proofread))
- **Hook:** "Would a Greek storyteller be proud of your myth?"
- **Key activity:** Partner feedback using a myth features checklist; one improvement for effect; proofread speech punctuation; publish on 'scroll' paper.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did you change after feedback and why?" → explains a specific change and its effect
- **Differentiation:** support: checklist with three items / stretch: add a narrator's frame ('The old ones say…')
- **Materials:** checklists, scroll paper, dowels
- **Joy:** myths hung as scrolls in the class 'agora'

### Lesson 46 — Myth festival
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can tell or read my myth to an audience." (UK NC Y3–4 SL)
- **Hook:** "Welcome to the festival of Dionysus — the Greeks' festival of stories and theatre!"
- **Key activity:** Groups perform myths with simple masks; audience identifies the explanation or moral.
- **Check for understanding:** "What was the moral or explanation in the myth you watched?" → names it accurately with a detail
- **Differentiation:** support: perform in a group with a narrator reading / stretch: perform solo from memory
- **Materials:** masks, props
- **Joy:** myth festival with Greek masks made in art

### Lesson 47 — Reading assessment: myths
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can answer questions on an unseen myth." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc)
- **Hook:** "An unseen myth: can you read it like an expert?"

- **Check for understanding:** "Why does Orpheus look back, and what does it tell us about him?" → he doubts/cannot bear not knowing — shows love and impatience; supported by evidence
- **Differentiation:** support: questions read aloud / stretch: an extended answer on the moral
- **Materials:** comprehension booklet
- **Joy:** class vote for the best myth of the unit

## Unit 5 — Poetry: poems to perform

**Essential question:** How can a poem come alive when we speak it out loud?

**Key vocabulary:** poem, poet, verse, stanza, line, rhyme, rhythm, repetition, onomatopoeia, alliteration, performance, volume, tone, pace, audience

### Lesson 48 — Poems are for voices
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a poem with a group, using volume, pace and expression to show meaning." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (prepare poems to read aloud and perform); SL)
- **Hook:** "Listen to 'Please Mrs Butler' by Allan Ahlberg. Who is speaking in each verse?"
- **Key activity:** Teacher performs the poem with two voices (the child and Mrs Butler). Class identifies the two speakers and how to show them. Groups of three prepare a performance, deciding who reads which lines and how Mrs Butler's patience changes.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does Mrs Butler's voice change from the first to the last reply, and why?" → she grows more exasperated each time; the replies get more ridiculous — voice rises in frustration
- **Differentiation:** support: a single speaking part with the lines highlighted / stretch: add a third voice (the class) and justify its lines
- **Materials:** copies of 'Please Mrs Butler' (Allan Ahlberg), highlighters
- **Joy:** "Staffroom drama": groups perform with a teacher's mug prop

### Lesson 49 — Sound poems: onomatopoeia
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify onomatopoeia and explain how sound words create an effect." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (words that capture imagination))
- **Hook:** "What if a stranger came and took away every sound in your house?"
- **Key activity:** Read 'The Sound Collector' by Roger McGough. List all the sound words; sort into onomatopoeia and descriptive phrases; discuss why the ending feels empty and eerie. Groups perform with sound effects.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does the poem feel so quiet and sad at the end?" → after all the lively sounds are listed, they are taken away — the silence contrasts with the noise
- **Differentiation:** support: a list of sound words with actions / stretch: write a new verse in the same rhyme pattern with new household sounds
- **Materials:** copies of 'The Sound Collector', percussion instruments and household objects
- **Joy:** "Sound collector" game — a child 'steals' sounds one by one from the room

### Lesson 50 — Rhythm and rhyme: learn a poem by heart
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can learn a poem by heart using rhythm and rhyme as memory hooks." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (recite poetry by heart))
- **Hook:** "Can you remember a whole poem? Poets built rhythm into poems partly so people could!"
- **Key activity:** Choose a short rhythmic poem (e.g. Michael Rosen's 'Chocolate Cake' in extract or another class favourite). Techniques: clap the beat, learn a stanza at a time, actions for key images, back-chaining. Pairs test each other.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which memory trick helped you most, and why do you think rhythm helps?" → names a technique; rhythm and rhyme create patterns the brain predicts, so the next word is easier to recall
- **Differentiation:** support: learn one stanza with picture cues / stretch: learn the whole poem and perform without the text
- **Materials:** poem cards, drums
- **Joy:** "Poem in your pocket": a folded copy that the child performs anywhere during the week

### Lesson 51 — Alliteration and tongue-twisters
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify and write alliteration and explain its effect." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc; Wc)
- **Hook:** "Six slippery snails slid slowly seaward."
- **Key activity:** Read alliterative poems and tongue-twisters; discuss effect (speed, fun, mood). Children write an alliterative animal poem, one line per animal from a different continent.
- **Check for understanding:** "What effect does the repeated 's' sound have in the snail line?" → it sounds slow and slithery — the sound matches the movement
- **Differentiation:** support: an alliterative word bank per letter / stretch: use alliteration to create a mood (menacing, gentle) rather than just fun
- **Materials:** word banks, animal picture cards
- **Joy:** tongue-twister championship

### Lesson 52 — Poems with a message
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can discuss how a poet uses humour to make a serious point." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (discuss themes))
- **Hook:** "Can a funny poem make you think seriously?"
- **Key activity:** Read a poem from Joseph Coelho's *Werewolf Club Rules!* or Michael Rosen's work that mixes humour and feeling; discuss the poet's message; children find the line where the tone shifts.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where does the poem change from funny to serious, and how can you tell?" → identifies the line and names the change in word choice or tone
- **Differentiation:** support: poem split into sections with a tone scale / stretch: compare how two poets use humour differently
- **Materials:** poetry collections, tone scales
- **Joy:** "Laugh-then-think" poem swap

### Lesson 53 — Writing a performance poem
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a poem for performance with repetition and a strong rhythm." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (compose poetry))
- **Hook:** "Write a poem that begs to be read out loud."
- **Key activity:** Model a list poem with a repeated line ('In my school bag there is…'). Children draft their own, marking where voices, pauses and sound effects go.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where did you use repetition, and what will your voice do there?" → points to the repeated line and explains the performance choice
- **Differentiation:** support: a frame with the repeated line given / stretch: include a surprise final line that changes the meaning
- **Materials:** drafting books, performance-mark symbols
- **Joy:** "Open mic" rehearsal

### Lesson 54 — Poetry slam
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform my poem to an audience and give kind, specific feedback." (UK NC Y3–4 SL)
- **Hook:** "Tonight: the Year 3 Poetry Slam!"
- **Key activity:** Children perform their poem or a learned poem; audience gives 'two stars and a wish' using performance vocabulary (volume, pace, expression, eye contact).
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one specific star and one wish for a performer." → feedback names a technique (e.g. the pause before the last line) and a precise improvement
- **Differentiation:** support: perform in a pair / stretch: perform a learned poem and their own
- **Materials:** microphone prop, score cards
- **Joy:** poetry slam with finger-clicks instead of applause

**End-of-unit check:** Perform a learned poem by heart with expression; identify onomatopoeia, alliteration and rhyme and explain their effect; write and perform a poem with repetition.

## Unit 9 — Non-fiction: explanation texts

**Essential question:** How do writers explain how and why something happens so clearly that a reader could explain it too?

**Key vocabulary:** explanation, process, cause, effect, sequence, because, so, therefore, as a result, consequently, when, after, diagram, label, caption, technical vocabulary, present tense, glossary

### Lesson 80 — What makes a good explanation?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify the purpose and features of an explanation text." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (non-fiction structure); Wc)
- **Hook:** "How does a sandwich become energy for your legs? Let's find a text that explains it."

- **Differentiation:** support: publish three stages rather than five, with a diagram template / stretch: add a three-question quiz at the end so readers can check their understanding
- **Materials:** good paper, fine-liners, diagram templates
- **Joy:** children deliver their books to the younger class and read them together

### Lesson 89 — Explaining aloud

- **Objective:** "By the end, I can give a clear 60-second spoken explanation of a process using technical vocabulary and a diagram." (UK NC Y3–4 SL (explain; use subject vocabulary))

### Lesson 90 — Asking research questions

### Lesson 93 — Note-taking in my own words
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make notes as keywords and phrases and turn them back into my own sentences." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (retrieve and record information); Wc)
- **Hook:** "If you copy a whole sentence from a book, whose writing is it — yours or the author's?"
- **Key activity:** I do: read a paragraph about Roman aqueducts aloud, close the book, write 3–5 keywords, then say the idea in my own words. We do: a second paragraph together on the board. You do: two paragraphs from their own topic book using a 'read, cover, keywords, say, write' routine.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why should research notes be keywords, not whole sentences?" → keywords make you understand the idea and then write it in your own words; copying is someone else's writing and may not show you understood
- **Differentiation:** support: a note frame with three keyword boxes per paragraph and a partner to say the idea to / stretch: make notes from a short museum video clip, where copying isn't possible, and compare with book notes
- **Materials:** topic books, note frames, a short museum video
- **Joy:** "Close the book" challenge: can you explain it without peeking?

### Lesson 94 — Reliable sources
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can judge whether a source is reliable and check a fact in two trusted places." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc; links to Computing (evaluating online content))
- **Hook:** "A website says every Roman ate pizza every day. Should we believe it?"
- **Key activity:** Compare three printed 'sources': a museum website page (e.g. the British Museum or a national museum), a children's encyclopedia entry and an anonymous social-media-style post. Use a checklist: Who made it? Are they experts? When? Can I find it somewhere else? Children rate each and check two facts in a second source.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is this fact reliable? How did you check it?" → confirmed in a second trusted source such as a museum or encyclopedia, and explains why the original source is or isn't trustworthy
- **Differentiation:** support: the checklist with picture icons and only two sources to compare / stretch: find a fact on which two sources disagree and explain which to trust and why
- **Materials:** printed web pages, encyclopedias, checklists
- **Joy:** "Fact or fake?" quiz game with thumbs up/down cards

### Lesson 95 — Word study: -tion, -sion, -ssion, -cian
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose the correct ending for the /shun/ sound." (UK NC Y3–4 Wt)
- **Hook:** "Invasion, construction, musician, discussion: four spellings, one sound."
- **Key activity:** Rules: -tion most common (after t roots: invent → invention); -sion after d/s roots (extend → extension; invade → invasion); -ssion after ss or mit roots (discuss → discussion; permit → permission); -cian for people jobs (music → musician). Sort Roman-themed words.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it 'musician' but 'invention'?" → -cian is used for people with a job linked to a root ending in c/cs; invent ends in t, so -tion
- **Differentiation:** support: word sort by root ending / stretch: explain 'permit → permission'
- **Materials:** word cards
- **Joy:** "Shun sort" race

### Lesson 96 — Organising notes into sections
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can organise research notes under subheadings and order the sections logically." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (organise paragraphs around a theme; headings and subheadings))
- **Hook:** "Your notes are like a messy bedroom. Let's tidy them into labelled boxes."
- **Key activity:** Children cut their notes into strips and sort them under subheadings (e.g. Daily life, Buildings, Army, Legacy); discard repeats; decide the order of sections and justify it (general → specific, or most interesting last).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did you put these notes under this subheading, and why does this section come first?" → the notes share one topic; the order helps the reader build understanding (e.g. overview before details)
- **Differentiation:** support: subheadings provided, with one note already placed under each / stretch: create a section that compares Rome (or Kush) with life today

- **Joy:** "Tidy-up" race: whose notes are sorted first with no strays?

### Lesson 97 — Topic sentences and cohesion
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write paragraphs with a topic sentence and use pronouns and nouns to avoid repetition while keeping meaning clear." (UK NC Y3–4 Wg (choose nouns or pronouns for clarity and cohesion))
- **Hook:** "The Romans built roads. The Romans built baths. The Romans built aqueducts. The Romans … — what's wrong with this paragraph?"

- **Differentiation:** support: sentence strips to rearrange with pronoun cards / stretch: find a place in their own notes where a pronoun could confuse and choose the clearest noun

- **Joy:** "Repetition robot": the class 'repairs' a robot that repeats itself

### Lesson 99 — Drafting the conclusion and glossary

## Unit 11 — Poetry: small poems, big pictures

### Lesson 104 — Haiku: a moment in 17 syllables

- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the features of a haiku and count syllables accurately." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (recognise forms of poetry))
- **Hook:** "A frog, a pond, a splash — can three lines hold a whole moment?"

- **Differentiation:** support: clapping syllables of two-syllable nature words first with picture cards / stretch: compare two translations and argue which captures the moment better

- **Joy:** "Haiku walk": notice one tiny moment outdoors

### Lesson 105 — Writing haiku from observation
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a haiku that captures one precise moment using sense details." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (compose poetry))
- **Hook:** "Find something small outside that no one else has noticed."

### Lesson 106 — Tanka and cinquain
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare haiku, tanka and cinquain forms and write one." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc; Wc)
- **Hook:** "A haiku is a snapshot. A tanka adds two lines — what could they add?"

- **Materials:** poem cards, syllable grids
- **Joy:** "Form detectives": identify mystery poems by counting

### Lesson 107 — Imagery: Grace Nichols

- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can punctuate direct speech accurately, including reporting clauses before, after and in the middle." (UK NC Y3–4 Wg (inverted commas and other punctuation for direct speech))
- **Hook:** "'Help,' she whispered. She whispered, 'Help.' 'Help,' she whispered, 'I'm stuck.' — three patterns, one set of rules."

### Lesson 152 — Reading assessment: The Wild Robot

## Unit 16 — Poetry: story poems and classics to learn by heart

**Essential question:** How do poems tell stories, and why do some poems last for a hundred years or more?

**Key vocabulary:** narrative poem, ballad, verse, stanza, rhyme scheme, rhythm, metre, refrain, character, nonsense, recite, anthology, classic

### Lesson 153 — A mystery cat: narrative and character
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a narrative poem builds a character through description and refrain." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (recognise forms of poetry; discuss language))
- **Hook:** "A cat who breaks every law but is never, ever there when the crime is discovered…"
- **Key activity:** Read 'Macavity: The Mystery Cat' from T. S. Eliot's *Old Possum's Book of Practical Cats*. Children list Macavity's crimes and the descriptions; identify the refrain ('Macavity's not there!') and discuss its comic effect.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does the poet keep repeating 'Macavity's not there!'?" → it's a refrain that builds humour and mystery — the reader starts to expect it, and it shows how clever he is at escaping
- **Differentiation:** support: verse-by-verse picture prompts / stretch: explain how the rhythm makes the poem feel sly and quick
- **Materials:** copies of the poem
- **Joy:** "Wanted" poster for Macavity using evidence from the poem

### Lesson 154 — A ghost story in verse
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can follow the plot of a narrative poem and explain how rhyme and rhythm create humour." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc)
- **Hook:** "A colonel who isn't afraid of the ghost in his castle — at all!"
- **Key activity:** Read Charles Causley's 'Colonel Fazackerley'; map the story's events verse by verse; perform the colonel and the ghost in pairs.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does the colonel's reaction to the ghost make the poem funny?" → he treats the frightening ghost as a joke and a party entertainer, the opposite of what we expect
- **Differentiation:** support: event cards to order / stretch: explain the rhyme scheme and how it helps the humour
- **Materials:** copies of the poem
- **Joy:** spooky-but-funny performance with torches

### Lesson 155 — Rhythm you can hear
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how rhythm matches a poem's subject." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc)
- **Hook:** "Faster than fairies, faster than witches — can you hear the train?"
- **Key activity:** Read Robert Louis Stevenson's 'From a Railway Carriage' aloud to a drum beat; children tap the rhythm and discuss how it mirrors a train's movement; list the fleeting images.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does the rhythm make you feel like you're on a train?" → the fast, regular beat mimics the wheels on the track, and the quick images flash past like views from a window
- **Differentiation:** support: tap along with a partner / stretch: write two lines in the same rhythm about a different vehicle
- **Materials:** copies of the poem, drums
- **Joy:** "Poem train": the class moves as a train while reciting

### Lesson 156 — Nonsense and sound
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how nonsense poems use invented words and sound for effect." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc)
- **Hook:** "They went to sea in a sieve, they did!"
- **Key activity:** Read Edward Lear's 'The Jumblies'; identify the refrain and invented words; discuss why nonsense can still create pictures and feelings.
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose an invented word. What do you think it means, and what clues help?" → offers a meaning based on sound and context
- **Differentiation:** support: illustrated verse / stretch: invent a word and use it in a verse in the same pattern
- **Materials:** copies of the poem
- **Joy:** build a 'sieve boat' from a colander for performance

### Lesson 157 — Poems from around the world
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare poems from different cultures and explain what each says about childhood." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (poems from other cultures))
- **Hook:** "A boy in India floats paper boats down a stream. A poet in America tells us to hold fast to dreams."
- **Key activity:** Read Rabindranath Tagore's 'Paper Boats' and Langston Hughes's 'Dreams'; compare images and message; discuss the poets' lives and countries.
- **Check for understanding:** "What do both poems say about hope or wishes, and how do their images differ?" → both express hope; Tagore uses paper boats carrying wishes, Hughes uses broken-winged birds and barren fields to show life without dreams
- **Differentiation:** support: poems with key images highlighted / stretch: explain which image is stronger and why
- **Materials:** copies of the poems, world map
- **Joy:** fold and float paper boats with wishes written inside

### Lesson 158 — Learning a classic by heart
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can learn a classic poem by heart and recite it with expression that shows its meaning." (UK NC Y3–4 Rc (learn a wider range of poetry by heart))
- **Hook:** "Poems can live in your head for the rest of your life. Which one will you carry with you?"
- **Key activity:** Children choose a poem from the unit (or 'Dreams' for a short option). Recap memory strategies from Term 1 (chunking, actions for images, back-chaining from the last line). Pairs rehearse, prompting each other only with the first word of a line, then add expression that shows meaning.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which strategy helped most with this poem, and why does it suit this poem?" → names a strategy with a reason linked to the poem (e.g. the refrain in 'Macavity' anchors each verse)
- **Differentiation:** support: learn one or two stanzas with picture cues / stretch: learn a whole longer poem and explain one choice of expression
- **Materials:** poem cards, picture cues
- **Joy:** "Poem in your pocket" day — recite for any adult in school who asks

### Lesson 159 — Writing a narrative poem
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a short narrative poem with a character, a refrain and a clear story shape." (UK NC Y3–4 Wc (compose poetry))
- **Hook:** "Write the story of a creature who is never where you expect it to be."
- **Key activity:** Plan four verses (set-up, problem, twist, ending) and a refrain that returns at the end of each verse. Teacher models drafting one verse, reading aloud to test rhythm and rhyme. Children draft, clapping the beat to find lines that stumble.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where does your refrain appear, and what does it add to the story?" → explains where it repeats and its effect (humour, suspense, tying verses together)
- **Differentiation:** support: a frame with the refrain supplied and rhyming word banks / stretch: keep a consistent rhyme scheme (e.g. ABCB) and explain it
- **Materials:** drafting books, rhyming dictionaries
- **Joy:** "Refrain rap": perform the refrain with body percussion

### Lesson 160 — Class anthology and recital
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a learned and an original poem and compile a class anthology." (UK NC Y3–4 SL; Wc)
- **Hook:** "Families are invited to our end-of-year poetry recital — and the anthology goes in the library."
- **Key activity:** Each child writes a final copy of their original poem with a short note on why they wrote it; an editorial team orders the anthology and writes an introduction. At the recital, children recite a learned classic and read their own poem.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did you choose your learned poem, and what does your own poem show about you as a poet?" → gives a reason linked to language or feeling, and names a technique in their own poem
- **Differentiation:** support: perform with a partner / stretch: introduce each poem to the audience with a short spoken 'poet's note'
- **Materials:** anthology pages, a binder, microphone prop
- **Joy:** family recital with a printed programme

**End-of-unit check:** Recite a classic poem by heart with expression; explain how rhythm, rhyme and refrain work in a narrative poem; write a short narrative poem with a refrain; compare two poems from different cultures.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Reads fluently and with stamina** — reads an unseen age-appropriate passage aloud accurately at a natural pace with expression that shows meaning, and sustains 20–30 minutes of independent reading. *See it:* a 1-minute read-aloud and a reading-log conversation.
2. **Infers and justifies** — answers "why" and "how do you know" questions with evidence from the text. *See it:* explains a character's motive in an unseen extract, quoting a phrase.
3. **Summarises** — summarises a chapter or section in a few sentences, choosing the most important events or ideas. *See it:* a 30-word summary of an unseen chapter.
4. **Talks about language and structure** — explains the effect of a simile, repetition, refrain, short sentences, or a story-within-a-story. *See it:* annotation of a poem or passage.
5. **Writes in paragraphs** — organises narrative and non-fiction into paragraphs with a clear focus, topic sentences and headings where appropriate. *See it:* the independent writing assessment.
6. **Uses this year's grammar for effect** — fronted adverbials with commas, expanded noun phrases with prepositional phrases, pronouns for cohesion, Standard English verb forms, present perfect. *See it:* highlighted in their own writing, not in a worksheet.
7. **Punctuates speech and possession accurately** — inverted commas with reporting clauses in all positions; singular and plural possessive apostrophes. *See it:* a dialogue in their own story.
8. **Researches and reports** — asks questions, finds facts in more than one source, makes notes in their own words and writes a report with a glossary and sources. *See it:* the Term 2 report.
9. **Spells the year's patterns** — prefixes, suffixes, /shun/ endings, Greek and French-origin spellings, homophones and most Year 3–4 statutory words, in their own writing. *See it:* dictated sentences and edited drafts.
10. **Speaks with confidence** — recites a poem by heart, presents research for 3 minutes, and takes part in discussion by building on and challenging others' ideas. *See it:* the poetry recital and presentation day.

## Teacher guidance

**Shape of a typical week.** Two reading lessons on the class text (reading aloud, discussion, a comprehension focus), one grammar-for-writing or word-study lesson taught *through* the text, and two writing lessons building towards a published piece. Every day: 20 minutes' independent reading outside these lessons; a daily read-aloud of a different book purely for pleasure; 5 minutes of spelling or handwriting practice.

**Reading aloud matters most.** Children at 8–9 can understand far more than they can yet read alone. Read the class novels aloud with children following in their own copies, then have them re-read key passages themselves. Model the thinking of a good reader ("I'm wondering why…", "That word surprises me because…").

**Grammar in service of writing.** Every grammar lesson ends with children using the feature in writing about the class text. Avoid decontextualised drills; judge mastery by use in independent writing.

**Common misconceptions to watch for.**
- *Commas after fronted adverbials are optional* — at this stage, teach always to use one (it helps the reader).
- *Apostrophes for plurals* ("banana's for sale") — the apostrophe shows possession or omission, never a plain plural.
- *Plural possession* — the apostrophe goes after the plural ending: girls' coats, but children's toys (irregular plural + 's).
- *Speech punctuation outside inverted commas* — the punctuation that belongs to the spoken words goes inside the closing inverted commas.
- *'Could of'* for *could have* — written from how it sounds; teach the contraction *could've*.
- *Standard English* — non-standard forms (we was, I done) are not 'wrong' in speech communities; children learn when formal writing needs Standard English, and writers may use dialect in dialogue deliberately.
- *Summarising means retelling everything* — teach selection: what can be removed without the story falling apart.
- *Inference is guessing* — every inference needs evidence; ask "What in the text makes you think that?"
- *Copying = research* — insist on 'read, cover, keywords, say, write'.
- *y → i rule* — only when the y follows a consonant (happy → happiness, but play → played).

**Sensitive content.** *The Boy at the Back of the Class* and *The Arrival* deal with war and displacement. Know your class: some children may have lived these experiences. Invite, never require, personal sharing, and give children a private way to talk to an adult.

**Differentiation.** Support means *more access*: pre-teaching vocabulary, audio versions of the class text, partner reading, sentence frames, dictation of first paragraphs. Stretch means *more depth*: comparing texts, explaining authorial choices, writing from an unusual viewpoint, borrowing a technique deliberately — never just 'write more'.

**Vertical connections.** Year 2 finished phonics and began joined handwriting, paragraphs, conjunctions, prefixes/suffixes and simple speech punctuation. Year 4 builds on this year's paragraphing, inference and speech punctuation to teach persuasive writing, relative clauses, modal verbs, parenthesis, cohesion across paragraphs and comparison of texts. A child leaving Year 3 should be a fluent, willing reader of chapter books who writes organised paragraphs for a real reader.
