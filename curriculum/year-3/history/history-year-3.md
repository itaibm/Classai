# Year 3 History (ages 8–9) — Scope & Sequence

## Year overview

Year 3 history is the year of **great ancient civilisations on three continents** — and of learning *how we know* about people who lived 2,000–3,500 years ago. Children study **Ancient Greece** (Europe), the **Roman Empire** across Europe, North Africa and West Asia (with Roman Britain as one case study and Roman North Africa as another), the **Kingdom of Kush** in Nubia (Africa), and **Ancient China from the Shang to the Han dynasty and the Silk Road** (Asia). The year ends by connecting these worlds on one timeline — the Silk Road linked Han China and Rome — and tracing their legacy in the children's own town, wherever that is.

**Every unit asks "How do we know?"** Children handle sources — pottery, coins, inscriptions, oracle bones, buildings, writing by ancient historians, archaeological finds — and ask who made them, why, and what they can and cannot tell us. They meet *interpretation*: why the Romans and Boudica, or Kushite and Egyptian records, tell different stories about the same events.

**Chronology.** Children use BC/BCE and AD/CE dates, place every civilisation on a class timeline that runs the whole year, and recognise that these civilisations overlapped in time (the Kushite pharaohs, the Olympic Games and the founding of Rome all fall in the 700s BC).

**What this year assumes (from Year 2, ages 7–8):** the Stone Age to Iron Age, Ancient Egypt, Sumer and the Indus Valley; the idea of a civilisation; sequencing on a timeline; simple use of sources. **What Year 4 (ages 9–10) relies on:** confident BC/AD chronology, the concept of empire, trade and legacy, and evaluating sources — as it moves to the medieval world around AD 900: the Vikings, Abbasid Baghdad, the Kingdom of Benin and the Maya.

Lessons are **40 minutes**, one a week. The school's own country and town are used as worked examples in the final unit; teachers outside the UK use Roman Britain as a case study of how an empire changed a conquered land, and add their own region's ancient history where relevant.

## Time budget

35 lessons at 1 a week = 35 weeks. The class timeline grows through the year; every unit ends with a "How do we know?" mastery task.

## Unit 1 — Time detectives: chronology and evidence

**Essential question:** How do historians put the past in order, and how do they know what happened?

**Key vocabulary:** chronology, timeline, century, millennium, BC, AD, BCE, CE, ancient, civilisation, source, primary source, secondary source, evidence, archaeologist, artefact

### Lesson 1 — Our year's timeline
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can order the civilisations we have studied and will study on a timeline using BC and AD." (UK NC KS2 Hi (chronology))
- **Hook:** "Which is older: the pyramids of Giza or the Great Wall of China? The answer surprises most adults."
- **Key activity:** Build a long class timeline (washing line) from 3000 BC to AD 500; peg on Year 2 civilisations (Sumer, Indus, Egypt) and this year's (Greece, Rome, Kush, China). Explain counting backwards in BC and forwards in AD from the year 1.
- **Check for understanding:** "Legend says Rome was founded in 753 BC; Kushite kings began ruling Egypt around 744 BC. Which came first, how many years apart, and how do you know?" → Rome's founding, about 9 years earlier — BC years count down towards year 1, so the bigger BC number is further back in time
- **Differentiation:** support: number-line cards with only centuries marked / stretch: calculate how many years apart 776 BC and AD 43 are (818 — no year zero)
- **Materials:** washing line, pegs, date cards, picture cards
- **Joy:** "Human timeline": children holding date cards stand in order

### Lesson 2 — Sources: who made it, and why?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the maker and purpose of a source affect what it tells us." (UK NC KS2 Hi (sources; how evidence is used))
- **Hook:** "If people in 2,000 years found only your school's prospectus, would they know what a wet Monday here is really like?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of Year 2's primary and secondary sources. New question: *who made it and why?* Children examine ancient sources (a Roman coin showing an emperor as a victor, a Greek vase made to sell, a king's victory inscription, a shopping list scratched on a pot) and sort them into 'made to impress' and 'made for everyday use', then say what each can and can't tell us.
- **Check for understanding:** "A Roman coin shows the emperor as a mighty winner. Why might that not be the whole truth — and why is the coin still useful evidence?" → it was made on the emperor's orders to make him look good, so it shows his side; it is still useful because it tells us his name, how he wanted to be seen and that coins like it were used in trade
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards with the questions 'Who made it? Why?' on each / stretch: explain why an everyday object (a shopping list) can sometimes be more trustworthy than a grand inscription
- **Materials:** source picture cards (coins, vase, inscription, graffiti), sorting mats
- **Joy:** "Future archaeologist": bury and later 'excavate' a class time capsule

### Lesson 3 — The first historians: can we trust them?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how historians use ancient written accounts, checking them against other evidence." (UK NC KS2 Hi (how knowledge of the past is constructed from sources))
- **Hook:** "About 2,500 years ago a Greek writer, Herodotus, reported giant gold-digging ants in India. Should we believe the rest of his book?"
- **Key activity:** Meet Herodotus (c. 484–425 BC), often called 'the father of history', who travelled, asked questions and wrote down what he saw and was told. Children sort short adapted extracts into 'he saw it', 'someone told him' and 'a story he repeated', then check two claims against archaeological evidence (e.g. his description of Egyptian mummification, which matches real mummies). Link: every later unit uses written accounts (Tacitus, Pliny, Piye's stela) that must be checked the same way.
- **Check for understanding:** "Herodotus's story about giant ants is almost certainly not true. Does that make all of his *Histories* useless? Explain how a historian should use him." → no — he recorded what he was told, so some parts are unreliable; historians check each claim against other sources and archaeology and trust the parts that are confirmed
- **Differentiation:** support: three extracts with picture clues and the three sorting labels / stretch: explain why an eyewitness account can still be wrong
- **Materials:** adapted Herodotus extracts, photos of Egyptian mummies and tools, sorting labels
- **Joy:** "Fact or fable?" game: teams bet counters on which Herodotus stories archaeology has confirmed

**End-of-unit check:** Place six civilisations in order on a BC/AD timeline and explain how BC dates work; explain who made a source, why, and how that affects what it tells us.

## Unit 2 — Ancient Greece

**Essential question:** How did a land of small, rival city-states change the way the world thinks, governs and plays?

**Key vocabulary:** Greece, city-state, polis, Athens, Sparta, democracy, citizen, vote, assembly, Olympics, philosopher, theatre, temple, Parthenon, myth, legacy, Herodotus

### Lesson 4 — A land of city-states
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why Ancient Greece was made of many city-states rather than one country." (UK NC KS2 Hi (Ancient Greece))
- **Hook:** "Greece is full of mountains and islands. How might that stop people becoming one country?"
- **Key activity:** Relief map of Greece and the Aegean; children discover how mountains and sea separated communities; learn that each polis (Athens, Sparta, Corinth, Thebes) had its own rulers, army and customs yet shared language, gods and the Olympic Games.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did geography help create separate city-states?" → mountains and sea made travel between communities hard, so each grew independently
- **Differentiation:** support: map with key features labelled / stretch: explain what united the Greeks despite having separate city-states
- **Materials:** relief map, atlas, city-state cards
- **Joy:** build a salt-dough relief map of Greece

### Lesson 5 — Athens and Sparta
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare life in Athens and Sparta using evidence." (UK NC KS2 Hi)
- **Hook:** "Would you rather grow up in Athens or Sparta? Choose carefully!"
- **Key activity:** Evidence cards on education, the army, girls' and women's lives, and government in each; Venn diagram; debate. Note the evidence is mostly written by Athenians — how might that bias it?
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one way life for girls differed in Athens and Sparta, and how we know." → Spartan girls trained in athletics and had more freedom; Athenian girls mostly stayed at home — known from ancient writers such as Xenophon and Plutarch
- **Differentiation:** support: evidence cards with pictures / stretch: explain why most of our evidence about Sparta comes from non-Spartans and what that means
- **Materials:** evidence cards, Venn diagrams
- **Joy:** "Athens vs Sparta" debate with shields

### Lesson 6 — The birth of democracy
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Athenian democracy worked and who could and couldn't take part." (UK NC KS2 Hi)
- **Hook:** "Everyone in Athens could vote … or could they?"
- **Key activity:** Re-enact an assembly vote (with pebbles/pottery shards); then reveal that only free adult male citizens voted — women, enslaved people and foreigners could not. Compare with democracy today.
- **Check for understanding:** "Was Athenian democracy fair? Give reasons for and against." → it was new and let many citizens vote directly, but excluded women, enslaved people and foreigners
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards of who could vote / stretch: compare with how your own country chooses its leaders today
- **Materials:** pebbles or clay tokens, voting urns
- **Joy:** class assembly vote with ostracism shards (on a harmless question)

### Lesson 7 — The Olympic Games
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the ancient Olympic Games and compare them with today's." (UK NC KS2 Hi)
- **Hook:** "The first recorded Olympics were in 776 BC. What sports would you have seen?"
- **Key activity:** Sources: vase paintings of athletes; the stadium at Olympia; events (running, wrestling, pentathlon, chariot racing); truce; only men competed (women had separate games for Hera). Compare with modern Olympics.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does this vase painting tell us about the ancient Olympics, and what can't it tell us?" → it shows the event and style of competing; it can't tell us rules, results or how athletes felt
- **Differentiation:** support: a comparison chart with pictures / stretch: explain why the Games had a sacred truce
- **Materials:** vase photos, map of Olympia
- **Joy:** mini 'ancient Olympics' in PE

### Lesson 8 — Greek thinkers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Greek philosophers and scientists changed how people think." (UK NC KS2 Hi)
- **Hook:** "Why do we still ask questions like Socrates did?"
- **Key activity:** Mini-stories: Socrates (questioning), Pythagoras (maths), Hippocrates (medicine; the Hippocratic Oath), Archimedes (the 'Eureka!' bath). Children try a Socratic dialogue on 'What is courage?'.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which Greek thinker's idea do we still use today, and how?" → e.g. Hippocrates — doctors observe symptoms and take an oath to help patients
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards / stretch: explain the difference between a myth explanation and a philosopher's explanation
- **Materials:** thinker cards, bath and floating objects
- **Joy:** "Eureka!" water-displacement demonstration

### Lesson 9 — Theatre and the arts
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe Greek theatre and architecture and their influence today." (UK NC KS2 Hi)
- **Hook:** "Why did Greek actors wear masks?"
- **Key activity:** Theatre design (the stepped, curved seating of theatres such as Epidaurus, which carries sound remarkably well), masks, comedy and tragedy; Parthenon columns; spot Greek-style columns in photos of buildings worldwide.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why could everyone in a huge Greek theatre hear the actors?" → the curved, stepped seating carried sound well
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards / stretch: explain how masks helped actors play multiple roles
- **Materials:** masks, column photos
- **Joy:** perform a short chorus with masks

### Lesson 10 — Alexander the Great and the spread of Greek ideas
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Greek language and ideas spread across three continents and judge whether Alexander deserves the name 'Great'." (UK NC KS2 Hi (Ancient Greece; significance))
- **Hook:** "A king from Macedonia, still in his twenties, led an army from Greece to Egypt and on to the edge of India. What did he leave behind?"
- **Key activity:** Trace Alexander's route (336–323 BC) on a map across Europe, Africa and Asia; learn about Alexandria in Egypt and its great library, where scholars from many lands worked in Greek. Evidence cards give different views of Alexander (brilliant leader; destructive conqueror). Children place themselves on a 'Great? / Not great?' line with reasons.
- **Check for understanding:** "How did Alexander's conquests spread Greek ideas, and what is one reason someone might say he was not 'great'?" → he founded cities such as Alexandria where Greek was spoken and learning flourished; but his wars killed many people and destroyed cities
- **Differentiation:** support: a map with the route pre-drawn and three evidence cards / stretch: explain why people living in the lands he conquered might describe him differently from Greek writers
- **Materials:** maps, evidence cards, string for routes
- **Joy:** "Route race": trace the journey and calculate its length with the map scale

### Lesson 11 — Unit review: the Greek legacy
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the legacy of Ancient Greece and support it with evidence." (UK NC KS2 Hi (legacy; how we know))
- **Hook:** "Spend one day without anything the Greeks gave us — no Olympics, no alphabet, no democracy, no theatre. What's left?"
- **Key activity:** Groups sort legacy cards (alphabet, democracy, Olympics, theatre, columns in architecture, geometry, medicine, words like 'telephone'); rank them by importance and justify; each child writes a museum label for one Greek source explaining what it proves and what it can't.
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose the Greek legacy you think matters most today. Explain why, and name a source that shows it existed in Ancient Greece." → a reasoned choice (e.g. democracy — citizens choose their leaders) supported by a source (e.g. voting tokens and ostraka from Athens)
- **Differentiation:** support: legacy cards with pictures and a sentence frame / stretch: argue for a less obvious legacy (e.g. Greek words in science) and defend it against the class favourite
- **Materials:** legacy cards, source photos, label templates
- **Joy:** "Greek legacy museum" for another class

**End-of-unit check:** Explain two differences between Athens and Sparta with evidence; explain who could vote in Athenian democracy; describe two Greek legacies we still live with; say how a vase painting is useful and limited as evidence.

## Unit 3 — The Roman Empire: three continents, one empire

**Essential question:** How did Rome build an empire around the Mediterranean, and what happened to the peoples it ruled?

**Key vocabulary:** Rome, republic, empire, emperor, legion, legionary, conquest, province, citizen, Latin, aqueduct, road, villa, forum, amphitheatre, rebellion, Romanisation, legacy

### Lesson 12 — From village to empire
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how Rome grew from a small city to an empire across Europe, Africa and Asia." (UK NC KS2 Hi (Roman Empire))
- **Hook:** "A city whose legend says it was founded by twins raised by a wolf ended up ruling from Scotland's border to Egypt. How?"
- **Key activity:** Tell the legend of Romulus and Remus (and note it's a legend, not evidence). Maps at three dates show growth; children colour the empire at its height around AD 117 across three continents and name modern countries it covered. Timeline: Republic (509 BC), Empire from Augustus (27 BC).
- **Check for understanding:** "Is the story of Romulus and Remus good evidence for how Rome began? Explain." → no — it's a legend told centuries later; archaeology of early settlements on the hills is better evidence
- **Differentiation:** support: an outline map with the empire's edge already drawn / stretch: explain the difference between a republic and an empire
- **Materials:** historical maps, atlases, coloured pencils
- **Joy:** "Empire jigsaw": assemble the empire's provinces from puzzle pieces

### Lesson 13 — The Roman army
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why the Roman army was so successful, using evidence." (UK NC KS2 Hi)
- **Hook:** "A legionary marched about 30 km a day carrying his kit. What made the army so hard to beat?"
- **Key activity:** Handle replica equipment (or pictures); learn organisation (contubernium, century, legion); practise the testudo (tortoise) formation with card shields in the playground; sources: Trajan's Column carvings.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does Trajan's Column show about the Roman army, and why might it exaggerate?" → it shows soldiers building, marching and fighting; it was made to celebrate the emperor's victories, so it shows Romans in the best light
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards of army features / stretch: compare discipline, training and engineering as reasons for success and rank them
- **Materials:** replica or picture kit, card shields
- **Joy:** playground testudo formation

### Lesson 14 — Case study: Roman Britain
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how and why Rome invaded Britain and how Britons responded." (UK NC KS2 Hi (Roman Empire and its impact on Britain))
- **Hook:** "Julius Caesar came twice and left. Why did Emperor Claudius invade in AD 43 — and stay?"
- **Key activity:** Timeline of invasion (55–54 BC Caesar; AD 43 Claudius). Evidence of different British responses: some tribes allied (e.g. the Atrebates), others resisted. Map of Roman towns and roads.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did some British tribes welcome the Romans while others fought them?" → some gained trade, wealth and power as allies; others lost land and freedom and resisted
- **Differentiation:** support: picture timeline with three key dates / stretch: explain why Claudius, a new emperor, wanted a victory
- **Materials:** timelines, maps of Roman Britain
- **Joy:** "Tribal council": groups decide whether to fight or ally

### Lesson 15 — Boudica: two sides of a story
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare different interpretations of Boudica's revolt and explain why they differ." (UK NC KS2 Hi (interpretation))
- **Hook:** "Hero or villain? It depends who's telling the story."
- **Key activity:** Read short extracts adapted from Tacitus and Cassius Dio (Roman writers); consider what the Iceni might have said (no written records of their own). Compare the heroic statue of Boudica beside the Houses of Parliament in London (made by Thomas Thornycroft in Victorian times and put up in 1902) with the Roman accounts.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we only have Roman accounts of Boudica's revolt, and how might that affect the story?" → the Iceni didn't leave written records; Roman writers wrote from the winners' view and may exaggerate her cruelty
- **Differentiation:** support: extracts rewritten simply with key words highlighted / stretch: explain why the Victorians made Boudica a national heroine
- **Materials:** extract cards, photo of the Westminster Boudica statue
- **Joy:** "Courtroom": Boudica's case heard from both sides

### Lesson 16 — Case study: Roman North Africa
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe life in a Roman province in Africa using archaeological evidence." (UK NC KS2 Hi)
- **Hook:** "One of Rome's emperors was born in Africa — and his city's ruins still stand."
- **Key activity:** Explore Leptis Magna (Libya) and mosaics from North Africa showing farming, fishing and games. Septimius Severus (born in Leptis Magna, emperor AD 193–211), who died at York in Britain.
- **Check for understanding:** "What do North African mosaics tell us about life there, and what can't they tell us?" → they show farming, animals, food and entertainment; they show wealthy people's choices, not ordinary lives
- **Differentiation:** support: mosaic photos with guiding questions / stretch: explain how an emperor from Africa ended up dying in Britain and what that shows about the empire
- **Materials:** mosaic photos, map
- **Joy:** make a paper mosaic of daily life

### Lesson 17 — Roads, aqueducts and towns
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Roman engineering changed the places Rome ruled and why it was built to last." (UK NC KS2 Hi (impact; legacy))
- **Hook:** "How did the Romans bring fresh water into cities from mountains many kilometres away — with no pumps?"
- **Key activity:** Groups build a model aqueduct from guttering and books with a very gentle slope and test water flow; examine photos of the Pont du Gard (France) and the aqueduct of Segovia (Spain); build a road cross-section in a clear cup (big stones, gravel, sand, paving) to see why roads lasted; compare a Roman town plan (forum, baths, amphitheatre, grid streets).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did aqueducts slope only very gently downhill?" → gravity kept the water flowing steadily; too steep a slope would make it rush and damage the channel
- **Differentiation:** support: step-by-step model cards and a pre-set slope / stretch: explain why Roman roads were built as straight as possible and in layers
- **Materials:** guttering, books, water trays, cups, gravel, sand, stones, photos
- **Joy:** "Build-an-aqueduct" challenge: whose water reaches the 'city' bucket?

### Lesson 18 — Pompeii: a moment frozen in time
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what Pompeii tells us about everyday Roman life and why it is unusual evidence." (UK NC KS2 Hi (sources))
- **Hook:** "In AD 79, Mount Vesuvius buried a whole town in ash in a day. Why are archaeologists, in a strange way, grateful?"
- **Key activity:** Explore photos of Pompeii's streets, stepping stones, bakeries with ovens, shop counters, wall paintings and graffiti; read a short adapted extract from Pliny the Younger's eyewitness letter about the eruption (seen from across the bay). Children 'walk' a street and infer daily life from each find.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is Pompeii better evidence of daily life than most Roman sites — and what might it still not show?" → it was buried suddenly, so objects were left where people used them; but it shows one wealthy-ish town in one moment, not the whole empire over centuries
- **Differentiation:** support: photo cards with 'I see… I think…' prompts / stretch: compare Pliny's written account with the archaeological evidence and say what each adds
- **Materials:** Pompeii photos, adapted extract, street map
- **Joy:** "Pompeii graffiti wall": children write (harmless) Roman-style messages

### Lesson 19 — Unit review: the Roman legacy
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can weigh the benefits and costs of Roman rule for conquered peoples using evidence." (UK NC KS2 Hi (significance; interpretation))
- **Hook:** "Was Roman rule good or bad for the people it ruled? Historians still argue."
- **Key activity:** Groups sort evidence cards (roads, towns, trade, Latin and law, baths vs. taxes, enslavement, loss of land, crushed revolts) into benefits and costs, noting *for whom* (a wealthy Briton, an enslaved person, a Berber farmer). Each child writes a short balanced paragraph.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one benefit and one cost of Roman rule, and say who benefited or suffered." → e.g. roads and trade helped merchants and wealthy townspeople; enslaved people and those who lost land suffered
- **Differentiation:** support: evidence cards with pictures and a two-column frame / stretch: write a conclusion that answers 'good or bad?' with 'it depends on…' and justify it
- **Materials:** evidence cards, writing frames
- **Joy:** "Roman legacy" debate with a vote at the end

**End-of-unit check:** Explain two reasons for the Roman army's success; compare two interpretations of Boudica; describe what Pompeii or North African mosaics tell us and their limits; give one benefit and one cost of Roman rule with evidence.

## Unit 4 — The Kingdom of Kush

**Essential question:** Why was Kush one of Africa's great powers, and why is its story less well known than Egypt's?

**Key vocabulary:** Nubia, Kush, Kerma, Napata, Meroë, pharaoh, 25th Dynasty, pyramid, iron, trade, kandake, Meroitic script, decipher, Aksum

### Lesson 20 — Nubia and the Nile
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can locate Kush and explain how the Nile and its resources made it powerful." (UK NC KS2 Hi (a non-European society))
- **Hook:** "South of Egypt, along the Nile, there are more pyramids than in Egypt itself. Who built them?"
- **Key activity:** Map of the Nile from Egypt into modern Sudan; mark the cataracts, Kerma, Napata and Meroë. Explore Kush's resources — gold, ivory, ebony, cattle — and trade routes to Egypt, the Red Sea and inner Africa. Place Kush on the class timeline beside Egypt.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why was Kush's position on the Nile important for its wealth?" → the river gave water for farming and a route for trade, and Kush controlled goods such as gold and ivory travelling north
- **Differentiation:** support: a map with places pre-labelled / stretch: explain why the cataracts (rapids) made Kush easier to defend
- **Materials:** maps, atlas, photos of the Meroë pyramids
- **Joy:** count and compare photos of Egyptian and Nubian pyramids

### Lesson 21 — Pharaohs from Kush
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Kushite kings came to rule Egypt as its 25th Dynasty." (UK NC KS2 Hi)
- **Hook:** "For about a century, Egypt was ruled by kings from Kush. How do we know?"
- **Key activity:** Evidence: King Piye's victory stela (inscription), statues of Taharqa, temple building at Jebel Barkal. Children read an adapted extract of Piye's stela and discuss what a victory inscription is designed to do.
- **Check for understanding:** "Piye's stela describes his great victory. Why might we be careful about trusting it completely?" → it was made by Piye to celebrate himself, so it shows his side at its best
- **Differentiation:** support: stela extract simplified with key sentences highlighted / stretch: compare how the Egyptians and Kushites might each have described the same events
- **Materials:** adapted stela extract, photos of Taharqa statues
- **Joy:** "Carve a stela": children write a victory inscription for a class achievement

### Lesson 22 — Meroë: iron and pyramids
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe life in Meroë and explain why it was an important centre of iron-working and trade." (UK NC KS2 Hi)
- **Hook:** "Archaeologists found huge mounds of black slag near Meroë. What were people making?"
- **Key activity:** Evidence of iron-smelting (slag heaps, furnaces), the steep pyramids of Meroë, temples, and trade goods. Children infer from photos of slag and tools; learn how iron tools and weapons helped farming and power.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does the slag at Meroë tell us about what happened there?" → iron was smelted there on a large scale — slag is the waste left over
- **Differentiation:** support: photo cards with 'I see / I think' prompts / stretch: explain how iron-working could make a kingdom richer and stronger
- **Materials:** photos, maps
- **Joy:** make model steep-sided Meroë pyramids from card

### Lesson 23 — Queens of Kush and a mystery script
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the role of the kandakes and explain why Meroitic writing is still a puzzle." (UK NC KS2 Hi (how we know; limits of evidence))
- **Hook:** "Kush had powerful ruling queens. One is said to have fought the Roman army. Why don't we know more?"
- **Key activity:** The kandakes (ruling queens), including Amanirenas, who fought Rome around 25–21 BC. Meroitic script: its letters can be read aloud, but most of the language is still not understood. Children attempt to 'decode' a made-up script using a key, then discuss why the real one is still hard.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can historians read the sounds of Meroitic writing but still not understand most of it?" → they know which sound each letter makes, but the language itself isn't fully understood — there's no complete translation like the Rosetta Stone for it
- **Differentiation:** support: decoding game with a full key / stretch: explain what historians would need to fully decipher Meroitic
- **Materials:** made-up script sheets, photos of Meroitic inscriptions
- **Joy:** "Code-breakers" decoding challenge

### Lesson 24 — Unit review: why Kush matters
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the significance of Kush and why its history has often been overlooked." (UK NC KS2 Hi (significance; interpretation))
- **Hook:** "Why do so many people know about Egypt but not about Kush?"
- **Key activity:** Groups discuss reasons (less studied by European archaeologists until recently; script not deciphered; fewer famous finds; bias in older history books). Each child writes a short 'Kush deserves to be famous because…' paragraph with two pieces of evidence.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give two reasons why Kush was important and one reason it has been overlooked." → e.g. it ruled Egypt and was a major iron-working and trading power; it was overlooked because its script is undeciphered and older histories focused on Egypt
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence frames and evidence cards / stretch: design a museum display that would make Kush famous, justifying each object
- **Materials:** evidence cards, writing frames
- **Joy:** "Kush museum" poster exhibition

**End-of-unit check:** Locate Kush on a map and explain the Nile's importance; explain how Kushite kings ruled Egypt and how we know; describe iron-working at Meroë; give two reasons Kush was important and one reason it has been overlooked.

## Unit 5 — Ancient China: from the Shang to the Han and the Silk Road

**Essential question:** How did early Chinese dynasties create a civilisation whose inventions and ideas still shape the world?

**Key vocabulary:** dynasty, Shang, Zhou, Mandate of Heaven, Qin, Han, emperor, oracle bone, bronze, Confucius, Terracotta Army, Great Wall, silk, paper, Silk Road, trade, merchant, invention

### Lesson 25 — The Shang revisited: how the oracle bones were found
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the oracle bones were discovered and why they are such strong evidence about the Shang." (UK NC KS2 Hi (the Shang Dynasty; how we know))
- **Hook:** "In 1899 a scholar noticed ancient writing on 'dragon bones' sold as medicine. Where had they come from?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of Year 2's Shang (bronze, oracle bones, earliest Chinese writing). New: the detective story of the discovery — scholars tracked the bones to farmland near Anyang, and excavations there from 1928 uncovered the last Shang capital (Yin), confirming kings named in much later histories. Children compare what the bones say with what later written histories said.
- **Check for understanding:** "Later Chinese histories listed the Shang kings, but some people doubted they were real. How did the oracle bones settle the argument?" → the bones, made at the time, name many of the same kings, so an independent primary source confirmed the later written list
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture sequence of the discovery / stretch: explain why oracle bones are especially reliable (made at the time for the king's private questions, not for show) — and what they still can't tell us (ordinary people's lives)
- **Materials:** photos of oracle bones and the Anyang excavations, character cards, clay tiles
- **Joy:** carve an oracle-bone question into a clay tile

### Lesson 26 — The Zhou and the Mandate of Heaven
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the idea of the Mandate of Heaven and how it was used to explain why dynasties rose and fell." (UK NC KS2 Hi (beliefs; change over time))
- **Hook:** "In about 1046 BC the Zhou overthrew the Shang. How do you persuade people that your takeover is right?"
- **Key activity:** The Zhou claimed Heaven gave the right to rule to a good ruler and took it away from a cruel or careless one — signs of losing it were floods, famines and rebellions. Children sort event cards from Shang to Han into a 'dynasty cycle' (a strong new dynasty → good rule → decline and disasters → rebellion → new dynasty); then look at the long Zhou period, ending in the Warring States that the Qin would end.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why was the Mandate of Heaven a useful idea for the Zhou — and why might it also have worried later emperors?" → it made their rebellion look right, because the Shang had 'lost' Heaven's approval; but it meant any ruler who ruled badly, or whose land suffered disasters, could be overthrown too
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture cycle with four stages to label / stretch: compare the Mandate of Heaven with how Athenians chose leaders — which gave ordinary people more say?
- **Materials:** dynasty-cycle cards, timeline, map of the Warring States
- **Joy:** "Heaven's verdict": groups act out a ruler, a disaster and a rebellion while the class judges whether the Mandate has been lost

### Lesson 27 — Confucius and ideas that lasted
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain some of Confucius's ideas and why they influenced Chinese society for over 2,000 years." (UK NC KS2 Hi (beliefs; legacy))
- **Hook:** "What makes a good ruler, a good family member, a good friend? A teacher in China asked this about 2,500 years ago — and people still quote his answers."
- **Key activity:** Brief life of Confucius (551–479 BC) as a teacher; his sayings were collected by his followers in the *Analects*. Children discuss simple sayings (e.g. 'Do not do to others what you would not want done to yourself'), sort them into themes (learning, respect, good rulers), and connect to how Han emperors later chose officials by education.
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose one saying. What does it mean, and why might an emperor have liked Confucius's ideas?" → explains the meaning in their own words; an emperor would value ideas about respect, order and loyalty
- **Differentiation:** support: picture-supported sayings with a meaning to match / stretch: explain how Confucius's ideas about a good ruler fit with the Mandate of Heaven
- **Materials:** saying cards, theme sort sheets
- **Joy:** class 'wisdom wall' of sayings in brush-pen calligraphy

### Lesson 28 — The first emperor and the Terracotta Army
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Qin Shi Huang unified China and what the Terracotta Army reveals." (UK NC KS2 Hi)
- **Hook:** "In 1974, farmers digging a well found a clay soldier. There were thousands more."
- **Key activity:** Qin Shi Huang unified China in 221 BC — standard writing, coins, weights and measures; the Terracotta Army near Xi'an. Children examine photos (each face different) and infer.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might the first emperor have wanted thousands of clay soldiers buried near his tomb?" → to protect him in the afterlife and show his power
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards / stretch: explain why standardising writing and coins helped unite China
- **Materials:** photos, clay
- **Joy:** sculpt a clay warrior head

### Lesson 29 — The Great Wall
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why walls were built along China's northern frontier and how the Great Wall changed over time." (UK NC KS2 Hi (change over time; myths vs evidence))
- **Hook:** "Is the Great Wall one wall, built by one emperor, that you can see from space?"
- **Key activity:** Timeline of wall-building: earlier states' walls, joined and extended under Qin Shi Huang, extended under the Han, and largely rebuilt in stone and brick under the Ming (1368–1644), which is what most visitors see today. Children test the 'visible from space' claim against astronauts' statements (it's not visible to the naked eye from orbit in normal conditions).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it wrong to say the Great Wall was built by one emperor?" → it was built, joined, rebuilt and extended by many dynasties over about 2,000 years; most surviving sections are Ming
- **Differentiation:** support: timeline cards with pictures of wall styles / stretch: explain how a myth like 'visible from space' spreads and how you'd check it
- **Materials:** photos, timeline cards
- **Joy:** build a wall section with sugar cubes or clay bricks

### Lesson 30 — The Han dynasty: inventions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe inventions from Han China and judge their impact on the world." (UK NC KS2 Hi (achievements; significance))
- **Hook:** "Paper, a seismograph that detected distant earthquakes, the wheelbarrow — which Han-era invention changed the world most?"
- **Key activity:** Stations on Han-era inventions and developments (improved paper-making, traditionally credited to Cai Lun in AD 105; Zhang Heng's seismoscope; the wheelbarrow; the ship's rudder). Children make paper from recycled pulp; then rank inventions by impact with reasons.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which invention do you think had the greatest impact, and why?" → a reasoned choice, e.g. paper — it made books cheaper, spreading learning and record-keeping
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards with one sentence about each invention / stretch: explain why historians say Cai Lun 'improved' rather than 'invented' paper (earlier forms of paper have been found), and find out about Ban Zhao, a Han woman historian who helped finish the official history of the Han
- **Materials:** paper-making kit (pulp, screens, sponges), picture cards
- **Joy:** make paper and write your name in Chinese characters on it

### Lesson 31 — The Silk Road
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the Silk Road connected Han China with Central Asia, India, Persia and the Roman world." (UK NC KS2 Hi (connections; trade))
- **Hook:** "Roman writers complained about how much money was spent on Chinese silk — yet almost no Roman ever reached China. How did the silk arrive?"
- **Key activity:** Map of Silk Road routes (land and sea); Zhang Qian's journeys west (2nd century BC). A relay trading game: goods pass hand to hand across 'stations' (Chang'an, Central Asian oases, Persia, Syria, Rome), with prices rising at each stop; ideas (Buddhism), technologies and diseases travel too.
- **Check for understanding:** "What travelled along the Silk Road besides silk, and why did few people travel the whole route?" → spices, horses, glass, paper, ideas and religions (such as Buddhism) and diseases; most traders only travelled one section and passed goods on
- **Differentiation:** support: a map with the stations and goods pictured / stretch: explain why silk was so expensive by the time it reached Rome
- **Materials:** maps, goods picture cards, play coins
- **Joy:** "Silk Road relay" trading game

**End-of-unit check:** Explain what oracle bones tell us and why they are reliable; explain the Mandate of Heaven; describe what the Terracotta Army reveals about Qin Shi Huang; explain why the Great Wall was built over centuries; describe how the Silk Road connected China and Rome.

## Unit 6 — Connected worlds and the ancient past in our town

**Essential question:** How were the ancient civilisations connected, and how does the ancient world still reach the place where I live?

**Key vocabulary:** contemporary, overlap, connection, trade, legacy, local history, enquiry, evidence, museum, place name, heritage

### Lesson 32 — Who could have met whom?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use the class timeline to identify which civilisations existed at the same time and how they were connected." (UK NC KS2 Hi (chronology; connections))
- **Hook:** "Could a Kushite pharaoh have watched the first Olympic Games? Could a Roman have bought Han silk?"
- **Key activity:** Using the year's washing-line timeline, children find overlaps (Kushite 25th Dynasty c. 744–656 BC and the early Olympics from 776 BC; Han China and the Roman Empire both at their height around AD 100). Groups prepare 'meeting' cards: two people from different civilisations who lived at the same time, and how they might have been connected (trade, war, ideas).
- **Check for understanding:** "Name two civilisations that existed at the same time and describe one real connection between them." → e.g. Han China and Rome, linked by Silk Road trade; or Kush and Egypt, linked by the Nile, trade and conquest
- **Differentiation:** support: a timeline with overlaps already shaded / stretch: calculate how long each civilisation lasted and compare durations
- **Materials:** class timeline, date cards, maps
- **Joy:** "Time-travel dinner party": role-play two contemporaries meeting

### Lesson 33 — The ancient world in our language and lives
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify legacies of Greece, Rome, Kush and China in everyday life today." (UK NC KS2 Hi (legacy))
- **Hook:** "Before breakfast you've probably used something from each civilisation we studied. Let's prove it."
- **Key activity:** Legacy hunt: words (Greek 'telephone', Latin 'aqua'), calendar months (July, August from Roman rulers), paper, silk, democracy, the Olympic Games, straight roads, Roman numerals on clocks. Children photograph or list legacies found at school and home.
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose a legacy you found and explain which civilisation it comes from and how you know." → names the legacy, its origin and a reason (e.g. 'August' named after Emperor Augustus)
- **Differentiation:** support: a checklist of 10 legacies with pictures / stretch: find a legacy from one of the civilisations that most people wouldn't notice and explain it
- **Materials:** legacy checklists, cameras/tablets
- **Joy:** "Legacy scavenger hunt" around school

### Lesson 34 — Local history enquiry: how old is our place?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan and carry out a mini-enquiry into the oldest evidence of people in our own town or region." (UK NC KS2 Hi (local history study); adaptable worldwide)
- **Hook:** "What is the oldest thing in our town, and how do we know how old it is?"
- **Key activity:** The teacher prepares local sources (a museum object photo, an old map, place-name meanings, a local archaeological find or ancient site). Worked example: in Britain, a Roman road or place-name ending in '-chester' (from Latin castra); elsewhere, the region's own ancient site or oldest building. Children ask questions, examine sources and present one finding.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is your finding, and which source proves it?" → a finding about the local past supported by a named source (e.g. an old map showing a Roman road's line)
- **Differentiation:** support: a single source with guiding questions / stretch: compare two sources that disagree or leave gaps, and say what else you'd need to find out
- **Materials:** local maps, museum photos, place-name dictionary, visiting local historian if possible
- **Joy:** a walk to a local historic site or the local museum

### Lesson 35 — History fair: the ancient world
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present what I have learned about an ancient civilisation, using evidence to answer a big question." (UK NC KS2 Hi (communicate historical knowledge))
- **Hook:** "Which ancient civilisation would you most like to visit — and can you convince us with evidence?"
- **Key activity:** Each child chooses a civilisation and answers the question 'What made it remarkable?' with three pieces of evidence (a source, an achievement, a legacy) on a museum-style board; history fair for visitors; peers ask 'How do we know?'.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the strongest piece of evidence on your board, and why is it strong?" → names a primary source and explains why it's reliable (e.g. made at the time, not for show)
- **Differentiation:** support: a template board with the three boxes and labelled images / stretch: include an interpretation from a different point of view (e.g. a conquered people's)
- **Materials:** display boards, printed images, replica objects
- **Joy:** "Ancient world fair" with visitor passports stamped at each stall

**End-of-unit check:** Name two civilisations that existed at the same time and describe a connection; identify three ancient legacies in modern life; present one local-history finding with a named source.

## Books & resources

- ***D'Aulaires' Book of Greek Myths* by Ingri and Edgar Parin d'Aulaire** — shared with English; the myths show what Greeks believed, a key historical source of ideas.
- ***DK Eyewitness: Ancient Greece* and *DK Eyewitness: Ancient Rome*** — reliable, photograph-rich reference books full of real artefacts, ideal for 'how do we know?' questions.
- ***You Wouldn't Want to Be a Roman Gladiator!* (the *You Wouldn't Want to…* series, Salariya)** — funny and accurate; hooks reluctant readers into daily life (use alongside primary sources).
- ***Escape from Pompeii* by Christina Balit** — a picture book set during the eruption of AD 79; a vivid, evidence-based way into Pompeii.
- ***The Roman Mysteries* by Caroline Lawrence** (e.g. *The Thieves of Ostia*) — historical detective novels set in the Roman world, for confident independent readers.
- ***Where the Mountain Meets the Moon* by Grace Lin** — shared with English; Chinese folklore alongside the Ancient China unit.
- **The British Museum and the Metropolitan Museum of Art online collections** — free high-quality images of Greek vases, Roman coins, Kushite sculpture and Shang bronzes, with object descriptions.
- **The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston (Nubian collection)** — one of the largest collections of Kushite objects outside Sudan, with online images.
- **National Geographic Kids** and **BBC Bitesize (KS2 history)** — short, reliable overviews for independent research (checked against a second source).

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Chronology** — places Greece, Rome, Kush, Shang and Han China, and Year 2's civilisations on a BC/AD timeline and explains how BC dates count. *See it:* the class timeline task; "How many years between 776 BC and AD 43?"
2. **Sources** — distinguishes primary and secondary sources and explains what a given source can and can't tell us. *See it:* an unseen artefact photo with 'useful because… limited because…'.
3. **Interpretation** — explains why two accounts of the same event differ (Boudica; Piye's stela). *See it:* a short written comparison.
4. **Ancient Greece** — explains Athenian democracy (and its limits), compares Athens and Sparta, and names Greek legacies.
5. **The Roman Empire** — explains how the empire grew and what Roman rule meant for different conquered peoples, with Britain and North Africa as examples.
6. **Kush** — locates Kush, explains its wealth and power (trade, iron, ruling Egypt), and why its history has been overlooked.
7. **Ancient China** — explains what oracle bones reveal, the unification under Qin, Han inventions and the Silk Road.
8. **Connections and legacy** — describes a connection between two contemporary civilisations and identifies legacies in daily life and the local area. *See it:* the history fair board.

## Teacher guidance

**"How do we know?" every lesson.** Put a real source on the table (a replica, a museum photo, an adapted extract) in every lesson. Ask: Who made it? When? Why? What does it show? What doesn't it show?

**Common misconceptions to watch for.**
- *BC counts upwards* — use the timeline physically; the bigger the BC number, the longer ago.
- *There was a year 0* — there wasn't: 1 BC is followed by AD 1.
- *All Greeks were Athenians / democracy meant everyone voted* — stress city-states and who was excluded.
- *The Romans were 'civilised' and everyone else 'barbarian'* — that is the Roman viewpoint; explore Boudica, the Kushite kandake Amanirenas and others from their side.
- *Egypt was the only great African civilisation* — Kush ruled Egypt for about a century and built more pyramids than Egypt.
- *The Great Wall was built by one emperor / is visible from space* — both are myths.
- *Ancient people were less intelligent* — they solved hard problems (aqueducts, bronze casting, iron smelting, paper) without modern tools.
- *Legends are history* — Romulus and Remus is a legend; archaeology gives different evidence.

**Sensitive content.** Slavery existed in Greece, Rome and elsewhere; treat it honestly and with care. Warfare and conquest should be discussed from several viewpoints.

**Adapting for your country.** The Roman Britain case study shows how an empire changed a conquered land; schools elsewhere keep it as a case study and use the final unit to connect the ancient world to their own region (e.g. Roman or Greek sites around the Mediterranean, Silk Road cities in Central Asia, ancient kingdoms in Africa or the Americas).

**Differentiation.** Support = more concrete: artefact handling, picture timelines, adapted extracts with key words highlighted. Stretch = deeper historical thinking: compare interpretations, judge reliability, explain change and continuity across civilisations.

**Vertical connections.** Builds on Year 2 (Stone Age to Iron Age, Egypt, Sumer, Indus). Year 4 continues the timeline into the medieval world around AD 900 — the Vikings, Abbasid Baghdad, the Kingdom of Benin and the Maya — using the same source and interpretation skills. Year 2 already taught primary/secondary sources, the tray dig and the Shang basics, so Year 3 retrieves those briefly and goes deeper.
