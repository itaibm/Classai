# Year 3 Music (ages 8–9) — Scope & Sequence

## Year overview

Year 3 music turns children into **readers, players and listeners with ears for how music is built**. On the **recorder** (the Year 2–3 stage of the instrument pathway: percussion → recorder → ukulele/keyboard in Years 4–6) children extend from B, A, G to high C and D and low E and D, reading every note from the **treble stave** and playing simple melodies in rhythm. They learn to hear and create **texture** (layers, drones and ostinatos), recognise **structure** (verse–chorus, ABA, rondo), and travel through **music from around the world** — West African drumming, Brazilian samba, Indian classical music, Japanese taiko, Balinese gamelan and Andean panpipes. A first **map of Western music history** (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, 20th century) places composers from Vivaldi to Florence Price in time, and the year ends with children **composing, notating and performing** their own layered pieces.

**Every lesson** combines singing or playing, listening to a named piece, and thinking about how the music works. Singing continues weekly: rounds, call-and-response songs and songs from many cultures.

**What this year assumes (from Year 2, ages 7–8):** a steady beat; crotchets, quavers and rests read from simple notation; recorder notes B, A and G; improvising short phrases; describing music with pitch, tempo and dynamics. **What Year 4 (ages 9–10) relies on:** reading pitch on the stave and rhythm together, recognising and building ostinatos and drones, simple structures, and composing with notation — for singing in parts, layered two-part playing and composing longer pieces (and the move to ukulele or keyboard).

Lessons are **35 minutes**, one a week, plus daily singing where possible.

## Time budget

35 lessons at 1 a week = 35 weeks. Each unit ends with a performance or a listening conversation instead of a written test.

## Unit 1 — The recorder and the stave

**Essential question:** How do five lines and four spaces tell a musician exactly which note to play?

**Key vocabulary:** stave (staff), treble clef, line, space, note, pitch, crotchet (quarter note), quaver (eighth note), minim (half note), semibreve (whole note), rest, bar, bar line, time signature, recorder, thumb hole, tonguing, breath

### Lesson 1 — Five lines, four spaces
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can name notes on the treble stave using line and space patterns." (UK NC KS2 Music (use and understand staff notation))
- **Hook:** "Every Good Boy Deserves Football — and the spaces spell a word. What is it?"
- **Key activity:** Draw a giant floor stave with masking tape; children stand on lines and spaces to become notes. Teach line notes E G B D F (with a mnemonic children invent) and space notes F A C E. Play 'note detective' with flash cards.
- **Check for understanding:** "How can you tell whether a note sits on a line or in a space?" → a line note has the line running through its middle; a space note sits between two lines
- **Differentiation:** support: a stave card with the note names written in / stretch: read a four-note 'word' on the stave (e.g. B-A-G-E, F-A-C-E)
- **Materials:** masking tape floor stave, flash cards, whiteboards
- **Joy:** "Human stave": children become the notes of a tune

### Lesson 2 — Recorder: revise B, A, G
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play B, A and G with good tone and read them on the stave." (UK NC KS2 Music (play with accuracy, fluency, control))
- **Hook:** "Can you play so softly that the note is pure, not squeaky?"
- **Key activity:** Revise posture, left hand on top, thumb hole covered, gentle 'doo' tonguing and warm breath; echo-play; read B, A, G on the stave and play simple tunes (e.g. 'Hot Cross Buns' in B-A-G).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does blowing too hard make the recorder squeak?" → too much air overblows the note; a gentle, steady breath gives a clear sound
- **Differentiation:** support: a fingering chart and echo-playing with the teacher / stretch: play the tune with a steady beat while a partner claps the rhythm
- **Materials:** descant recorders (one per child, cleaned), fingering charts
- **Joy:** "Squeak patrol": the class listens for the purest note

### Lesson 3 — Recorder: high C and D
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play high C and D and read them on the stave." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "Two new notes up high — and now we can play a real tune!"
- **Key activity:** Teach C' (thumb and second finger) and D' (second finger only, thumb off); pitch ladder; play 'Mary Had a Little Lamb'-style tunes using G–D'.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does lifting fingers make the note higher?" → it shortens the column of vibrating air, which makes a higher pitch (links to Science: sound)
- **Differentiation:** support: fingering chart with coloured dots / stretch: play a 5-note tune from notation alone
- **Materials:** recorders, fingering charts, simple notation
- **Joy:** recorder band call-and-response

### Lesson 4 — Rhythm reading: note values and rests
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read and clap rhythms with semibreves, minims, crotchets, quavers and rests." (UK NC KS2 Music (staff and other notations))
- **Hook:** "A note can last one beat, two, four — or half a beat. How do you know?"
- **Key activity:** Teach note values with a 'rhythm pizza' (whole, halves, quarters, eighths); clap and say rhythms (ta, ta-a, ti-ti); read 4-bar rhythms in 4/4 including rests.
- **Check for understanding:** "How many crotchets fit in a minim, and how do you show silence?" → two crotchets = one minim; a rest shows silence for that many beats
- **Differentiation:** support: rhythm cards with syllables written / stretch: write a 4-bar rhythm for a partner
- **Materials:** rhythm cards, 'pizza' fractions, claves
- **Joy:** rhythm-pizza game (links with maths fractions)

### Lesson 5 — Recorder: low E and D
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play low E and D with a gentle breath and read them on the stave." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "Low notes need the softest breath of all — can you find them?"
- **Key activity:** Teach E and D (covering more holes, sealing gently); practise slow scales from D to D'; read short tunes with low notes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do low notes squeak if you blow too hard or leave a gap under a finger?" → they need very gentle breath and every hole sealed
- **Differentiation:** support: practise E before D, with a partner watching finger seals / stretch: play D–D' scale up and down evenly
- **Materials:** recorders, fingering charts
- **Joy:** "Soft-note challenge"

### Lesson 6 — Playing a notated melody
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play a complete short melody from staff notation with correct pitch and rhythm." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "Today you'll read and play a whole tune — no teacher copying!"
- **Key activity:** Choose a folk tune from another culture in the range (e.g. 'Frère Jacques' as a round, or 'Ode to Joy' theme); practise in sections; perform in groups.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where did you find it hardest to read, and what did you do?" → names a bar and a strategy (slow practice, naming notes first)
- **Differentiation:** support: a version with note names written under / stretch: play the round in two parts
- **Materials:** recorders, notation
- **Joy:** round performance

### Lesson 7 — Unit review: recorder concert
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a melody from notation to an audience with good tone and a steady shared beat." (UK NC KS2 Music (play and perform in ensemble contexts))
- **Hook:** "Our first recorder concert — the audience is waiting!"
- **Key activity:** Groups rehearse an 8-bar tune (notes D–D') from notation with a counted-in start and an agreed ending; some groups add a simple ostinato on glockenspiel; perform to another class; the audience gives feedback on tone, rhythm and keeping together.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did your group do to keep a steady beat together?" → watched a leader, counted in aloud, and listened to each other rather than rushing
- **Differentiation:** support: play the first phrase only, with note names written under / stretch: play an ostinato or harmony part against the tune
- **Materials:** recorders, glockenspiels, notation
- **Joy:** concert with invited guests and printed programmes

**End-of-unit check:** Performance and conversation: play an 8-bar melody from notation using notes D–D'; name notes on the stave; explain note values.

## Unit 2 — Texture: layers, drones and ostinatos

**Essential question:** How do musicians build music from layers that repeat and combine?

**Key vocabulary:** texture, thin, thick, layer, melody, accompaniment, drone, ostinato, rhythmic ostinato, melodic ostinato, unison, round, canon, ground bass

### Lesson 8 — Thin and thick textures
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe texture as thin or thick and explain how layers change it." (UK NC KS2 Music (listen with attention to detail))
- **Hook:** "One voice, then two, then twenty — what changes?"
- **Key activity:** Listen to a solo voice, a duet and a full choir/orchestra; children build texture live: one child hums, others add layers one at a time. Draw texture as stacked lines.
- **Check for understanding:** "What made the texture thicker, and what happened to the sound?" → adding more layers or instruments; it became fuller and richer
- **Differentiation:** support: texture picture cards / stretch: describe texture changes across a whole piece
- **Materials:** recordings, percussion
- **Joy:** "Layer cake" performance

### Lesson 9 — Ostinato: patterns that repeat
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can create and hold a rhythmic ostinato against another part." (UK NC KS2 Music (improvise and compose))
- **Hook:** "Ravel wrote a piece where the same snare-drum rhythm repeats for about 15 minutes! Can you keep going for one?"
- **Key activity:** Listen to the opening of Ravel's *Boléro*; clap its ostinato; children create word-rhythm ostinatos (e.g. 'Pass the pineapple') and layer three at once.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it hard to keep your ostinato steady when other parts are playing?" → other rhythms pull you off; you must listen to the beat and focus on your pattern
- **Differentiation:** support: start with the teacher playing the beat / stretch: layer an ostinato that fits between others' notes
- **Materials:** untuned percussion, word cards
- **Joy:** "Pineapple ostinato" layers

### Lesson 10 — Melodic ostinato and drone
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play a melodic ostinato or drone on recorder or tuned percussion to accompany a song." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "Some music has a note that hums underneath the whole time — like bagpipes."
- **Key activity:** Listen to a drone (bagpipes, Indian tanpura); play a G-D drone on glockenspiels while singing a pentatonic song; add a 4-note melodic ostinato.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does a drone add to the song?" → a steady, grounding sound underneath the melody
- **Differentiation:** support: play a single-note drone / stretch: play a two-note ostinato while singing
- **Materials:** glockenspiels, xylophones, tanpura recording
- **Joy:** drone-and-song performance

### Lesson 11 — Holst's Mars: ostinato that builds tension
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a repeating rhythm can create tension." (UK NC KS2 Music (great composers))
- **Hook:** "Holst's 'Mars, the Bringer of War' has a rhythm that feels like marching machines. Count it — it's in 5!"
- **Key activity:** Listen to 'Mars' from Holst's *The Planets*; tap the 5/4 ostinato; discuss how volume and texture build.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does Holst create tension in 'Mars'?" → a relentless repeating rhythm, growing louder and thicker
- **Differentiation:** support: count with a visual 5-beat grid / stretch: compose a 5-beat ostinato
- **Materials:** recording, percussion
- **Joy:** 'machine march' movement

### Lesson 12 — Gamelan: interlocking layers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain and play interlocking parts in the style of Indonesian gamelan." (UK NC KS2 Music (music from different traditions))
- **Hook:** "In Bali and Java, a whole orchestra of gongs and metallophones plays like the cogs of one giant clock."
- **Key activity:** Watch and listen to a gamelan ensemble; notice the slow gong marking cycles, the steady core melody and fast decorating parts. In pairs on glockenspiels or chime bars, play an interlocking pattern (one plays on the beat, the other off the beat, together making a continuous line); layer a slow 'gong' on a drum or cymbal marking every 8 beats.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does 'interlocking' mean in gamelan music, and why must the players listen so carefully?" → two parts fill each other's gaps to make one fast pattern; if one player drifts, the combined pattern breaks
- **Differentiation:** support: a two-note on-beat/off-beat pattern with a visual beat grid / stretch: add a third, slower core-melody layer and explain its role
- **Materials:** glockenspiels, chime bars, a gong or cymbal, gamelan recording, map of Indonesia
- **Joy:** class 'gamelan' performance with the lights dimmed

### Lesson 13 — Unit review: layered performance
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a layered piece combining melody, drone and ostinato and explain each layer's job." (UK NC KS2 Music (perform; appraise))
- **Hook:** "Build a piece like an architect: foundations, walls, then the decoration."
- **Key activity:** Groups of five build a piece on a pentatonic scale: a drone (foundation), a rhythmic ostinato, a melodic ostinato, and a melody on recorder or voice; rehearse, perform and record; listeners identify the layers.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which layer did you play, what was its job, and how did the texture change when your layer entered?" → names the layer and its role (e.g. the drone keeps a steady base) and describes the texture thickening
- **Differentiation:** support: play the drone or a two-note ostinato with a leader / stretch: play the melody over the others and add a dynamic change
- **Materials:** tuned and untuned percussion, recorders
- **Joy:** recorded 'album track' for the class playlist

**End-of-unit check:** Performance and conversation: play or sing in a layered piece and explain the roles of drone, ostinato and melody.

## Unit 3 — Structure: how music is organised

**Essential question:** How do composers organise music so that listeners can follow it?

**Key vocabulary:** structure, form, section, repeat, contrast, verse, chorus, bridge, binary (AB), ternary (ABA), rondo (ABACA), theme, episode, call and response, introduction, coda

### Lesson 14 — Verse and chorus
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify verse and chorus in songs and explain how they differ." (UK NC KS2 Music (listen; appraise))
- **Hook:** "Which part of your favourite song does everyone sing along to?"
- **Key activity:** Listen to two songs (e.g. a children's pop song and a folk song); raise a card at each verse and chorus; map the song as a sequence of shapes. Discuss: the chorus repeats words and melody; verses change words.
- **Check for understanding:** "How can you tell the chorus from a verse?" → the chorus has the same words and tune each time; each verse has new words to the same verse tune
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards for verse/chorus / stretch: spot a bridge or introduction and explain its purpose
- **Materials:** recordings, shape cards
- **Joy:** "Card-raise" listening game

### Lesson 15 — ABA: ternary form
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise and create music in ternary (ABA) form." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "'Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star' is a musical sandwich. Where's the filling?"
- **Key activity:** Sing 'Twinkle, Twinkle' and map it as A–B–A; listen to a ternary piece; groups compose an A section (4 bars) and a contrasting B section on tuned percussion and perform ABA.
- **Check for understanding:** "What makes your B section contrast with A?" → a different pitch range, rhythm, dynamics or instrument
- **Differentiation:** support: A section given; children create B / stretch: add a short introduction and coda
- **Materials:** glockenspiels, recordings
- **Joy:** 'musical sandwich' with a bread-and-filling visual

### Lesson 16 — Rondo: the returning theme
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise rondo form (ABACA) in a famous piece." (UK NC KS2 Music (great composers))
- **Hook:** "Beethoven's 'Für Elise' keeps coming home to the same tune. Can you catch it every time?"
- **Key activity:** Listen to 'Für Elise' (a rondo) and Mozart's 'Rondo alla Turca'; children hold up an 'A' card whenever the main theme returns; map the form.
- **Check for understanding:** "How did you know when the A theme came back?" → recognised the same melody (the rising and falling opening figure) returning after a contrasting section
- **Differentiation:** support: play the A theme on a loop first so it's familiar / stretch: describe how each episode (B, C) contrasts with A
- **Materials:** recordings, letter cards
- **Joy:** "Catch the theme" game

### Lesson 17 — Composing a class rondo
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compose and perform a class rondo with a returning theme and contrasting episodes." (UK NC KS2 Music (compose))
- **Hook:** "Everyone plays A together — then each group gets a turn in the spotlight."
- **Key activity:** Class learns an A section (a rhythm chant or recorder tune); groups compose 8-beat episodes (B, C, D) with contrasting instruments and dynamics; perform ABACADA.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does the A section need to be easy to remember?" → it returns several times and holds the piece together for listeners
- **Differentiation:** support: groups given a rhythm template for their episode / stretch: include a dynamic or tempo contrast and notate it
- **Materials:** percussion, recorders, rhythm grids
- **Joy:** class rondo performance

### Lesson 18 — Call and response
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform call-and-response songs and explain where this structure comes from." (UK NC KS2 Music (music from different traditions))
- **Hook:** "One voice calls, the whole group answers — music as conversation."
- **Key activity:** Learn a call-and-response song from West Africa (e.g. 'Che Che Koolay', a Ghanaian children's singing game) and a work song; children take turns as leader improvising calls.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the role of the leader and of the group in call and response?" → the leader sings a call (which can change); the group answers with a response (often the same)
- **Differentiation:** support: echo response first / stretch: improvise a new call that the group can answer
- **Materials:** drums, song sheets
- **Joy:** leader-and-group singing circle

### Lesson 19 — Unit review: structure detective
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify the structure of unfamiliar pieces and explain my reasoning." (UK NC KS2 Music (appraise))
- **Hook:** "Three mystery pieces. Can you crack their structure?"
- **Key activity:** Children listen to three short unfamiliar pieces and map each using letters; discuss answers and evidence.
- **Check for understanding:** "What structure did you hear in piece 2, and what clues told you?" → names the form (e.g. ABA) and describes hearing the return of the opening
- **Differentiation:** support: a choice of three form diagrams to match / stretch: explain where a piece surprised you by not following a standard form
- **Materials:** recordings, letter cards
- **Joy:** "Structure detective" badges

**End-of-unit check:** Listening conversation: map the structure of a heard piece (verse–chorus, ABA or rondo) with reasons; perform in the class rondo.

## Unit 4 — Music around the world

**Essential question:** How do music traditions around the world use rhythm, melody and instruments differently — and what do they share?

**Key vocabulary:** tradition, djembe, polyrhythm, samba, batucada, surdo, tamborim, agogô, raga, tala, sitar, tabla, taiko, panpipes (siku), charango, improvise, cycle

### Lesson 20 — West African drumming
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play interlocking djembe rhythms and follow a lead drummer's signals." (UK NC KS2 Music (music from different traditions))
- **Hook:** "In many West African communities, drums call people together, tell stories and keep the dance going."
- **Key activity:** Watch a West African drum ensemble (e.g. from Ghana, Guinea or Senegal); learn bass, tone and slap sounds on hand drums (or buckets); play three layered patterns and respond to a lead drummer's call signals to start and stop.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does the lead drummer do, and how do the other drummers know when to change?" → the lead drummer plays call signals that tell the group when to start, stop or change pattern
- **Differentiation:** support: play the simplest bass-note pattern / stretch: switch between two patterns on the leader's signal
- **Materials:** djembes or buckets, recordings, map of West Africa
- **Joy:** drum circle with a 'call' signal

### Lesson 21 — Brazilian samba
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play in a samba band with layered parts and a call-and-response structure." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "Rio's carnival is powered by hundreds of drummers playing together — how do they stay together?"
- **Key activity:** Learn the samba instruments (surdo keeps the pulse, tamborim, agogô bells, shakers); layer parts; the leader's whistle and 'apito' calls signal breaks. Links to Spanish/Portuguese-speaking South America.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the surdo so important in samba?" → it plays the steady, deep pulse that everyone else locks onto
- **Differentiation:** support: play the surdo pulse part / stretch: play a 'break' pattern from memory on the whistle signal
- **Materials:** samba kit or classroom substitutes, whistle
- **Joy:** 'carnival' parade around the playground

### Lesson 22 — Indian classical music: raga and tala
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how raga and tala work and improvise over a drone." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "A sitar player can improvise for an hour on just a few notes. How?"
- **Key activity:** Listen to Ravi Shankar playing a raga; notice the drone (tanpura), the melody (sitar) and rhythmic cycle (tabla, tala). Clap a simple tala cycle (e.g. 8 beats); improvise on a limited set of notes over a drone on glockenspiels.
- **Check for understanding:** "What are raga and tala?" → a raga is the set of notes and rules for a melody; a tala is the repeating rhythmic cycle
- **Differentiation:** support: improvise with three notes only / stretch: improvise a short phrase that ends on the drone note at the end of the cycle
- **Materials:** glockenspiels, drone recording, video of Ravi Shankar
- **Joy:** 'raga relay' improvisation circle

### Lesson 23 — Japanese taiko drumming
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a taiko-style piece using big movements, dynamics and unison." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "Taiko drummers play with their whole body — it's music and martial art together."
- **Key activity:** Watch taiko; learn kiai (spirited shouts), strong stances and unison strikes on gym balls with drumsticks or tyres; perform a short piece with loud/soft contrasts.
- **Check for understanding:** "How do taiko drummers stay perfectly together?" → they watch each other, move in unison and use shouts and counting to keep time
- **Differentiation:** support: copy a 4-beat pattern / stretch: lead a section with a call
- **Materials:** gym balls in baskets, drumsticks (or tyres)
- **Joy:** 'fitness drumming' taiko style

### Lesson 24 — Andean music: panpipes and charango
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe Andean instruments and play a simple panpipe-style melody." (UK NC KS2 Music; links to Spanish and geography)
- **Hook:** "High in the Andes, music is played on pipes made of cane."
- **Key activity:** Listen to Andean music (siku panpipes, quena, charango); link to the straw panpipes made in science; play a pentatonic tune on recorders in the Andean style with a drone.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does a panpipe make different pitches?" → each pipe is a different length — shorter pipes make higher notes
- **Differentiation:** support: play the tune's opening phrase / stretch: play a two-part version with a partner as siku players often do (sharing notes between two players)
- **Materials:** recorders, straw panpipes from science, recordings
- **Joy:** Andean ensemble performance

### Lesson 25 — Unit review: world music festival
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare two music traditions and perform in one." (UK NC KS2 Music (appraise; perform))
- **Hook:** "Welcome to our World Music Festival!"
- **Key activity:** Groups perform a short piece from one tradition; listeners compare it with another using musical vocabulary (rhythm, instruments, texture, structure).
- **Check for understanding:** "Compare two traditions we studied. What is one similarity and one difference?" → e.g. both samba and West African drumming use layered rhythms and a leader's calls; samba uses whistles and a steady surdo pulse, while djembe uses hand-drum sounds
- **Differentiation:** support: comparison prompts / stretch: explain how a tradition connects to its place and people
- **Materials:** instruments, costumes, map
- **Joy:** festival with flags on a world map

**End-of-unit check:** Listening conversation: compare two world traditions using musical vocabulary; perform a part in one tradition's style.

## Unit 5 — A first map of music history

**Essential question:** How has Western classical music changed over 400 years, and how can we hear the differences?

**Key vocabulary:** era, Baroque, Classical, Romantic, 20th century, composer, orchestra, harpsichord, piano, symphony, concerto, programme music, ragtime, jazz

### Lesson 26 — Baroque: Vivaldi's Four Seasons
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe features of Baroque music and hear how Vivaldi paints a season in sound." (UK NC KS2 Music (great composers; history of music))
- **Hook:** "Can a violin sound like birdsong, a thunderstorm or someone slipping on ice?"
- **Key activity:** Listen to 'Spring' and 'Winter' from Vivaldi's *The Four Seasons* (1720s); spot the harpsichord, busy continuous rhythms and 'terraced' (sudden) dynamics; match passages to the pictures Vivaldi described. Place Baroque (about 1600–1750) on a timeline alongside Bach.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does Vivaldi make 'Winter' sound cold?" → shivering repeated notes, sharp short sounds, and stamping rhythms suggest chattering teeth and cold feet
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards to match to passages / stretch: identify a harpsichord and explain why Baroque music often has sudden loud/soft changes
- **Materials:** recordings, picture cards, timeline
- **Joy:** draw the season as you listen

### Lesson 27 — Classical: Mozart and Haydn
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe features of Classical-era music, including balanced phrases and surprise." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "Haydn put a sudden loud chord in a quiet piece to wake up a sleepy audience — or so the story goes!"
- **Key activity:** Listen to the second movement of Haydn's 'Surprise' Symphony (No. 94) and Mozart's *Eine kleine Nachtmusik*; notice balanced question-and-answer phrases, the piano replacing harpsichord, clear melodies. Place Classical (about 1750–1820) on the timeline.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the 'surprise' in Haydn's symphony, and how does it work?" → a very quiet, gentle tune is suddenly interrupted by a loud chord — the contrast in dynamics surprises the listener
- **Differentiation:** support: a listening grid with pictures / stretch: explain what makes Classical phrases sound balanced (question and answer of equal length)
- **Materials:** recordings, timeline
- **Joy:** 'surprise' game: freeze on the loud chord

### Lesson 28 — Romantic: music that tells stories
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how Romantic composers used big orchestras and emotion to tell stories." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "A troll king's hall, a sugar plum fairy, a dancing orchestra of flowers — Romantic composers told stories without words."
- **Key activity:** Listen to Grieg's 'In the Hall of the Mountain King' (tempo and dynamics grow) and Tchaikovsky's 'Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy' (the celesta); hear Clara Schumann's piano music; place Romantic (about 1820–1900) on the timeline.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does Grieg build excitement in 'In the Hall of the Mountain King'?" → the same tune repeats getting faster, louder and with more instruments
- **Differentiation:** support: tempo and dynamics cards to raise / stretch: compare how Grieg and Vivaldi each tell a story in sound
- **Materials:** recordings, timeline
- **Joy:** 'Mountain King' chase in the hall (walk-then-run)

### Lesson 29 — The 20th century: ragtime, jazz and new voices
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how music changed in the 20th century and place four eras in order by listening." (UK NC KS2 Music)
- **Hook:** "Syncopation! Why does ragtime make your feet want to tap off the beat?"
- **Key activity:** Listen to Scott Joplin's 'The Entertainer' (ragtime syncopation), Louis Armstrong (jazz improvisation) and part of Florence Price's Symphony No. 1 (the first symphony by a Black woman composer performed by a major American orchestra, in 1933). Then play 'era detective': order four unlabelled extracts on the timeline with reasons.
- **Check for understanding:** "Put these four extracts in order of era and explain one clue for each." → e.g. harpsichord = Baroque; balanced phrases and piano = Classical; big emotional orchestra = Romantic; syncopation/jazz = 20th century
- **Differentiation:** support: era clue cards with pictures / stretch: explain what syncopation is and clap an example
- **Materials:** recordings, timeline, clue cards
- **Joy:** 'era detective' listening game

**End-of-unit check:** Listening conversation: place four extracts on the timeline (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, 20th century) with a musical clue for each; name a composer from each era.

## Unit 6 — Compose, notate and perform

**Essential question:** How do composers turn ideas into music that others can read and perform?

**Key vocabulary:** compose, pentatonic scale, melody, phrase, question and answer, notation, graphic score, rhythm grid, dynamics, tempo, ensemble, rehearse, perform, evaluate

### Lesson 30 — The pentatonic scale
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can improvise melodies on the pentatonic scale and explain why they always 'fit'." (UK NC KS2 Music (improvise and compose))
- **Hook:** "Take away two notes and — magic — every note sounds good together. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Remove F and B bars from glockenspiels to leave C D E G A (pentatonic); pairs improvise question-and-answer phrases over a drone; listen to pentatonic tunes from China, Scotland and West Africa.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do improvised pentatonic melodies usually sound good together?" → the pentatonic scale has no notes that clash sharply when played together (no semitones)
- **Differentiation:** support: improvise with three notes / stretch: end every 'answer' phrase on the home note C
- **Materials:** glockenspiels with bars removed
- **Joy:** 'question-and-answer' musical conversations

### Lesson 31 — Planning a composition
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a composition with a clear structure and mood." (UK NC KS2 Music (compose))
- **Hook:** "Your group will compose a piece called 'Journey' — through a jungle, a city or space."
- **Key activity:** Groups choose a theme and plan an ABA or rondo structure, choosing instruments, dynamics and tempo for each section, and a drone or ostinato layer.
- **Check for understanding:** "What structure will your piece have, and how will section B contrast with A?" → names the structure and a specific contrast
- **Differentiation:** support: a planning grid with choices / stretch: plan a transition between sections
- **Materials:** planning grids, instruments
- **Joy:** 'composer's desk' planning

### Lesson 32 — Composing and notating
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compose melodies and rhythms for our piece and record them in staff notation or a graphic score." (UK NC KS2 Music (use and understand staff and other musical notations))
- **Hook:** "If you can't write it down, can anyone else play it — or can you remember it next week?"
- **Key activity:** Groups compose each section on instruments, then notate: rhythm on a grid, melody on the stave (using the notes learned on recorder) or as a graphic score with a key for pitch, length and dynamics. Test the notation by asking another group to play one line from it.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show how your notation tells another player what to play and how loudly." → explains the symbols for pitch, rhythm and dynamics, and the other group could play it accurately
- **Differentiation:** support: a graphic-score template with a symbol key / stretch: notate the main melody on the treble stave with correct note values
- **Materials:** rhythm grids, manuscript paper, graphic-score templates, instruments
- **Joy:** 'score swap': another group performs your notation

### Lesson 33 — Rehearsing and refining
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can rehearse our composition, listen back to a recording and improve it." (UK NC KS2 Music (appraise and improve))
- **Hook:** "Every professional band records, listens back and changes things. Let's be professionals."
- **Key activity:** Groups rehearse, record on a tablet, and listen back with a checklist (steady beat, clear structure, contrast between sections, dynamics, a strong ending); they choose two improvements and re-record.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did you notice when you listened back, and what did you change?" → names a specific issue (e.g. the ostinato sped up) and the change made (e.g. a player counted in and kept the beat)
- **Differentiation:** support: a two-item checklist and adult support to operate the recording / stretch: add a planned dynamic build or a coda and explain its effect
- **Materials:** instruments, tablets for recording, checklists
- **Joy:** 'recording studio' with a red 'on air' light

### Lesson 34 — Performance day
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform our composition to an audience with confidence and control." (UK NC KS2 Music (perform in ensemble contexts))
- **Hook:** "Tonight's concert: premieres of brand-new music by Year 3 composers!"
- **Key activity:** Each group introduces its piece (title, structure, instruments, the journey it describes) and performs; the audience listens for the structure and gives 'two stars and a wish' using musical vocabulary.
- **Check for understanding:** "Introduce your piece: what is its structure and how does section B contrast with A?" → names the structure and describes a clear contrast (instrument, dynamics, tempo or pitch)
- **Differentiation:** support: perform the drone or ostinato part with a leader / stretch: perform the melody and lead the group's entries
- **Materials:** instruments, programme, chairs set as a concert hall
- **Joy:** concert with printed programmes and an interval

### Lesson 35 — Evaluating our year in music
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate our compositions and reflect on my progress as a musician." (UK NC KS2 Music (appraise; develop an understanding of the history of music))
- **Hook:** "Listen to your first recorder recording from September — then to your composition. Who is this musician?"
- **Key activity:** Children listen to recordings of the concert and of their early recorder playing; complete a musician's reflection (what I can now play, read, hear and create; my favourite piece of the year and why); set a goal for Year 4 (e.g. ukulele or keyboard).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the biggest change in you as a musician this year? Give evidence from the recordings." → names a specific change (e.g. reading notes on the stave, holding an ostinato) with evidence
- **Differentiation:** support: reflection prompts with pictures / stretch: write a short review of another group's composition using musical vocabulary
- **Materials:** recordings, reflection sheets
- **Joy:** 'Year 3 album' playlist shared with families

**End-of-unit check:** Performance and conversation: perform in the group composition and explain its structure, notation and one improvement made in rehearsal.

## End-of-year mastery checks

Assessed through performance, listening and conversation, not written tests:
1. **Reads the stave** — names and plays notes from low D to high D on the treble stave. *See it:* sight-reads a 4-bar tune on recorder.
2. **Reads rhythm** — reads and performs semibreves, minims, crotchets, quavers and rests in 4/4. *See it:* claps a 4-bar rhythm from notation.
3. **Plays with control** — plays a recorder melody with good tone, steady beat and correct fingering in an ensemble.
4. **Texture** — holds a drone or ostinato against other parts and describes texture as thin or thick.
5. **Structure** — identifies verse–chorus, ABA and rondo by ear, with reasons.
6. **World music** — describes and plays in at least two world traditions (e.g. samba, West African drumming, gamelan, raga) using correct vocabulary.
7. **Music history** — places four eras in order by listening, with a clue for each and a named composer.
8. **Composes** — composes, notates and performs a structured group piece and explains one improvement made after listening back.

## Teacher guidance

**Sound before symbol.** Children play and sing a pattern before seeing it written. Every lesson includes active listening to a named piece — always ask "What do you notice? What do you wonder?" before giving information.

**Common misconceptions to watch for.**
- *Louder means higher* — pitch (high/low) and dynamics (loud/soft) are different; use gestures for each.
- *Faster means higher* — tempo and pitch are different too.
- *Blowing harder plays higher notes on the recorder* — it only makes squeaks; finger positions change the pitch.
- *Notes on lines and spaces are the same* — check whether the line passes through the note head.
- *Ostinato just means 'repeat'* — it's a pattern repeated persistently throughout a section, usually under or alongside other parts.
- *Classical music means all old music* — 'Classical' is also a specific era (about 1750–1820).
- *Music from other cultures is 'simple'* — gamelan, raga and West African drumming are highly sophisticated systems with years of training.

**Recorder hygiene.** One recorder per child, labelled; clean regularly with warm soapy water; never share mouthpieces.

**Differentiation.** Support = simpler parts (drone, pulse, a single-note ostinato), note names written under notation, playing with a buddy. Stretch = harder parts (melody, harmony, leading), reading without note names, improvising, notating compositions on the stave.

**Vertical connections.** Builds on Year 2's recorder B-A-G, rhythm reading and improvisation. Year 4 adds singing in parts (rounds and two-part songs), full notation of pitch and rhythm, longer layered compositions and the next instrument step (ukulele or keyboard).
