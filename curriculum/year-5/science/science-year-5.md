## Year overview

Year 5 science asks pupils to **think like scientists independently**. Every unit runs on the
working-scientifically cycle — **question → predict → plan a fair test → measure (with repeats) →
record → conclude → evaluate** — and by the end of the year pupils design and run their own
investigation for a science fair.

The year covers all four strands. **Biology:** how living things are classified (Linnaeus to
microorganisms); the circulatory system and a healthy body; evolution and inheritance, from fossils to
natural selection. **Physics:** light travelling in straight lines, reflection, refraction and how we
see; electricity, circuit symbols and how voltage changes brightness. **Chemistry:** the particle
model, identifying substances by their properties, irreversible chemical reactions and what speeds them
up, and a first look at conservation of mass (acids, alkalis and the pH scale are kept for Year 6, and
rusting was investigated in Year 4, so neither is repeated here). **Earth and space:** Earth's atmosphere, the carbon cycle and the scientific evidence for climate
change.

**What this year assumes (from Year 4):** life cycles and reproduction in plants and animals; the
solar system, day and night and the Moon; forces including gravity, friction, air resistance and
simple machines; properties of materials, dissolving, separating mixtures and reversible versus
irreversible changes; and the habit of fair testing with one variable changed.

**What Year 6 relies on:** the particle model of solids, liquids and gases; confident use of repeat
readings, means and line graphs; the idea of chemical reactions making new substances; circuits
drawn with symbols; and the concept of adaptation and natural selection — foundations for cells,
energy, chemical reactions and ecosystems in Year 6.

Scientists from many times and places appear in every unit: Carl Linnaeus, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek,
Ibn al-Nafis, William Harvey, Charles Drew, Mary Anning, Charles Darwin, Alfred Russel Wallace, Gregor
Mendel, Ibn al-Haytham, Isaac Newton, Alessandro Volta, Lewis Latimer, Eunice Foote, Svante Arrhenius
and Wangari Maathai.

## Unit 1 — Classifying living things

**Essential question:** How do scientists sort millions of living things so that we can understand and talk about them?

**Key vocabulary:** classify, classification, kingdom, species, vertebrate, invertebrate, mammal, bird, reptile, amphibian, fish, arthropod, mollusc, microorganism, bacteria, fungi, characteristic, dichotomous key, Linnaeus, binomial name

### Lesson 1 — Why classify?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why scientists classify living things and sort organisms using observable characteristics." (UK NC Y6 living things / NGSS MS-LS1 preview)
- **Hook:** Tip out a box of 30 mixed picture cards (a mushroom, a whale, a spider, an oak, a jellyfish…). "Sort them — any way you like."
- **Key activity:** Groups sort, then share their criteria; discuss which criteria are scientific (observable, consistent) and which aren't ("cute"). Introduce the idea of kingdoms (animals, plants, fungi and microorganisms).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'lives in water' a poor way to classify animals scientifically?" → Very different animals (whales, fish, jellyfish) live in water; it groups them by habitat, not by shared body features.
- **Differentiation:** support: sort into two groups first using one yes/no question / stretch: find an organism that breaks your sorting rule and revise the rule.
- **Materials:** living-thing picture cards, sorting hoops, hand lenses, real specimens (leaves, shells, a mushroom).
- **Joy:** "Mystery organism" — one card is hidden; groups ask yes/no questions to identify it.

### Lesson 2 — Carl Linnaeus and scientific names
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Linnaeus's system works and why every species has a two-part Latin name." (UK NC Y6 living things)
- **Hook:** "A 'robin' in Britain and a 'robin' in North America are different birds. How do scientists avoid confusion?"
- **Key activity:** Linnaeus (Sweden, 1700s) and binomial names (*Homo sapiens*, *Panthera leo*, *Turdus migratorius* for the American robin, *Erithacus rubecula* for the European robin). Pupils decode names and build a nested classification from kingdom to species for a lion.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do lions and tigers share the first part of their scientific names (*Panthera*)?" → They belong to the same genus — they are closely related.
- **Differentiation:** support: match five common names to scientific names with picture support / stretch: invent a scientific name for a new creature and justify both parts.
- **Materials:** classification nesting boxes (or cups), species cards with scientific names, world map.
- **Joy:** "Name that species" — pupils invent Latin names for classroom pets or toys.

### Lesson 3 — Vertebrates
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can classify vertebrates into five groups using their characteristics." (UK NC Y6 living things)
- **Hook:** "A bat flies but isn't a bird. A whale swims but isn't a fish. How do we know?"
- **Key activity:** Characteristics tables for mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish (body covering, breathing, reproduction, temperature regulation). Sort 'tricky' animals (platypus, penguin, dolphin, axolotl, sea snake) with evidence.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a dolphin a mammal and not a fish?" → It breathes air with lungs, is warm-blooded, gives birth to live young and feeds them milk.
- **Differentiation:** support: characteristics table with picture icons / stretch: explain why the platypus (egg-laying mammal) challenged scientists' categories.
- **Materials:** animal fact cards, characteristics tables, skeleton pictures or model skeleton.
- **Joy:** "Classification court" — tricky animals are 'tried' and the jury decides their group.

### Lesson 4 — Invertebrates
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can classify invertebrates into main groups such as arthropods, molluscs and worms using observation." (UK NC Y6 living things)
- **Hook:** "About 97% of animal species have no backbone. Let's go and find some."
- **Key activity:** Minibeast hunt in the school grounds; observe with hand lenses; classify finds into insects (6 legs, 3 body parts), arachnids (8 legs, 2 body parts), crustaceans (e.g. woodlice), myriapods, molluscs, worms. Return animals carefully.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is a spider an insect? How do you know?" → No: it has 8 legs and 2 body parts; insects have 6 legs and 3 body parts.
- **Differentiation:** support: identification chart with pictures / stretch: count legs and body segments to classify an unfamiliar invertebrate and justify.
- **Materials:** hand lenses, bug pots, soft paintbrushes, trays, invertebrate ID charts, gloves.
- **Joy:** the minibeast hunt (with a class tally of finds).

### Lesson 5 — Microorganisms
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe microorganisms (bacteria, some fungi) and give examples of helpful and harmful ones." (UK NC Y6 living things)
- **Hook:** "Antonie van Leeuwenhoek looked through a tiny lens in the 1670s and saw 'animalcules'. What had he found?"
- **Key activity:** Observe yeast activity (yeast + warm water + sugar in a bottle with a balloon on top — the balloon inflates as carbon dioxide is released). Discuss helpful (yeast in bread, bacteria in yoghurt, decomposers) and harmful microbes (disease); link to handwashing.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did the balloon inflate?" → The yeast (a living microorganism) fed on the sugar and released carbon dioxide gas.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided recording sheet with pictures / stretch: plan a fair test to see how temperature affects yeast activity, naming the variables.
- **Materials:** dried yeast, sugar, warm water, plastic bottles, balloons, thermometer, microscope or digital microscope (optional).
- **Joy:** the inflating balloon race between warm and cold bottles.

### Lesson 6 — Investigation: what makes mould grow?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a fair test with one changed variable and record observations over time." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Why does bread go mouldy faster in some places than others?"
- **Key activity:** Groups choose a variable (warmth, moisture, light), plan a fair test with sealed bags of bread, predict, set up, and plan how to measure (percentage of a grid covered each day). Safety: bags remain sealed and are disposed of unopened.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which variables must stay the same in your test, and why?" → e.g. type and size of bread, bag type, time — so only the changed variable affects the result.
- **Differentiation:** support: a partly completed planning sheet / stretch: include a control and explain its purpose.
- **Materials:** bread slices, sealable bags, water sprayer, grid overlays, labels, access to a warm and cool place. Safety: never open mouldy bags.
- **Joy:** "Mould diaries" — daily photos compiled into a time-lapse.

### Lesson 7 — Classification keys
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use and create a dichotomous key to identify living things." (UK NC Y6 living things / working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Can we identify any of these ten leaves with only yes/no questions?"
- **Key activity:** Use a branching key for local trees or invertebrates; then groups build their own key for 8 organisms, testing it on another group.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'Is it big?' a poor question for a key?" → 'Big' is subjective; key questions must be observable and answered the same way by everyone.
- **Differentiation:** support: build a key for 4 organisms with a template / stretch: build a key for 10 organisms including microorganisms and fungi.
- **Materials:** leaf samples or picture cards, key templates, hand lenses.
- **Joy:** "Key-test challenge" — can another group identify your organisms using only your key?

### Lesson 8 — Unit 1 review and mould results
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw conclusions from my mould investigation and show what I know about classification." (UK NC Y6 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Time to open the mould diaries (without opening the bags!)."
- **Key activity:** Groups present results in a table and line graph, draw conclusions and evaluate the fair test; short mastery task on classification.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does your evidence show, and how confident are you? What would make it more reliable?" → A conclusion linked to data, with a sensible improvement (e.g. more repeats, measuring more precisely).
- **Differentiation:** support: conclusion frame ("We found that… because…") / stretch: explain an unexpected result and propose a new test.
- **Materials:** mould bags (sealed), graph paper, mastery task sheet.
- **Joy:** "Mould awards" — the hairiest, the most colourful, the slowest.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil classifies ten organisms into vertebrate and invertebrate groups with reasons, builds a working key for six organisms, and explains one fair-test result using evidence.

## Unit 2 — The circulatory system and a healthy body

**Essential question:** How does the heart keep every part of the body supplied — and how can we look after it?

**Key vocabulary:** circulatory system, heart, blood, blood vessel, artery, vein, capillary, oxygen, carbon dioxide, lungs, pulse, heart rate, nutrients, digestion, lifestyle, diet, exercise, drug, repeat readings, mean

### Lesson 9 — The body's transport system
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can name the parts of the circulatory system and describe their jobs." (UK NC Y6 animals including humans)
- **Hook:** "Your heart has been beating since before you were born. What is it actually doing?"
- **Key activity:** Life-size body outline; pupils add heart, lungs, arteries (away from heart), veins (back to heart), capillaries. Role-play: pupils act as blood cells carrying oxygen 'cards' from the lungs to muscles and returning with carbon dioxide.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does blood need to go to the lungs as well as to the rest of the body?" → To collect oxygen and get rid of carbon dioxide before carrying oxygen to the body.
- **Differentiation:** support: labelled diagram with a word bank / stretch: explain why the heart has two sides (one pumps to the lungs, one to the body).
- **Materials:** large paper body outlines, red and blue wool, oxygen/CO₂ cards, heart model.
- **Joy:** "Human circulation" — the playground becomes a giant circulatory system.

### Lesson 10 — Inside the heart
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the heart as a double pump with four chambers and valves." (UK NC Y6 animals including humans)
- **Hook:** "Make a fist. That's about the size of your heart."
- **Key activity:** Examine a heart model (or, where appropriate and with sensitivity, a real lamb's heart dissection by the teacher with gloves). Pupils label chambers, valves and main vessels; model a valve with a flap of card.
- **Check for understanding:** "What would happen if a heart valve didn't close properly?" → Blood would flow backwards, so the heart would pump less efficiently.
- **Differentiation:** support: a simplified two-sided diagram / stretch: trace the full path of blood from the body, through the heart and lungs, and back to the body.
- **Materials:** heart model, lamb's heart (optional, teacher-handled, gloves, hygiene rules), labelled diagrams.
- **Joy:** hearing a real heartbeat with a stethoscope (or a funnel and tubing).

### Lesson 11 — Who discovered circulation?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how ideas about blood changed through evidence, from Galen to Ibn al-Nafis to William Harvey." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically — scientists over time)
- **Hook:** "For over a thousand years, many doctors believed blood was made in the liver and used up by the body. How did we find out that was wrong?"
- **Key activity:** Timeline cards: Galen (Roman era), Ibn al-Nafis (13th-century Cairo, described blood passing through the lungs), William Harvey (1628, showed blood circulates). Discuss how evidence changes ideas; recreate Harvey's simple arm-vein demonstration with a volunteer's forearm (gentle pressure only).
- **Check for understanding:** "What evidence made Harvey conclude that blood must circulate rather than be constantly made?" → He calculated the heart pumps far more blood in an hour than the body could make, so it must be the same blood going round.
- **Differentiation:** support: timeline cards with pictures / stretch: explain why Ibn al-Nafis's discovery was not widely known in Europe for centuries.
- **Materials:** timeline cards, portraits or images, stopwatch.
- **Joy:** "Harvey's calculation" — estimate how much blood your heart pumps in a day.

### Lesson 12 — Investigation: exercise and heart rate
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan and carry out an investigation into how exercise affects pulse rate, taking repeat readings." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Find your pulse. Now do 30 star jumps. What happens?"
- **Key activity:** Teach how to take a pulse (wrist or neck, 15 seconds × 4). Plan a fair test: resting pulse, pulse after 1, 2 and 3 minutes of exercise, recovery. Take repeat readings and calculate means (maths link).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we take three readings and find the mean rather than just one?" → Single readings can be inaccurate; repeats and a mean make results more reliable.
- **Differentiation:** support: a results table with rows ready and a partner to time / stretch: design an extension (does fitness affect recovery time?) and identify the variables.
- **Materials:** stopwatches, results tables, space for exercise, pulse oximeters (optional).
- **Joy:** exercise stations to music.

### Lesson 13 — Graphing and concluding
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present pulse data in a line graph and write a conclusion with a scientific explanation." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "What does the shape of your graph tell you about your heart?"
- **Key activity:** Draw line graphs of pulse over time (axes, scales, labels); describe the pattern; explain it (muscles need more oxygen during exercise, so the heart beats faster).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does the pulse rate stay high for a while after exercise stops?" → Muscles still need extra oxygen to recover and remove waste, so the heart keeps pumping faster before slowing down.
- **Differentiation:** support: axes pre-drawn with scales / stretch: compare two pupils' recovery curves and suggest why they differ.
- **Materials:** graph paper, rulers, class data, spreadsheet (optional).
- **Joy:** "Heart detectives" — predict an athlete's graph shape and compare with the class's.

### Lesson 14 — Nutrients and water: how they travel
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how nutrients and water are absorbed and transported in the body." (UK NC Y6 animals including humans)
- **Hook:** "Where does the food go after you swallow it?"
- **Key activity:** Model digestion and absorption: squeeze a mixture through a leg of tights (small intestine) to show small particles passing through into 'blood' (a bowl of water). Link to the circulatory system delivering nutrients to cells.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does food need to be broken down before it can enter the blood?" → Only small particles can pass through the intestine wall into the blood.
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled sequence of pictures / stretch: explain why the small intestine is very long and folded (more surface area for absorption).
- **Materials:** crackers, banana, water, zip bag, tights, bowls, scissors (a messy-model kit), aprons.
- **Joy:** the (gloriously messy) digestion model.

### Lesson 15 — Diet, exercise and lifestyle
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how diet, exercise and sleep affect the way the body works." (UK NC Y6 animals including humans)
- **Hook:** "If your body were a car, what would be its fuel, its servicing and its rest?"
- **Key activity:** Analyse a range of meals from different cultures against a balanced plate (proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins and minerals, fibre, water); link exercise to heart health; discuss sleep. Avoid labelling foods 'good' or 'bad' — focus on balance.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it more accurate to talk about a balanced diet than 'good' and 'bad' foods?" → All foods provide something; health depends on the overall balance and quantity over time.
- **Differentiation:** support: sort foods into nutrient groups with pictures / stretch: analyse a real food label and explain what it tells a shopper.
- **Materials:** balanced-plate poster, food labels, meal photo cards from several cultures.
- **Joy:** design a balanced 'world menu' for a day.

### Lesson 16 — Drugs, medicines and the body
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how some drugs, including alcohol, tobacco and caffeine, affect the body, and explain how medicines are used safely." (UK NC Y6 animals including humans)
- **Hook:** "Is every drug harmful? Is every medicine safe?"
- **Key activity:** Card sort: medicines (helpful when used correctly), legal drugs (caffeine, alcohol, nicotine) and their effects on heart and lungs. Discuss safe medicine rules (only from a trusted adult, follow the label). Link to vaping as a current concern.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why should medicines only be taken as prescribed or directed on the label?" → The right dose helps; too much, or the wrong medicine, can harm the body.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards with simple effect statements / stretch: evaluate a health poster about smoking for accuracy and persuasiveness.
- **Materials:** card sort, empty medicine packaging (safe), health posters. **Parent note:** share the lesson content with families beforehand; handle sensitively, as some pupils' family members may smoke, vape or drink. Facts only — no scare stories — and the message 'if you are ever offered something, you can say no and tell a trusted adult' (links to PE & Health).
- **Joy:** design a health campaign poster aimed at younger children.

### Lesson 17 — Charles Drew and blood donation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how scientific work on blood saves lives today." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically — scientists)
- **Hook:** "One donation of blood can help save more than one life. How?"
- **Key activity:** Learn about Charles Drew (African American doctor who developed large-scale blood banking in the 1940s) and blood donation today; how blood is separated into parts (plasma, red cells). Discuss why donors are needed worldwide.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why was being able to store blood (not just transfuse it directly from person to person) such an important breakthrough?" → Stored blood can be taken to where it's needed, such as hospitals and emergencies, saving many more lives.
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture sequence of the blood donation process / stretch: research how blood types were discovered (Karl Landsteiner, 1901) and why they matter.
- **Materials:** information texts, images of blood donation, a model 'blood' (coloured water, oil and beads to show components).
- **Joy:** make a model blood sample in a jar showing its parts.

### Lesson 18 — Unit 2 mastery check
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show my understanding of the circulatory system and a healthy lifestyle." (UK NC Y6 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "You are a doctor explaining to a patient how their heart works."
- **Key activity:** Mastery task: label the circulatory system, explain the pulse investigation results and give lifestyle advice with scientific reasons; role-play doctor–patient explanations.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain the journey of an oxygen particle from the air to a muscle in your leg." → Breathed into lungs, passes into blood, carried to the heart, pumped through arteries to capillaries in the leg muscle.
- **Differentiation:** support: a diagram with key words / stretch: explain what happens to carbon dioxide made by the muscle.
- **Materials:** mastery task, diagrams, doctor role-play props.
- **Joy:** doctor role-play with (toy) stethoscopes.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil traces blood through the heart, lungs and body, explains pulse-rate data from their own investigation using a graph, and gives three pieces of lifestyle advice with scientific reasons.

## Unit 3 — Evolution and inheritance

**Essential question:** How do living things change over very long periods of time, and how do we know?

**Key vocabulary:** fossil, extinct, evolution, adaptation, variation, inherit, inheritance, offspring, characteristic, natural selection, survival, environment, species, gene (introduced), selective breeding

### Lesson 19 — Fossils: stories in stone
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how fossils form and what they tell us about living things long ago." (UK NC Y6 evolution and inheritance)
- **Hook:** Pass round real fossils (ammonites, shark teeth, plant fossils). "This was alive millions of years ago."
- **Key activity:** Model fossil formation: press a shell into clay, fill the mould with plaster of Paris to make a cast. Sequence the stages (death, burial in sediment, minerals replace remains, uplift and erosion, discovery).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are fossils of soft-bodied animals like jellyfish very rare?" → Soft parts rot quickly before they can be buried and replaced by minerals; hard parts like shells and bones last longer.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture sequence cards to order / stretch: explain what fossils in rock layers tell us about the order in which organisms lived.
- **Materials:** real fossils, modelling clay, shells, plaster of Paris, water, cups.
- **Joy:** make your own fossil cast.

### Lesson 20 — Mary Anning, fossil hunter
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain Mary Anning's contribution to science and how she was treated." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically — scientists)
- **Hook:** "In 1811–12, Mary Anning and her brother found the first ichthyosaur skeleton to be recognised by scientists. She was about 12. Why don't more people know her name?"
- **Key activity:** Read about Anning's discoveries at Lyme Regis (ichthyosaur, plesiosaur) and why, as a working-class woman, she was often not credited. Discuss fairness in science.
- **Check for understanding:** "How did Anning's discoveries help change ideas about the history of life on Earth?" → They showed animals very different from any alive today had existed and become extinct.
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture biography with key facts / stretch: compare how Anning was treated with another scientist whose work was overlooked.
- **Materials:** information texts, fossil pictures, a picture book biography.
- **Joy:** "Palaeontologist's notebook" — draw a fossil like a scientist, with labels and scale.

### Lesson 21 — Variation and inheritance
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that offspring inherit characteristics from their parents but are not identical to them." (UK NC Y6 evolution and inheritance)
- **Hook:** "Why do puppies from the same litter look different?"
- **Key activity:** Class survey of inherited characteristics that are easy to observe (e.g. ear-lobe shape, tongue-rolling — noting that some traits are more complex than simple inheritance). Separate inherited features from those affected by environment (a scar, a haircut, a skill).
- **Check for understanding:** "Is speaking French an inherited characteristic? Explain." → No: languages are learned from the environment, not passed on in genes.
- **Differentiation:** support: sort cards into inherited / environmental / both / stretch: explain why identical twins can still differ (environmental effects).
- **Materials:** trait survey sheets, mirrors, picture cards of animal families.
- **Joy:** "Alien family" — design alien parents and draw their offspring with a mix of features.

### Lesson 22 — Gregor Mendel and pea plants
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how Mendel's experiments showed that characteristics are passed on in patterns." (UK NC Y6 evolution and inheritance — enrichment)
- **Hook:** "A monk grew thousands of pea plants in a monastery garden in the 1850s–60s. What did he discover?"
- **Key activity:** Simulate Mendel's cross with coloured beads in bags (each 'parent' gives one bead; tall is dominant). Record outcomes; discover that a hidden characteristic can reappear in the next generation.
- **Check for understanding:** "How can two tall plants have a short offspring?" → Each parent can carry a hidden 'short' factor; if the offspring inherits two, it is short.
- **Differentiation:** support: model with two colours and a results table / stretch: predict the ratio of tall to short in the second generation (about 3 : 1) and compare with class results.
- **Materials:** coloured beads, paper bags, results tables, pea pods (real).
- **Joy:** the bead-bag 'breeding' game.

### Lesson 23 — Adaptation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how animals and plants are adapted to their environments." (UK NC Y6 evolution and inheritance)
- **Hook:** "Why does a polar bear have black skin under its white fur?"
- **Key activity:** Investigate adaptations: "blubber glove" test (hand in a glove of lard vs empty glove in ice water — time how long it's comfortable) for insulation. Study adaptations from different biomes (camel, cactus, Arctic fox, mangrove).
- **Check for understanding:** "How does the blubber investigation help explain how Arctic animals survive?" → The fat layer insulated the hand, keeping it warm longer; blubber keeps heat in the animal's body.
- **Differentiation:** support: match animals to habitats with adaptation cards / stretch: design an animal adapted to a newly invented environment and justify each feature.
- **Materials:** lard, gloves, zip bags, ice water, stopwatch, adaptation cards. Check for food allergies; use vegetable shortening if needed.
- **Joy:** the blubber glove challenge.

### Lesson 24 — Darwin, Wallace and natural selection
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain natural selection using the finches of the Galápagos and a simulation." (UK NC Y6 evolution and inheritance)
- **Hook:** "Darwin noticed that finches on different Galápagos islands had different beaks. Why?"
- **Key activity:** "Beak" simulation: pupils use different tools (tweezers, spoons, pegs, chopsticks) to pick up 'food' (seeds, rice, marbles, elastic bands). Tools that suit the food 'survive' and 'reproduce'. Discuss variation, competition, survival, inheritance over many generations. Credit Alfred Russel Wallace, who reached the same idea independently.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain, in three steps, how a population of finches could change over many generations." → There is variation in beaks; birds with beaks best suited to the available food survive and breed more; they pass on those beak features, so over time more of the population has them.
- **Differentiation:** support: a three-step frame with picture cues / stretch: explain why natural selection needs very long periods of time.
- **Materials:** tweezers, spoons, clothes pegs, chopsticks, a range of 'foods', cups (stomachs), timers, data tables.
- **Joy:** the beak simulation game.

### Lesson 25 — Evidence for evolution
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe different kinds of evidence for evolution." (UK NC Y6 evolution and inheritance)
- **Hook:** "Your arm, a bat's wing and a whale's flipper have the same bones. Coincidence?"
- **Key activity:** Compare limb bone diagrams (human, bat, whale, cat); explore fossil sequences (e.g. horse ancestors, and *Archaeopteryx* as a link between dinosaurs and birds); discuss how evidence from fossils, anatomy and today's DNA studies fit together.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do scientists think bats and whales share a common ancestor with humans?" → Their limbs have the same basic bone structure, adapted for different uses, suggesting descent from a shared ancestor.
- **Differentiation:** support: colour-code matching bones on diagrams / stretch: explain what a 'transitional fossil' is, using *Archaeopteryx*.
- **Materials:** limb bone diagrams, coloured pencils, fossil images.
- **Joy:** "Bone detectives" — identify mystery limbs.

### Lesson 26 — Selective breeding: humans as the selectors
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare selective breeding by humans with natural selection." (UK NC Y6 evolution and inheritance)
- **Hook:** "Chihuahuas and Great Danes are the same species. How?"
- **Key activity:** Explore dog breeds, wheat, maize (from teosinte in Mexico), and cabbage relatives (broccoli, cauliflower, kale — all from wild cabbage). Compare with natural selection in a Venn diagram.

- **Differentiation:** support: Venn diagram with statements to sort / stretch: discuss a benefit and a problem of selective breeding (e.g. breathing problems in some dog breeds).

## Unit 4 — Light and how we see

**Essential question:** How does light travel, and how does it let us see the world?

**Key vocabulary:** light source, reflect, reflection, mirror, angle of incidence, angle of reflection, straight line, ray, shadow, opaque, translucent, transparent, refraction, spectrum, prism, periscope, retina, pupil, lens

### Lesson 29 — Light travels in straight lines
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show that light travels in straight lines and use this to explain shadows." (UK NC Y6 light)
- **Hook:** "Can you see a torch beam around a corner?"
- **Key activity:** Line up three cards with holes; light passes through only when holes are aligned. Explore shadows with a torch and objects; draw ray diagrams with rulers.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a shadow have the same outline as the object that makes it?" → Light travels in straight lines, so the opaque object blocks rays and leaves a dark area of the same shape.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided ray-diagram template / stretch: explain why a shadow gets bigger as the object moves closer to the light source.
- **Materials:** torches, card with holes, modelling clay stands, rulers, opaque objects.
- **Joy:** shadow puppet show.

### Lesson 30 — How we see
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that we see objects because light travels from a source, reflects off the object and enters our eyes." (UK NC Y6 light)
- **Hook:** "Do our eyes send out rays to see things? Many ancient thinkers thought so."
- **Key activity:** Ibn al-Haytham (Cairo, around 1011–1021) showed light enters the eye. Pupils draw ray diagrams from source → object → eye with arrows in the right direction; explore a dark box with an object inside (you can't see it until light gets in).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can't you see a book in a completely dark room even with your eyes open?" → No light is reflecting off the book into your eyes.
- **Differentiation:** support: arrow-direction cards to place on a diagram / stretch: explain what a camera obscura shows about light and the eye.
- **Materials:** shoebox 'dark boxes', torches, ray diagram sheets, eye model.
- **Joy:** build a pinhole camera obscura from a box and tracing paper.

### Lesson 31 — Reflection and mirrors
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can investigate how light reflects from a mirror and measure the angles of incidence and reflection." (UK NC Y6 light / working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Can you hit a target on the wall with a torch beam — only using a mirror?"
- **Key activity:** Use ray boxes (or torches with slit card) and mirrors on paper; draw the normal; measure angles with a protractor; discover that angle of incidence = angle of reflection.
- **Check for understanding:** "If a ray hits a mirror at 40° to the normal, at what angle does it reflect? Why?" → 40°: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-drawn normals and angle lines / stretch: predict and test a path using two mirrors to hit a target.
- **Materials:** ray boxes or slit torches, plane mirrors, protractors, paper, rulers.
- **Joy:** "Laser maze" — use mirrors to guide a beam around obstacles to a target (torch only; never lasers aimed at eyes).

### Lesson 32 — Periscopes
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can design and build a periscope and explain how it works using reflection." (UK NC Y6 light / design)
- **Hook:** "Submarines see above the water without surfacing. How?"
- **Key activity:** Build a periscope from card (or juice cartons) and two small mirrors at 45°; test; draw a ray diagram of the light's path.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must the mirrors in a periscope be at 45°?" → So light is turned through 90° at each mirror, travelling down the tube and into the eye.
- **Differentiation:** support: a pre-marked net to cut and fold / stretch: design a periscope that sees around a corner sideways, and explain the change.
- **Materials:** card or cartons, small safety mirrors, scissors, tape, protractors.
- **Joy:** periscope spy games around the classroom.

### Lesson 33 — Measuring light through materials
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can measure how much light passes through materials with a sensor and use the data to go beyond 'transparent, translucent, opaque'." (UK NC Y6 light / working scientifically — builds on Year 4's sorting by eye)
- **Hook:** "Which material would make the best curtains for a bedroom that faces the sunrise?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of Year 4's three categories, then the new step: a fair test using a torch and a light sensor (data logger or phone app) at a fixed distance; measure the light (lux) through each material, rank them on a continuous scale and discover that 'translucent' covers a huge range.
- **Check for understanding:** "Tissue and greaseproof paper are both 'translucent'. Why is the sensor reading more useful than the label when choosing a curtain — and what must stay the same for the comparison to be fair?" → The reading shows how much light each lets through, so you can compare two 'translucent' materials; torch, distance, sensor position and angle must stay the same so only the material affects the reading.
- **Differentiation:** support: classify by eye before measuring / stretch: test layers of the same material and graph the pattern.
- **Materials:** torches, light sensors or data loggers, material samples (cling film, tissue, card, fabric, foil, bubble wrap).
- **Joy:** "Curtain designer" — choose and justify a curtain fabric from data.

### Lesson 34 — Refraction: bending light
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe refraction and explain everyday effects such as a 'bent' straw in water." (UK NC Y6 light — extension / Cambridge Stage 6)
- **Hook:** Put a pencil in a glass of water. "Is the pencil broken?"
- **Key activity:** Explore refraction: bent straw, the disappearing coin (coin in a cup appears when water is added), a glass of water as a lens. Explain that light changes direction when it passes between materials such as air and water.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a coin at the bottom of a cup become visible when water is poured in (with your eye in the same place)?" → Light from the coin bends as it leaves the water, so it now reaches your eye.
- **Differentiation:** support: stations with picture instruction cards / stretch: explain why a swimming pool looks shallower than it is.
- **Materials:** clear cups, water, straws, pencils, coins, a magnifying glass.
- **Joy:** the "magic coin" trick performed for another class.

### Lesson 35 — Colour and the spectrum
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that white light is made of a spectrum of colours, as Isaac Newton showed with a prism." (UK NC Y6 light — extension)
- **Hook:** "Where does a rainbow come from?"
- **Key activity:** Split light with prisms (or a CD, or a glass of water in sunlight); make a Newton colour wheel that looks whitish when spun fast. Discuss that coloured objects reflect some colours and absorb others.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a red apple look red in white light?" → It reflects red light into our eyes and absorbs the other colours.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided colour-wheel making / stretch: predict how a red apple would look under blue light, test with filters and explain.
- **Materials:** prisms, torches, card discs, pencils, coloured filters, a sunny window.
- **Joy:** spinning Newton colour wheels.

### Lesson 36 — The eye
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can name parts of the eye and explain their roles in seeing." (UK NC Y6 light / animals including humans)
- **Hook:** "Look at a partner's eyes, then shine a torch near (not into) them. What happens to the pupil?"
- **Key activity:** Observe pupils changing size in bright and dim light; label an eye diagram (cornea, iris, pupil, lens, retina, optic nerve); link to how a pinhole camera forms an upside-down image.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does your pupil get smaller in bright light?" → To let in less light and protect the retina.
- **Differentiation:** support: labelled eye model with word bank / stretch: explain how glasses help someone who is short-sighted (lenses bend light to focus it on the retina).
- **Materials:** eye model, diagrams, torches (not shone into eyes), pinhole cameras from Lesson 30.
- **Joy:** "Optical illusion gallery" — explore how the brain interprets what the eyes see.

### Lesson 37 — Unit 4 mastery check
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show my understanding of light, reflection, shadows and seeing." (UK NC Y6 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "The case of the invisible thief — solve it using the science of light."
- **Key activity:** Mastery task: draw ray diagrams for seeing, reflection and shadows; explain periscope and refraction examples; interpret data from the transparency test.
- **Check for understanding:** "Draw and explain how you can see a cat in a sunny garden." → Light travels from the Sun in straight lines, reflects off the cat and enters your eye — with arrows in that direction.
- **Differentiation:** support: partly drawn diagrams / stretch: explain how a mirror image differs from the real object (left–right reversed).
- **Materials:** mastery task, rulers, protractors, mirrors.
- **Joy:** solving the 'thief' mystery using a light clue.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil draws correct ray diagrams showing how we see objects and how mirrors reflect, explains how a periscope works, and describes a fair test on light and materials.

## Unit 5 — Electricity and circuits

**Essential question:** How can we control electricity to make things work — safely?

**Key vocabulary:** circuit, series circuit, cell, battery, voltage, volt, current, component, bulb, buzzer, motor, switch, conductor, insulator, circuit symbol, circuit diagram, brightness, resistance (introduced), short circuit

### Lesson 38 — Circuits revisited
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can build complete circuits and explain why a circuit must be a complete loop." (UK NC Y6 electricity)
- **Hook:** "One bulb, one cell, one wire. Can you light it?"
- **Key activity:** Circuit challenges with increasing difficulty; troubleshoot circuits that don't work (loose connections, gaps, flat cells). Safety: never use mains electricity; batteries only.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a bulb go out when you open a switch?" → The switch breaks the loop, so current can't flow round the circuit.
- **Differentiation:** support: step-by-step picture cards / stretch: build a circuit where one switch controls two bulbs.
- **Materials:** cells (1.5 V) in holders, bulbs in holders, wires with crocodile clips, switches, buzzers, motors.
- **Joy:** "Circuit doctor" — diagnose and fix broken circuits.

### Lesson 39 — Circuit symbols and diagrams
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use recognised symbols to draw circuit diagrams and build circuits from diagrams." (UK NC Y6 electricity)
- **Hook:** "An electrician in Japan and one in Brazil can read the same diagram. How?"
- **Key activity:** Learn symbols (cell, battery, bulb, switch open/closed, buzzer, motor, wire). Draw diagrams of circuits built; then build circuits from given diagrams. Note: symbols are internationally agreed.
- **Check for understanding:** "This diagram shows a battery of two cells, a bulb and an open switch. Will the bulb light? Redraw it so that it will, and explain why the battery symbol has two parts." → No — the open switch breaks the loop; redraw with the switch closed. A battery is two or more cells joined together, so it is drawn as several cell symbols.
- **Differentiation:** support: symbol cards to match to components / stretch: draw a diagram for a circuit with two bulbs and a switch that controls only one bulb (requires a parallel branch — explain).
- **Materials:** circuit kits, symbol cards, rulers, squared paper.
- **Joy:** "Diagram relay" — one partner draws, the other builds, then swap.

### Lesson 40 — Investigation: voltage and brightness
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can investigate how the number of cells affects the brightness of a bulb and explain the pattern." (UK NC Y6 electricity / working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "What happens if we add more cells? Predict!"
- **Key activity:** Fair test: add 1, 2, 3 cells (within the bulb's rating) and measure brightness with a light sensor or rate it on a scale; keep the same bulb and wires. Record and graph.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does adding cells make the bulb brighter?" → More cells give a higher voltage, which pushes more current through the bulb.
- **Differentiation:** support: a results table and brightness scale card / stretch: explain what happens if the voltage is too high for the bulb (it may blow) and why bulbs have a voltage rating.
- **Materials:** cells, bulbs of known voltage rating, light sensors or brightness scale, wires. Safety: never exceed the bulb's rating by more than a small amount; no rechargeable-cell short circuits.
- **Joy:** "Brightness bet" — predictions before each test.

### Lesson 41 — More components: what changes?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can investigate how adding bulbs in series affects brightness and explain why." (UK NC Y6 electricity)
- **Hook:** "Two bulbs in a row with one cell. Will they be brighter, dimmer or the same?"
- **Key activity:** Build series circuits with 1, 2 and 3 bulbs and one cell; observe; explain using the idea that the cell's 'push' is shared among more components (resistance increases).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are three bulbs in series dimmer than one bulb with the same cell?" → The same voltage is shared among more bulbs, and more components mean more resistance, so less current flows.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided observation sheet / stretch: design a way to make three bulbs as bright as one (add cells) and test it.
- **Materials:** circuit kits, extra bulbs, cells.
- **Joy:** "Brightness puzzle cards" to solve.

### Lesson 42 — Conductors, insulators and resistance
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can investigate how the length of a conductor affects the brightness of a bulb and explain it using the idea of resistance." (UK NC Y6 electricity / working scientifically — builds on Year 4's conductor/insulator sorting)
- **Hook:** "Is a pencil 'lead' a conductor? (It's actually graphite.) And does it matter how long the path is?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval: use a tester circuit to re-sort five materials (Year 4). Then the new idea: slide a crocodile clip along a pencil 'lead' (graphite) or a 30 cm length of thin nichrome resistance wire (short tests only — it can get warm), measuring brightness at 2 cm steps with a light sensor or brightness scale; graph and explain — a longer path means more resistance, so less current.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does the bulb get dimmer as the graphite path gets longer — and why are household wires made of copper rather than graphite?" → A longer path has more resistance, so less current flows; copper has very low resistance, so it wastes little energy, and its plastic coating insulates to keep people safe.
- **Differentiation:** support: prediction and results table with pictures, and three lengths only / stretch: predict and test what a thicker graphite line (drawn heavily on paper) does to brightness, and explain.
- **Materials:** circuit kits, test materials, a propelling-pencil graphite rod (2B or softer) or thick 6B pencil lines on paper, rulers, light sensor or brightness scale.
- **Joy:** "Draw a dimmer switch" — a graphite line drawn heavily on paper becomes a home-made dimmer as the clip slides along it.

### Lesson 43 — Electricity pioneers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe contributions of scientists and inventors to our use of electricity." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically — scientists)
- **Hook:** "The unit 'volt' is named after a person. Who?"
- **Key activity:** Research stations: Alessandro Volta (Italy — the first battery, the voltaic pile, around 1800); Michael Faraday (England — electromagnetic induction); Lewis Latimer (USA — improved carbon filaments for light bulbs); build a simple 'lemon battery' or coin-and-vinegar battery that lights an LED.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did Volta's pile show about making electricity from chemicals?" → Stacking two different metals separated by a salty or acidic material produces a steady electric current.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided research cards / stretch: explain why several lemon cells must be joined in series to light an LED.
- **Materials:** lemons, zinc-coated (galvanised) nails, copper coins or strips, wires, LEDs, research cards.
- **Joy:** the lemon battery.

### Lesson 44 — Design: a burglar alarm
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can design, build and test a circuit that solves a problem, using a pressure switch." (UK NC Y6 electricity / design & technology)
- **Hook:** "Someone keeps taking biscuits from the tin. Catch them!"
- **Key activity:** Design a pressure switch from card, foil and sponge; build a circuit with a buzzer or bulb that turns on when stepped on or when a lid opens; test and improve; draw the circuit diagram.

### Lesson 45 — Electrical safety and saving energy

- **Key activity:** Mastery task: draw circuit diagrams, predict brightness changes, explain conductors and insulators; practical challenge to wire a model room.

## Unit 6 — Chemistry: particles, properties and reactions

**Essential question:** How can we tell when a new substance has been made, and what happens to the particles?

**Key vocabulary:** particle, solid, liquid, gas, state, dissolve, solution, property, identify, chemical reaction, new substance, reactant, product, reversible, irreversible, rate (speed of reaction), casein, conservation of mass

### Lesson 47 — The particle model
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use the particle model to describe solids, liquids and gases." (Cambridge Primary Stage 6 chemistry / NGSS 5-PS1-1)
- **Hook:** "Why can you walk through air but not through a wall?"
- **Key activity:** Pupils act as particles: solid (packed, vibrating in place), liquid (close, sliding past), gas (spread out, moving fast). Draw particle diagrams; explain properties (shape, flow, compression — squash a syringe of air vs water).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can gases be squashed but liquids hardly at all?" → Gas particles are far apart with space between them; liquid particles are already touching.
- **Differentiation:** support: particle diagram templates / stretch: use the model to explain why a balloon shrinks in the freezer.
- **Materials:** plastic syringes (no needles), water, playground space, particle diagram sheets.
- **Joy:** "Human particles" drama in the hall.

### Lesson 48 — Dissolving: where does the sugar go?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain dissolving using the particle model and show that mass is conserved." (NGSS 5-PS1-2 / UK NC Y5 materials review)
- **Hook:** "Weigh water, weigh sugar, dissolve. Will the total mass change?"
- **Key activity:** Measure the mass of water and sugar separately, dissolve, and weigh again (the total is the same). Explain with particles: sugar particles spread between water particles.
- **Check for understanding:** "The sugar has 'disappeared'. Why is the mass the same?" → The sugar particles are still there, spread out among the water particles; nothing has been lost.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided table for masses / stretch: design a fair test for how temperature affects dissolving speed.
- **Materials:** digital scales, beakers, sugar, water, spoons.
- **Joy:** "Magic-mass" reveal.

### Lesson 49 — Mystery powders: identifying substances by their properties
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify unknown substances by testing and comparing their properties, without tasting." (NGSS 5-PS1-3 / Cambridge Primary Stage 6 chemistry)
- **Hook:** "Five jars of white powder have lost their labels: flour, cornflour, sugar, salt and bicarbonate of soda. How can a chemist tell them apart without tasting?"
- **Key activity:** Teach first: why we never taste in science, and how a property (look, feel, dissolving in water, fizzing with vinegar, turning blue-black with iodine — iodine dropped by the adult) acts as a 'fingerprint'. We do: test one known powder together and record. You do: groups test all five known powders, build a results table, then use it to identify two unlabelled 'mystery' powders.
- **Check for understanding:** "Powder A fizzes with vinegar; powder B turns blue-black with iodine and doesn't dissolve. Which is bicarbonate and which is cornflour — and why is testing better than just looking?" → A is bicarbonate (fizzing shows a gas is made), B is cornflour (iodine shows starch); all five powders look almost the same, so only tests of properties tell them apart reliably.
- **Differentiation:** support: three powders only, with a picture results table / stretch: design a flow chart (key) that identifies any of the five powders in the fewest tests, and justify its order.
- **Materials:** flour, cornflour, sugar, salt, bicarbonate of soda in labelled pots plus two unlabelled mystery pots, water, vinegar, dilute iodine solution (adult-held dropper), spotting tiles, hand lenses, goggles.
- **Joy:** "Crime-lab chemists" — identify the powder 'spilled at the scene'.

### Lesson 50 — Signs of a chemical reaction
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify evidence that a chemical reaction has made a new substance." (NGSS 5-PS1-4 / UK NC Y5 changes review)
- **Hook:** "Vinegar + bicarbonate of soda in a bottle, balloon on top. What will happen?"
- **Key activity:** Carousel of changes: vinegar and bicarbonate (gas), burning a candle (teacher demonstration), baking a cake (compare raw and baked), milk and vinegar (a solid forms), ice melting and salt dissolving (as 'no new substance' comparisons). Record signs of a new substance: gas, colour change, temperature change, new solid, light. Retrieve Year 4's reversible/irreversible idea and connect it to 'new substance made'.
- **Check for understanding:** "Ice melting and bicarbonate fizzing both 'change'. Which one made a new substance, and what is your evidence?" → The fizzing: a gas (carbon dioxide) was made that wasn't there before and the change can't easily be reversed; melting ice is still water, just in a different state.
- **Differentiation:** support: a signs-of-reaction checklist with pictures / stretch: find a change that shows a sign (e.g. bubbles when water boils) but is NOT a chemical reaction, and explain why one sign alone isn't proof.
- **Materials:** vinegar, bicarbonate, balloons, bottles, milk, ice, salt, candle (teacher only), goggles.
- **Joy:** balloon inflation race.

### Lesson 51 — Making new materials: milk plastic
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make a new material through a chemical reaction and explain why it cannot be turned back into what it was made from." (NGSS 5-PS1-4 / Cambridge Primary Stage 6 chemistry)
- **Hook:** "Before modern plastics, buttons and jewellery were made from milk. How?"
- **Key activity:** Adult warms milk (not boiling); pupils stir in vinegar, watch curds form, strain through a cloth, press out the liquid and mould the solid (casein) into shapes to dry for two days. Discuss other useful reactions that make new materials around the world: baking bread, making paneer and cheese, plaster setting, concrete hardening.
- **Check for understanding:** "Could you get the milk and vinegar back from your milk plastic? Explain using the idea of a new substance." → No: the milk protein reacted with the acid to form a new solid (casein plastic) with different properties; it is an irreversible chemical change.
- **Differentiation:** support: step-by-step picture method card and a partner / stretch: plan a fair test to find which amount of vinegar makes the most plastic from 100 ml of milk, naming the variables.
- **Materials:** milk, white vinegar, jug, spoons, sieve and cloth, kitchen paper, cookie cutters or moulds, food colouring (optional), hot plate or microwave (adult only), goggles.
- **Joy:** keep your own milk-plastic charm or button.

### Lesson 52 — Investigation: what makes a reaction go faster? (planning)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a fair test into how temperature affects the speed of a reaction, identifying the variables and how to measure." (Cambridge Primary Stage 6 / UK NC Y6 working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Drop a fizzy vitamin tablet into cold water and another into warm water. Which finishes first — and can we measure it properly?"
- **Key activity:** Watch the demonstration, then plan together: changed variable (water temperature), measured variable (time until fizzing stops), controlled variables (tablet size, water volume, container, no stirring). Groups choose four or five temperatures, draw a results table with room for three repeats and predict with a reason using particles.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must every tablet be the same size, and why should the water not be stirred?" → Both would change how fast the reaction happens, so we couldn't tell whether temperature caused the difference.
- **Differentiation:** support: a planning sheet with the variables started / stretch: add a second question (does crushing the tablet change the time?) and explain why it must be a separate test.
- **Materials:** effervescent vitamin C or antacid tablets (adult-supervised; not to be swallowed), clear cups, measuring jug, thermometers, stopwatches, planning sheets. Hot water from the tap only (no kettle-hot water).
- **Joy:** "Fizz forecast" — pupils bet on the finishing order.

### Lesson 53 — Investigation: what makes a reaction go faster? (testing and graphing)
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can carry out my plan with repeat readings, calculate means and draw a line graph of temperature against reaction time." (Cambridge Primary Stage 6 / UK NC Y6 working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Our predictions are on the board. Time to find out."
- **Key activity:** Groups run the test, three repeats per temperature; calculate means; spot any anomaly and repeat it; plot a line graph (temperature on the x-axis); describe the pattern and explain it with particles moving faster and meeting more often when warmer.
- **Check for understanding:** "Use your graph to predict the time at a temperature you didn't test. How confident are you, and why?" → A value read from the line between two tested points, with confidence based on how close the repeats were.
- **Differentiation:** support: axes pre-drawn and scaled / stretch: explain why the graph is a curve rather than a straight line, and suggest how to make the timing more accurate.
- **Materials:** as Lesson 52, plus graph paper, rulers, calculators.
- **Joy:** "Fastest fizz" — the class reveals the finishing order against the forecasts.

### Lesson 54 — Conservation of mass
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show that mass is conserved in a chemical reaction in a closed container." (NGSS 5-PS1-2)
- **Hook:** "When the balloon inflated, did the reaction make new 'stuff' out of nothing?"
- **Key activity:** Weigh vinegar and bicarbonate in a sealed bottle with a balloon before and after the reaction; the mass stays the same. Repeat open (gas escapes, mass drops). Explain with particles rearranging.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does the mass drop when the reaction happens in an open container?" → The gas escapes into the air, so it isn't weighed — but its mass still exists.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided recording with before/after columns / stretch: explain what 'atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed' means.
- **Materials:** digital scales, bottles, balloons, vinegar, bicarbonate, goggles.
- **Joy:** the sealed vs open "mass mystery".

### Lesson 55 — Unit 6 mastery check
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show my understanding of particles, properties, chemical reactions and conservation of mass." (Cambridge Stage 6 / NGSS 5-PS1 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "The milk-plastic buttons are dry. What do they prove?"
- **Key activity:** Examine the dried milk plastic; mastery task: particle diagrams, identify a mystery powder from given test results, sort changes into 'new substance' or 'no new substance' with evidence, explain an open-vs-closed mass result, and interpret a reaction-speed graph.
- **Check for understanding:** "A pupil says: 'When wood burns, the mass goes down, so mass is destroyed.' Is she right?" → No: the wood reacts with oxygen and makes gases (carbon dioxide and water vapour) and smoke that escape into the air; if everything were trapped and weighed, the total mass would be the same.
- **Differentiation:** support: conclusion frames and a word bank / stretch: explain why cooking an egg is a chemical change but melting butter is not, using the particle model.
- **Materials:** dried milk plastic, mastery task, reaction-speed graphs.
- **Joy:** "Chemistry show-and-tell" — each pupil demonstrates one change to a partner and explains it.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil uses the particle model to explain dissolving, identifies an unknown substance from its properties, gives evidence that a chemical reaction has made a new substance, explains a reaction-speed result from a graph, and explains why mass is conserved in a closed container.

## Unit 7 — Earth's atmosphere and climate

**Essential question:** How does the atmosphere keep Earth warm — and what is changing?

**Key vocabulary:** atmosphere, layer, oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, greenhouse effect, greenhouse gas, fossil fuel, carbon cycle, photosynthesis, respiration, weather, climate, average, evidence, data, renewable, sustainability

### Lesson 56 — The atmosphere: our protective blanket
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the composition of the atmosphere and why it matters for life." (NGSS 5-ESS2-1)
- **Hook:** "If Earth were an apple, the atmosphere would be thinner than the skin."
- **Key activity:** Model the atmosphere's composition with 100 beads (about 78 nitrogen, 21 oxygen, 1 other gases including a tiny amount of carbon dioxide). Discuss what the atmosphere does: air to breathe, weather, protection from some solar radiation, keeping Earth warm.
- **Check for understanding:** "Carbon dioxide is only a tiny fraction of the air. Why can it still matter so much?" → Small amounts can have big effects because carbon dioxide traps heat; changes to it change how warm Earth is.
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled bead model and pie chart / stretch: draw the pie chart accurately with a protractor (maths link).
- **Materials:** 100 coloured beads, pie-chart templates, apple, globe.
- **Joy:** "Apple skin" demonstration.

### Lesson 57 — The carbon cycle
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how carbon moves between living things, the air, the oceans and the ground." (NGSS 5-LS2-1, 5-ESS2-1)
- **Hook:** "The carbon in your body may once have been part of a dinosaur. How?"
- **Key activity:** Carbon cycle role-play: pupils as carbon atoms roll dice at stations (air, plant, animal, ocean, fossil fuel, soil) that send them to the next place, recording their journey. Link photosynthesis (plants take in CO₂) and respiration (living things give out CO₂).
- **Check for understanding:** "A forest is cut down and burned. Use the carbon cycle to explain two ways this changes the amount of carbon dioxide in the air." → Burning releases the carbon stored in the wood as carbon dioxide, and with fewer trees less carbon dioxide is taken in by photosynthesis — so the amount in the air rises.
- **Differentiation:** support: a simplified four-station cycle / stretch: explain how burning fossil fuels moves carbon from the ground to the air much faster than natural processes.
- **Materials:** station signs, dice, journey recording sheets.
- **Joy:** the carbon-journey dice game.

### Lesson 58 — Modelling the greenhouse effect
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can model the greenhouse effect with a fair test and explain what the model shows and doesn't show." (NGSS 5-ESS3-1 / working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Why is a car hot inside on a sunny day?"
- **Key activity:** Two identical jars with thermometers under a lamp (or sunshine): one covered with cling film (or containing extra CO₂ from vinegar and bicarbonate). Record temperatures every 2 minutes; graph. Discuss the model's limits (a jar is not the atmosphere).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is one way our jar model is like the real greenhouse effect, and one way it isn't?" → Like: something traps heat so the inside warms; unlike: the real atmosphere doesn't have a solid lid — gases absorb and re-emit heat.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-drawn results table and axes / stretch: identify variables to control and suggest how to improve the model.
- **Materials:** two jars, two thermometers, cling film, lamp, timer, graph paper.
- **Joy:** watching the temperature race.

### Lesson 59 — Eunice Foote and Svante Arrhenius
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how scientists discovered that carbon dioxide affects temperature." (NGSS nature of science)
- **Hook:** "In 1856, an American scientist showed that a jar of carbon dioxide heated up more in the sun than a jar of ordinary air. Her name was almost forgotten."
- **Key activity:** Learn about Eunice Foote (1856), John Tyndall (1859) and Svante Arrhenius (1896, first estimates of warming from CO₂). Compare Foote's experiment with our Lesson 58 model; discuss why she was overlooked.
- **Check for understanding:** "How was Eunice Foote's experiment similar to ours, and what did she conclude?" → She compared jars with different gases in sunlight; she concluded carbon dioxide would make the air warmer.
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture timeline / stretch: discuss why scientific ideas take time to be accepted.
- **Materials:** timeline cards, information texts.
- **Joy:** "Science time machine" — interview Eunice Foote (teacher in role).

### Lesson 60 — Weather or climate? Reading the evidence
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can distinguish weather from climate and interpret data showing long-term temperature change." (NGSS 5-ESS2 / working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "It snowed last week. So how can the world be warming?"
- **Key activity:** Define weather (day to day) and climate (average over 30+ years). Interpret a global average temperature graph (from NASA or a national meteorological service) and other evidence (sea-level rise, shrinking glaciers, earlier springs).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does one cold week not disprove global warming?" → Weather varies day to day; climate is the long-term average, which the data shows is rising.
- **Differentiation:** support: a simplified graph with guiding questions / stretch: calculate the change in average temperature between two decades on the graph.
- **Materials:** printed graphs from reputable sources, glacier before-and-after photographs, rulers.
- **Joy:** "Glacier then and now" photo comparison.

### Lesson 61 — Effects around the world
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how climate change affects different places and living things." (NGSS 5-ESS3-1 / sustainability)
- **Hook:** "How could a change of a degree or two matter to a coral reef, a Pacific island or a farmer in the Sahel?"
- **Key activity:** Jigsaw groups research case studies (coral bleaching, sea-level rise in low-lying islands, drought and farming, Arctic sea ice); present to the class.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are some countries affected more than others even though they produce less carbon dioxide?" → Their geography (low-lying, dry, hot) makes them more vulnerable; the effects are global, not only where CO₂ is emitted.
- **Differentiation:** support: case study cards with key facts / stretch: link one case study to evolution and adaptation (can species adapt fast enough?).
- **Materials:** case-study packs, world map.
- **Joy:** "Expert panel" presentations.

### Lesson 62 — Solutions: science and action
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate solutions to climate change, including renewable energy and restoring forests." (NGSS 5-ESS3-1 / sustainability)
- **Hook:** "Wangari Maathai's Green Belt Movement in Kenya has planted tens of millions of trees. Why trees?"
- **Key activity:** Evaluate solutions (renewable energy, efficient buildings, trees and forests, less waste, changes in transport); build and test a simple solar oven or wind turbine model; plan a school action.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain, using the carbon cycle, why planting trees can help." → Trees take in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis and store carbon in their wood.
- **Differentiation:** support: a pros-and-cons table / stretch: compare two solutions using data (cost, carbon saved) and recommend one.
- **Materials:** pizza-box solar oven kit (foil, cling film, black paper), wind turbine kit (optional), information on Wangari Maathai.
- **Joy:** melt chocolate in a solar oven.

### Lesson 63 — Unit 7 mastery check
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the greenhouse effect and the evidence for climate change." (NGSS 5-ESS — assessment)
- **Hook:** "A younger pupil asks: 'Is climate change real, and what can I do?' How will you answer?"
- **Key activity:** Mastery task: explain the greenhouse effect with a diagram, interpret a data graph, describe the carbon cycle, and write a scientifically accurate answer for a younger pupil.
- **Check for understanding:** "What evidence would you show to someone who doubts that Earth is warming?" → Long-term temperature records, melting glaciers, rising sea levels — data over decades, not single events.
- **Differentiation:** support: diagram template and word bank / stretch: explain the difference between evidence and opinion in climate debates.
- **Materials:** mastery task, graphs, diagram templates.
- **Joy:** "Climate explainers" filmed for younger classes (with permission).

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil explains the greenhouse effect with a labelled diagram, interprets a long-term temperature graph, and explains one solution using the carbon cycle.

## Unit 8 — Science fair: independent investigation

**Essential question:** Can I ask a question and answer it scientifically, on my own?

**Key vocabulary:** question, hypothesis, prediction, independent variable, dependent variable, control variable, method, reliability, accuracy, repeat, mean, conclusion, evaluation, poster, abstract

### Lesson 64 — Asking a testable question
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a testable scientific question and a prediction with a reason." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "'Why is the sky blue?' vs 'Does the colour of a cup affect how fast water in it warms in sunlight?' Which can we test in school?"
- **Key activity:** Sort questions into testable/not testable; pupils brainstorm, choose and refine their own question (individually or in pairs), identifying variables.
- **Check for understanding:** "What makes a question 'testable'?" → You can change one variable and measure the effect on another, with the equipment available.
- **Differentiation:** support: a menu of adaptable questions / stretch: choose a question needing a control group.
- **Materials:** question cards, variables planning sheet, past science-fair examples.
- **Joy:** "Question market" — pupils browse and trade question ideas.

### Lesson 65 — Planning the method
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a safe method that controls variables and includes repeat measurements." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Could someone else follow your method and get the same results?"
- **Key activity:** Write a numbered method, equipment list, risk assessment, and results table; partner tests the method for clarity.
- **Check for understanding:** "How many times will you repeat your measurements, and why?" → At least three, to check reliability and calculate a mean.
- **Differentiation:** support: a method template with sentence starters / stretch: explain how you'll check for anomalous results.
- **Materials:** planning templates, risk-assessment sheet.
- **Joy:** "Method swap" — can a partner follow your instructions exactly?

### Lesson 66 — Carrying out the investigation (1)
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can carry out my method carefully and record accurate results." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Scientists at work — lab coats on!"
- **Key activity:** Pupils conduct their investigations; teacher circulates checking safety, measurement technique and recording.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me a measurement you took. How did you make sure it was accurate?" → A description of technique (eye level, zeroing scales, same timing method).
- **Differentiation:** support: a partner or adult to help with measurement / stretch: record extra observations and uncertainties.
- **Materials:** equipment as planned, results tables, lab coats (old shirts).
- **Joy:** a real lab day.

### Lesson 67 — Carrying out the investigation (2) and analysing data
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can complete my measurements, calculate means and present data in a suitable graph." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Numbers don't speak until you graph them."
- **Key activity:** Finish repeats; calculate means; choose bar chart or line graph appropriately; identify patterns and anomalies.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did you choose a line graph or a bar chart for your data?" → Line graph for continuous data showing change; bar chart for categories.
- **Differentiation:** support: graph templates / stretch: use a spreadsheet to calculate means and draw the graph.
- **Materials:** graph paper, calculators, spreadsheet software.
- **Joy:** the "aha" moment when the pattern appears.

### Lesson 68 — Conclusions and evaluation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a conclusion supported by data and evaluate how reliable my investigation was." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically)
- **Hook:** "Was your prediction right? Does it matter if it wasn't?"
- **Key activity:** Write conclusion (answer the question, use data, explain with science) and evaluation (what went well, sources of error, improvements, next question).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a result that disproves your prediction still valuable?" → It teaches us something true; science advances by testing ideas, not by being right first time.
- **Differentiation:** support: conclusion and evaluation frames / stretch: suggest a follow-up investigation that tests your explanation.
- **Materials:** results, conclusion frames.
- **Joy:** "Surprise of the day" — share the most unexpected result.

### Lesson 69 — Preparing the science fair poster
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present my investigation clearly on a poster for an audience." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically — reporting)
- **Hook:** "A visitor has 60 seconds at your poster. What do they need to see?"
- **Key activity:** Poster sections: question, prediction, method (with photos), results (graph), conclusion, evaluation; practise a 1-minute explanation.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the one key finding a visitor should remember from your poster?" → A single clear sentence linked to the data.
- **Differentiation:** support: a poster template / stretch: write a 50-word abstract summarising the investigation.
- **Materials:** poster card, printouts, glue, pens.
- **Joy:** poster design time.

### Lesson 70 — Science fair
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain my investigation to visitors and answer their questions." (UK NC Y6 working scientifically — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Welcome to the Year 5 Science Fair!"
- **Key activity:** Pupils present to visitors (families, other classes); visitors use feedback cards; the teacher assesses working-scientifically skills.
- **Check for understanding:** "A visitor asks: 'How do you know your test was fair?' What's your answer?" → Named control variables and repeats.
- **Differentiation:** support: cue cards / stretch: act as a peer judge using the success criteria.
- **Materials:** posters, investigation equipment for demonstrations, feedback cards.
- **Joy:** the science fair itself, with certificates for curiosity, method and communication.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil independently plans, carries out and presents an investigation with a testable question, controlled variables, repeat readings and means, an appropriate graph, a conclusion and an evaluation.

## Teacher guidance

**Common misconceptions to watch for**
- *"We see because light comes from our eyes."* Always draw arrows from source to object to eye.
- *"Blood in veins is blue."* Deoxygenated blood is dark red; veins look bluish through the skin.
- *"Animals evolve because they need to / try to."* Individuals don't change on purpose; populations change over generations through variation and selection.
- *"Humans evolved from chimpanzees."* Humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor.
- *"Dissolved substances disappear."* Weighing shows the mass remains.
- *"Burning destroys mass."* The products are gases that escape; in a closed system mass is conserved.
- *"Batteries store current."* Cells provide a voltage (a push); current flows only in a complete circuit.
- *"The greenhouse effect is bad."* It keeps Earth habitable; the problem is its enhancement by extra greenhouse gases.
- *"Weather and climate are the same."* Climate is a long-term average.

**How to teach it well**
- Let pupils do the science: every unit includes at least one full investigation where pupils make real decisions about variables.
- Insist on repeats and means (maths link) and on graphs with labelled axes and units.
- Name scientists from many backgrounds and discuss how ideas change with evidence.
- Safety: goggles for all chemistry; teacher-only for heat and flames; no mains electricity; allergy checks for food; hygiene for any animal tissue; minibeasts returned unharmed.
- Handle sensitive topics (health, drugs, family inheritance, climate anxiety) with care, focusing on agency and hope.

**Vertical connections**
- *From Year 4:* life cycles, the solar system and forces, properties and changes of materials (including conductors, sorting by transparency and the rusting investigation — built on here, not repeated), fair testing.
- *Into Year 6:* cells and microscopes build on classification and microorganisms; body systems build on circulation; ecosystems and food webs build on adaptation and the carbon cycle; chemical reactions, elements, compounds, acids, alkalis and word equations build on signs of reaction, identifying substances and conservation of mass; energy transfers build on circuits and the greenhouse effect; waves build on light.
- *Across subjects:* maths (means, line graphs, pie charts, protractors for angles of reflection), geography (climate, biomes), English (explanation texts, research), computing (data loggers, spreadsheets), life skills (health, sustainability).
