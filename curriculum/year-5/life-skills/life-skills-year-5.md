## Year overview

Life skills in Year 5 helps pupils become **self-aware, safe, resilient, financially sensible and
actively caring citizens** as friendships, independence and online life all grow more complicated. Year 4
laid the foundations (identity maps, strengths, growth mindset, conflict steps and "I" statements, SMART
goals, earning, simple interest, a class market stall, a waste audit, rights and stereotypes). Year 5
does **not** repeat these; it applies them to harder situations. Six units cover the four strands:

- **Social–emotional learning and character:** identity across groups and the influences on it; values
  under pressure; a richer vocabulary for mixed emotions; changing friendships, group chats, dares and
  risk; bullying and being an upstander; disagreeing well; personal safety and independence; performance
  nerves, problem-solving, change and loss; helping a friend who is struggling; and leadership.
- **Money sense:** bank accounts and ways to pay, scams, value for money, tax and public services, careers,
  and planning a real class event on a budget (maths link: decimals and percentages).
- **Sustainability:** food waste, climate fairness and the science of changing habits, leading to a
  pupil-led **behaviour-change campaign** measured with before-and-after data.
- **Global citizenship:** rules, laws and justice; from stereotype to prejudice to discrimination;
  inclusion and disability; democracy in action; and using your voice with decision-makers.

Lessons are discussion-rich and practical: role-plays, scenarios, circle time, real prices, real data and
a real campaign. Pupils keep a reflective journal. (Puberty, body image, sleep and mental-health basics are
taught in **PE & Health Unit 4**; basic first aid in PE & Health; advertising and sponsored content in
**Computing Lesson 32**; global trade in **Geography Unit 4** (fair trade itself is Year 3 and Year 6 geography) — this subject does not repeat them.)

**What this year assumes (from Year 4):** an identity map and named character strengths; growth-mindset
language and the brain growing with practice; conflict-resolution steps, "I" statements and assertive
refusals; apologies and forgiveness; SMART goals and learning strategies; needs vs wants, a simple budget,
simple interest and the cost of borrowing; running a market stall and calculating profit; the SDGs and a
school waste audit; key children's rights with responsibilities; stereotypes and inclusion; and a class
service project.

**What Year 6 relies on:** a secure vocabulary for emotions and strategies for stress; strategies for
changing friendships, online group life, pressure and risk; knowing how to keep safe and get help; the
upstander toolkit; bank accounts, scams, unit pricing and tax; experience of leading a group campaign with
data; the chain from stereotype to discrimination; and experience of democracy and writing to a
decision-maker — ready for a values "compass", emotional intelligence, compound interest and credit,
payslips, rights in tension, and the self-directed Change-Maker capstone.

## Unit 1 — Who I am becoming: identity, values and influence

**Essential question:** Who and what shapes the person I am becoming — and how do I stay true to my values?

**Key vocabulary:** identity, group, belonging, code-switching, value, dilemma, integrity, influence, influencer, algorithm, emotion, mixed emotions, envy, jealousy, embarrassment, newcomer

### Lesson 1 — Many groups, one me
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the different groups I belong to shape my identity, and how I can adapt to a group without giving up who I am." (SEL — self-awareness)
- **Hook:** "Do you talk the same way to your grandparent, your best friend and your teacher? Are you a different person each time?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of the Year 4 identity map. New: a 'groups' Venn diagram (family, friends, clubs, languages, faith or culture, online groups); discuss code-switching (changing how we speak or act to fit a group) as normal and often useful; sort examples of adapting that is fine (using a different language at home) from 'losing yourself' (joining in with unkindness to fit in).
- **Check for understanding:** "Mo laughs at a joke about a classmate so his new group will like him. Is that the same as speaking differently at home and at school? Explain." → No: adapting how you speak doesn't go against your values; joining in with unkindness does, so he is giving up part of who he is to fit in.
- **Differentiation:** support: a Venn template with picture prompts and sorted example cards / stretch: explain how belonging to a group can make people treat 'outsiders' unfairly, and how to avoid it.
- **Materials:** Venn templates, example cards, reflective journals.
- **Joy:** "One me" collage — the groups you belong to, joined by the values that stay the same.

### Lesson 2 — Values under pressure: dilemmas
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can reason through a dilemma where two of my values conflict and explain my choice." (SEL — responsible decision-making)
- **Hook:** "Your best friend copied in a test and the teacher asks you if anyone cheated. Loyalty or honesty?"
- **Key activity:** Values card sort to choose your top three (retrieval from Year 4); then work through dilemmas using a three-question test — Who is affected? What would happen if everyone did this? Could I explain this choice to someone I respect? — in pairs, then as a class 'conscience alley'.
- **Check for understanding:** "Pick one dilemma. Which two values pull against each other, what do you choose, and what makes it the better choice?" → Two named values, a choice, and a reason that considers the people affected (not just 'because it's right').
- **Differentiation:** support: fewer dilemmas with the two values already named / stretch: argue the opposite choice as convincingly as you can, then say what would change your mind.
- **Materials:** values cards, dilemma cards, journals.
- **Joy:** "Conscience alley" drama.

### Lesson 3 — Who influences me?
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can map the people and things that influence my choices and decide which influences to trust." (SEL — self-awareness / media literacy)
- **Hook:** "Who chose the last video you watched — you, or an algorithm?"
- **Key activity:** An 'influence map' (family, friends, teachers, role models, celebrities and influencers, adverts, apps that recommend what to watch next); rate each by how much it influences you and how much it has your interests at heart; discuss how recommendation algorithms keep us watching (link to Computing Lesson 32); choose one positive influence to spend more time with.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might an app that recommends videos not have your best interests at heart, even if you enjoy it?" → It is designed to keep you watching (often to show adverts), so it recommends what holds attention, not what is good for you.
- **Differentiation:** support: an influence map with categories provided / stretch: compare an influence that helps you grow with one that just takes your time, using evidence.
- **Materials:** influence-map templates, journals.
- **Joy:** "Influence audit" — a secret ballot on the class's biggest influences.

### Lesson 4 — Feelings with more than one name
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can name mixed and complex emotions precisely and explain why naming a feeling accurately helps." (SEL — self-awareness)
- **Hook:** "Your best friend wins the prize you wanted. What do you feel? Just one thing?"
- **Key activity:** Build an emotion vocabulary beyond happy, sad, angry and scared (proud, relieved, frustrated, disappointed, embarrassed, guilty, envious, jealous, anxious, excited, overwhelmed); mixed emotions (nervous and excited at once); envy (wanting what someone has) vs jealousy (fearing losing someone to another); intensity scales; match feelings to scenarios from class texts.
- **Check for understanding:** "A refugee child who has just reached safety in a new country might feel both 'relieved' and 'homesick'. Explain how both can be true at once." → Mixed emotions are normal: they can be relieved to be safe and also miss home, family and friends — the feelings are about different things.
- **Differentiation:** support: an emotions word wheel with faces / stretch: explain why naming an emotion precisely makes it easier to choose a helpful response.
- **Materials:** emotion word wheels, scenario cards, journals.
- **Joy:** "Feelings charades" with tricky emotions.

### Lesson 5 — Belonging: welcoming newcomers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why belonging matters and plan specific ways to help others feel they belong." (SEL — social awareness)
- **Hook:** "Think of a time you were the new one. What did someone do that helped?"
- **Key activity:** Circle time on belonging; read a short extract about a newcomer (link to *The Arrival* in English); list what newcomers find hardest (language, routines, friendship groups, names mispronounced); create a class 'belonging charter' with specific actions.
- **Check for understanding:** "A new pupil speaks little of the school's language. Suggest two actions that would help, and explain why each would work." → Two concrete actions with reasons (e.g. learning to say their name correctly shows respect; a buddy who shows routines reduces confusion and loneliness).
- **Differentiation:** support: action cards to choose from / stretch: plan a welcome pack for new pupils, including one in their home language.
- **Materials:** story extract, charter paper, journals.
- **Joy:** signing the class belonging charter.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil explains how groups and influences shape identity without giving up their values, reasons through a values dilemma, names mixed emotions precisely, and plans concrete ways to help a newcomer belong.

## Unit 2 — Friendship, safety and speaking up

**Essential question:** How do I handle changing friendships, pressure and risk — online and offline — and when do I speak up?

**Key vocabulary:** friendship group, left out, group chat, screenshot, minimum age, dare, risk, consequence, peer pressure, bullying, bystander, upstander, disagree, evidence, personal safety, trusted adult, secret, surprise, report

### Lesson 6 — When friendships change
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why friendships change as we grow up and choose kind ways to handle drifting apart or being left out." (SEL — relationships)
- **Hook:** "Your best friend since Year 1 now spends every break with someone else. What's going on — and what can you do?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of Year 4's healthy and unhealthy friendship signs. New: why friendships shift at this age (new interests, clubs, moving classes); 'threes' and friendship triangles; scenarios — being left out, wanting to spend time with new friends without hurting old ones; strategies (talk honestly, widen your circle, don't force it, get help if it becomes unkind).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between a friend drifting away and a friend deliberately leaving you out, and should you respond differently?" → Drifting is a natural change of interests, best handled by talking and widening your circle; deliberately excluding is unkind, and if it is repeated it may be bullying and you should get help.
- **Differentiation:** support: scenario cards with suggested responses to choose from / stretch: write advice from both sides of a friendship that is changing.
- **Materials:** scenario cards, journals.
- **Joy:** "Friendship forecast" — kind advice for tricky friendship weather.

### Lesson 7 — Group chats and online friendships
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how misunderstandings and exclusion happen in group chats and use strategies to keep online friendships kind and safe." (SEL — relationships / online safety)
- **Hook:** A group chat message: "ok." — "Is this person angry, bored, or fine? How can you tell?"
- **Key activity:** Why tone is lost in text (no face, voice or body language); how screenshots mean nothing is truly private; exclusion by 'secret' second groups; most social media apps set a minimum age of 13 and why; strategies — assume the best and ask, take heated conversations offline, never forward hurtful content, know how to leave, mute and report, and tell a trusted adult.
- **Check for understanding:** "Someone posts an embarrassing photo of a classmate in the group chat and everyone reacts with laughing faces. What are two things you could do, and why does 'just one laughing face' matter?" → e.g. don't react or share, message the classmate privately, tell the poster to delete it or tell a trusted adult; every reaction spreads the hurt and tells the poster it is acceptable.
- **Differentiation:** support: a 'think before you send' checklist / stretch: write a set of group-chat rules for the class that everyone could agree to.
- **Materials:** printed mock chat conversations (no real pupils), checklists. Parent note: families are informed about this lesson and the minimum-age rules of common apps.
- **Joy:** "Emoji translator" — how many meanings can one message have?

### Lesson 8 — Dares, risk and pressure
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can weigh up a risk (how likely, how bad, is it worth it?) and use exit strategies when I'm pressured to do something unsafe." (SEL — responsible decision-making)
- **Hook:** "It's 'just a dare'. Everyone's watching. What's going through your head?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4's assertive refusals. New: a risk-weighing tool (What could go wrong? How likely? How bad? Can it be undone? Is it worth it?); why groups take bigger risks than individuals; online 'challenges' that spread quickly (discussed without naming or showing specific ones); exit lines that save face ("My mum tracks my phone", "I've got training", changing the subject, leaving with a friend); agreeing a code word with family to get picked up.
- **Check for understanding:** "Use the risk questions on this dare: climbing onto the garage roof to get a ball. What's your decision and why?" → Considers likelihood and severity (a fall could cause serious injury, which cannot be undone) and decides not to — suggests a safe alternative (ask an adult to get it).
- **Differentiation:** support: a risk-scale card (low, medium, high) with pictures / stretch: explain why the same dare feels harder to refuse in front of a group, and how having a friend who also says no helps.
- **Materials:** scenario cards, risk-weighing cards. Note: never show or describe dangerous online challenges in detail — focus on the decision-making.
- **Joy:** "Exit-line" role-play — the most creative safe way out.

### Lesson 9 — Bullying, bystanders and upstanders
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what bullying is (including online), how it differs from conflict, and how to be an upstander safely." (SEL — safety and relationships)
- **Hook:** "Is one argument bullying? What about one unkind message? What makes something bullying?"
- **Key activity:** Define bullying (repeated, intentional, with an imbalance of power) and distinguish it from conflict and one-off unkindness; roles (target, person bullying, bystander, upstander); safe upstander actions (don't join in, support the target privately, get others to help, report to an adult, save evidence online); the school's anti-bullying policy and reporting tools.
- **Check for understanding:** "Every day, two older pupils take a younger pupil's snack and laugh. Is this bullying? Give the reasons, then one safe thing a bystander could do." → Yes: it is repeated, deliberate and there is a power imbalance (older and two against one); safe action such as telling a staff member, staying with the younger pupil, or asking friends to come with you — not confronting alone if unsafe.
- **Differentiation:** support: a scenario sort (bullying / not bullying) with the three features as a checklist / stretch: design a campaign poster about being an upstander online.
- **Materials:** scenario cards, school anti-bullying policy (child-friendly version).
- **Joy:** "Upstander pledge" wall.

### Lesson 10 — Disagreeing well
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can disagree with someone's idea respectfully, using evidence, without attacking the person." (SEL — communication / oracy)
- **Hook:** "Should homework be banned? Take a side — now prove you understand the other side's best argument."
- **Key activity:** Sentence stems for disagreeing well ("I see why you think… but…", "What's your evidence for…?", "I've changed my mind because…"); attacking an idea vs attacking a person; a structured discussion (one topic, speaking in turn, summarise the previous speaker before replying); celebrate changing your mind when the evidence is good.
- **Check for understanding:** "Rewrite 'That's a stupid idea' as a respectful disagreement, and explain why yours is more likely to change the other person's mind." → e.g. "I see your point about free time, but I think homework helps us remember; here's why…" — it criticises the idea not the person, so they don't feel attacked and are more likely to listen.
- **Differentiation:** support: a sentence-stem card and a partner with the same view / stretch: summarise the opposing view so well that the other side agrees it's fair ('steelmanning').
- **Materials:** discussion stems, topic cards, talking tokens.
- **Joy:** "Mind-changer award" for the best-evidenced change of mind.

### Lesson 11 — Staying safe and getting help
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan how to stay safe as I become more independent (out and about and online), tell the difference between a safe and an unsafe secret, and know how to get help." (SEL — safety)
- **Hook:** "Next year some of you may walk to school alone or have your own phone. What do you need to know first?"
- **Key activity:** Independence scenarios (lost in town, phone battery dead, a stranger offers a lift, a new online 'friend' asks for photos or to meet); safe choices (plans agreed with family, staying in public places, asking a shop worker or police officer); surprises (end soon, make people happy) vs unsafe secrets (make you feel worried or uncomfortable, or someone says you'll be in trouble if you tell); never keep a secret that makes you feel unsafe; how to report online (the report button and a trusted adult); map five trusted adults.
- **Check for understanding:** "An online friend you've never met says 'Don't tell your parents about our chats — they wouldn't understand.' What should you do, and why is that request itself a warning sign?" → Stop replying, don't share anything, tell a trusted adult and report it; asking a child to keep a secret from their family is a warning sign that someone may mean harm.
- **Differentiation:** support: a 'safe or unsafe secret' sorting activity with pictures / stretch: write a safety guide for a younger sibling getting their first device.
- **Materials:** scenario cards, 'trusted adults' hand templates, reporting information. Parent note: families are informed in advance; follow safeguarding procedures for any disclosure.
- **Joy:** "Safety plan" cards to take home and agree with family.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil handles a changing-friendship scenario kindly, explains how to keep a group chat safe, weighs up a risky dare using the risk questions, identifies bullying and a safe upstander action, disagrees respectfully with evidence, and explains safe vs unsafe secrets and how to get help.

## Unit 3 — Resilience, change and helping each other

**Essential question:** How can I cope with pressure and change — and how can I help a friend who is struggling?

**Key vocabulary:** stress, pressure, nerves, fight-or-flight, performance, strategy, problem-solving, option, consequence, change, loss, grief, support network, help-seeking, confidential, role model, leadership

### Lesson 12 — Nerves and performance: making stress work for you
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a little stress can help performance but too much gets in the way, and use strategies to manage nerves before a test or performance." (SEL — self-management)
- **Hook:** "Your heart thumps before a race or a concert. Is that your body helping or hindering you?"
- **Key activity:** The body's alarm response (faster heart and breathing, 'butterflies') — useful for danger or a race; the 'stress curve' (too little stress → bored, some → focused, too much → overwhelmed); strategies before a performance (box breathing, preparing well, a routine, positive self-talk, reframing nerves as excitement); practise box breathing.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might a little nervousness before a spelling test actually help you, but a lot make you forget everything?" → Some stress makes you alert and focused; too much floods you with alarm signals so it's hard to think and remember.
- **Differentiation:** support: strategy picture cards / stretch: create a personal 'before-a-big-moment' plan for a real upcoming event.
- **Materials:** stress-curve diagram, strategy cards, calm music.
- **Joy:** a pupil-designed calm corner.

### Lesson 13 — Solving problems step by step
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a problem-solving method to break down a real personal problem and choose a sensible solution." (SEL — responsible decision-making)
- **Hook:** "You've got a big project due, football practice and a falling-out with a friend — all this week. Where do you even start?"
- **Key activity:** A five-step method (What exactly is the problem? What are my options? What might happen with each? Choose and try one. How did it go?); practise on scenario problems in pairs, then privately on one of your own; notice which problems you can control and which you can't (circle of control).
- **Check for understanding:** "For the busy-week problem, give two options and a likely consequence of each, then choose one and justify it." → Two realistic options with consequences and a reasoned choice (e.g. ask for a project extension early vs skip practice — asking early keeps a commitment and reduces stress).
- **Differentiation:** support: a problem-solving frame with options partly filled / stretch: sort a list of worries into 'can control' and 'can't control', with an action for the first and a coping strategy for the second.
- **Materials:** problem-solving frames, scenario cards, journals.
- **Joy:** "Problem-busters" agony-aunt column for fictional characters.

### Lesson 14 — Coping with change and loss
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the feelings that come with change and loss, ways to cope, and where to get support." (SEL — resilience)
- **Hook:** "Moving house, a new sibling, a friend leaving, someone dying. How does change feel?"
- **Key activity:** Types of change (link to *Skellig* and *A Long Walk to Water*); grief and loss are normal and don't follow a neat order; mixed feelings (from Lesson 4); coping strategies (talking, remembering, routines, being active); map a personal support network.
- **Check for understanding:** "A classmate's grandparent has died and they seem angry rather than sad. Why might that be, and what could you do?" → Grief can show as many feelings, including anger; be patient, include them, listen if they want to talk, and let an adult know if you are worried.
- **Differentiation:** support: a support-network template / stretch: write advice to a younger pupil facing a big change.
- **Materials:** support-network templates, story extracts. Handle sensitively: check pupils' circumstances beforehand (recent bereavements, family separation), let families know the lesson is coming, and allow any pupil to opt for a quieter task.
- **Joy:** "Circles of support" artwork.

### Lesson 15 — Asking for help and helping a friend
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why asking for help is a strength and how to support a friend who is struggling, including when I must tell an adult." (SEL — relationships / wellbeing)
- **Hook:** "Your friend has been quiet for weeks and says 'I'm fine'. What do you do?"
- **Key activity:** Why people don't ask for help (embarrassment, not wanting to be a burden) and why asking is brave; how to support a friend (notice, ask, listen without judging, don't try to fix everything); the limits of a friend's role — some worries are too big to keep, so you must tell a trusted adult even if your friend asks you not to; role-play a supportive conversation.
- **Check for understanding:** "A friend tells you someone at home is hurting them and makes you promise not to tell. What should you do, and is it breaking a promise?" → Tell a trusted adult straight away; keeping them safe matters more than the promise, and it is the right, caring thing to do.
- **Differentiation:** support: 'good things to say' phrase cards / stretch: write a short guide on 'how to be a good listener' for the class.
- **Materials:** phrase cards, scenario cards, help information (school staff, national children's helplines). Follow safeguarding procedures if a pupil discloses.
- **Joy:** "Kindness toolkit" — pupils build a class list of ways to check in on each other.

### Lesson 16 — Leadership and being a role model
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the qualities of good leaders and plan how I will lead and support younger pupils in Year 6." (SEL — leadership / transition)
- **Hook:** "Next year you'll be the oldest in school. Who will the younger ones copy — and what will they learn from you?"
- **Key activity:** Leaders who listen vs leaders who boss; qualities of leaders from history and the community; review the year's journal so far; plan a Year 6 leadership role (reading buddy, sports leader, eco-rep, peer mediator); write a letter to your future self.
- **Check for understanding:** "Describe one leadership quality you already show, with evidence, and one you will work on next year." → A named quality with a specific example and a realistic target.
- **Differentiation:** support: a leadership-qualities word bank / stretch: plan how you'll support a younger pupil who is finding school hard.
- **Materials:** journals, letter paper, envelopes.
- **Joy:** sealed letters to be opened in Year 6 Lesson 1.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil explains how some stress helps and too much hinders, applies a problem-solving method to a scenario, describes healthy ways to cope with change and loss, explains how to help a struggling friend and when to tell an adult, and names their support network.

## Unit 4 — Money in the real world

**Essential question:** How can I keep my money safe, spend it wisely and understand where money in society goes?

**Key vocabulary:** bank, current account, savings account, debit card, contactless, PIN, balance, statement, scam, phishing, value for money, unit price, discount, multi-buy, tax, public services, career, skill, budget, contingency

### Lesson 17 — Banks, accounts and ways to pay
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what banks do, the difference between a current and a savings account, and how cash, cards and phone payments work." (Money sense)
- **Hook:** "When you tap a card, no money moves in your hand. So where does it go?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4: banks keep money safe and pay interest because they lend it to others. New: current vs savings accounts; reading a simple bank statement (money in, money out, balance); debit cards, contactless and phone payments; why 'invisible' money can feel less real and is easier to overspend; keeping a spending record.
- **Check for understanding:** "Your statement starts at 40.00, then shows −12.50 (a game) and +15.00 (birthday money). What is the balance, and why is it worth checking statements?" → 42.50; to notice mistakes, forgotten spending or payments you didn't make (possible fraud).
- **Differentiation:** support: a statement with round numbers / stretch: explain why spending by card might feel different from spending cash, and one way to stay in control.
- **Materials:** mock bank statements, payment-method cards, calculators.
- **Joy:** "Follow the money" — act out a card payment from shop to bank and back.

### Lesson 18 — Scams and keeping money safe
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise common scams (including phishing messages) and explain rules that keep my money and accounts safe." (Money sense / online safety)
- **Hook:** A text: "You've won a free games console! Click here and enter your card details to pay postage." "What's wrong with this?"
- **Key activity:** Spot the signs of a scam (too good to be true, urgency, requests for passwords, PINs or card numbers, strange links or senders, spelling mistakes, pressure to keep it secret); 'free' game currency and account-sharing scams; rules: never share a PIN or password, don't click unexpected links, check with an adult, report it; what to do if you think you've been scammed (tell an adult quickly, contact the bank).
- **Check for understanding:** "A message that looks like it's from your games company says your account will be deleted in one hour unless you log in through its link. Name two warning signs and say what you should do." → Urgency and an unexpected link asking for a login; don't click — go to the real app or website yourself, or ask an adult.
- **Differentiation:** support: a scam-signs checklist with pictures / stretch: explain why scammers create urgency, and write a 'scam or safe?' quiz for another class.
- **Materials:** teacher-made example messages (no real links), checklists.
- **Joy:** "Scam spotters" game show.

### Lesson 19 — Value for money
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare prices using unit prices and percentage discounts to find the best value — and explain when the 'best deal' isn't the best choice." (Money sense / maths link: decimals and percentages)
- **Hook:** "Two boxes of the same cereal: 500 g for 2.40 or 750 g for 3.30. Which is better value?"
- **Key activity:** Unit pricing (price per 100 g); multi-buy offers ('3 for 2', 'buy one get one free'); percentage discounts (25% off 12.00); quality, durability and whether you'll really use it; compare real price labels or a shopping website.
- **Check for understanding:** "Work out which cereal box is better value, then explain when buying the bigger box might not be the wiser choice." → 500 g: 0.48 per 100 g; 750 g: 0.44 per 100 g, so the larger box is better value — but not if the extra goes stale and is thrown away or you don't have the money now.
- **Differentiation:** support: price per item with whole numbers / stretch: compare a 'cheap' item that lasts one year with a dearer one that lasts four.
- **Materials:** price labels or packaging, calculators, shopping scenarios.
- **Joy:** "Supermarket sweep" — best value on a budget.

### Lesson 20 — Taxes and public services
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what taxes are, what they pay for and why people disagree about how they should be spent." (Money sense / citizenship)
- **Hook:** "Who paid for the road outside, the local library and the streetlights?"
- **Key activity:** Types of tax simply (on income, on things we buy); public services they fund (schools, hospitals, roads, police, parks); a class simulation: as a town council, share out a fixed budget between services, then face a surprise (a flood, a new school needed); discuss trade-offs.
- **Check for understanding:** "Your council must cut 10% of its budget. Which service would you cut, which would you protect, and why?" → A choice that recognises a trade-off and gives a reason about who is affected.
- **Differentiation:** support: a budget of 10 tokens to share out / stretch: explain why some people want higher taxes and better services while others want lower taxes, giving a reason for each.
- **Materials:** budget tokens or spreadsheets, service cards, surprise-event cards.
- **Joy:** "Council chamber" debate.

### Lesson 21 — The world of work: careers and skills
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe a range of careers, the skills they need, and how stereotypes can limit people's career choices." (Money sense / careers)
- **Hook:** "Name a pilot, a nurse, an engineer and a nursery teacher. Did you picture a man or a woman for each?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4's earning and pay lesson briefly. New: job families (health, building, science, creative, caring, technology, business, public service); skills that transfer between jobs (communication, teamwork, problem-solving); challenge career stereotypes with real examples; jobs that didn't exist 20 years ago; a visitor or family interview about a job.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do skills like teamwork and problem-solving matter more and more, even for jobs that don't exist yet?" → Jobs change as technology changes; skills that transfer let you learn new jobs and work with others in any field.
- **Differentiation:** support: career picture cards with skills to match / stretch: research a job that didn't exist 20 years ago and the skills it needs.
- **Materials:** career cards, interview questions, visitor (optional).
- **Joy:** "Careers carousel" — a quick-fire question session with visitors or recorded interviews.

### Lesson 22 — Planning a class event on a budget
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a real class event within a fixed budget, comparing prices and keeping money aside for the unexpected." (Money sense / maths link)
- **Hook:** "We have 150 for our end-of-term celebration for 28 people. Can we make it brilliant without going over?"
- **Key activity:** Teams plan the event (food, activities, decorations) from real price lists; use unit pricing (Lesson 19); build a budget table in a spreadsheet; keep a 10% contingency; present plans; the class votes for the plan to carry out.
- **Check for understanding:** "Your plan costs 138. Is it within budget once you keep a 10% contingency? Show your working." → 10% of 150 = 15, leaving 135 to spend; 138 is 3 over, so something must be cut or swapped for better value.
- **Differentiation:** support: a partly completed budget table / stretch: plan two versions (basic and deluxe) and argue for one.
- **Materials:** price lists, spreadsheet templates, calculators.
- **Joy:** the winning plan is actually used for the end-of-term event.

### Lesson 23 — Money decisions: putting it together
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make and justify sensible money decisions in a month-long simulation, using what I've learned about accounts, scams, value and saving." (Money sense / review)
- **Hook:** "You get 20 pocket money a month and want a 60 skateboard. Let the month begin!"
- **Key activity:** A 'money month' simulation in pairs: weekly event cards (a scam message, a multi-buy offer, a friend's birthday, a chance to earn by helping a neighbour, a lost phone charger); record choices and balance in a table; reflect on how close you got to the savings goal and why.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which decision did the most to help or hurt your savings goal, and what would you do differently?" → A specific decision linked to its effect on the balance and a sensible change.
- **Differentiation:** support: fewer event cards and a simple recording table / stretch: work out how many months you'd need at your saving rate, and one way to reach the goal sooner.
- **Materials:** event cards, recording tables, calculators.
- **Joy:** "Money month" simulation.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil reads a simple bank statement, identifies scam warning signs, compares value using unit prices and discounts, explains what taxes pay for with a reasoned trade-off, and plans an event within budget with a contingency.

## Unit 5 — Sustainability: changing habits

**Essential question:** Why don't people change their habits even when they care about the planet — and how can we help them?

**Key vocabulary:** sustainability, food waste, emissions, climate change, climate justice, habit, behaviour change, nudge, social norm, campaign, baseline, target, impact, measure

### Lesson 24 — Food waste: from farm to bin
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain where food is wasted between farm and plate, and measure how much food our class wastes." (Sustainability / maths link)
- **Hook:** "UN estimates suggest that roughly a third of the world's food is lost or wasted. Where does it all go?"
- **Key activity:** Food's journey (farm, transport, shops, homes and schools) and where waste happens at each stage; why it matters (the land, water and energy used to grow it; methane from rotting food in landfill); weigh the class's lunch or snack waste for a week as a baseline (with hygiene precautions); link to SDG 12 (responsible consumption).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is throwing away an apple a waste of more than just an apple?" → All the water, land, energy and transport used to grow and deliver it are wasted too, and rotting food in landfill releases greenhouse gases.
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture sequence of the food journey / stretch: calculate the class's weekly waste per pupil and estimate a year's total.
- **Materials:** scales, gloves, food-waste tally sheets, SDG icons.
- **Joy:** "Waste detectives" weigh-in.

### Lesson 25 — Climate change: what's fair?
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why the people who contribute least to climate change are often hit hardest, and what that means for fairness." (Sustainability / global citizenship)
- **Hook:** "Two families: one takes several flights a year; one farms without electricity. A drought comes. Who suffers more?"
- **Key activity:** Recap from science and geography: burning fossil fuels adds greenhouse gases that warm the planet. New: compare average emissions per person in different countries (bar chart); who is most affected by floods, droughts and storms, and why (money for protection, dependence on farming); ideas of fairness — who should do most to fix it?; hopeful stories of action (e.g. communities planting mangroves or trees, youth campaigners); SDG 13.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do many people argue it's unfair that some of the poorest countries face the worst effects of climate change?" → They have produced a small share of emissions per person, yet they often have less money to protect themselves and depend more on farming and weather.
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture comparison of two families / stretch: debate who should pay to help countries adapt, giving a reason on each side.
- **Materials:** emissions-per-person chart, case-study cards, world map. Teacher note: keep the tone hopeful and action-focused to avoid climate anxiety.
- **Joy:** "Hope stories" — share an inspiring climate action.

### Lesson 26 — Why is changing habits so hard?
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why people often don't act on what they believe, and design a 'nudge' that makes a sustainable choice easier." (Sustainability / behaviour change)
- **Hook:** "Most people say they care about the planet. So why do lights stay on and bins overflow?"
- **Key activity:** Why habits stick (easy, automatic, what everyone else does); four ways to help change: make it **easy** (a recycling bin next to where waste is made), make it **normal** ("most pupils in Year 5 switch off screens"), make it **visible** (a food-waste chart), make it **timely** (a reminder at the moment of choice); spot nudges around school and in shops; design a nudge for one school habit.
- **Check for understanding:** "Design a nudge to reduce paper towels in the toilets, and explain which of the four ways it uses and why it might work." → A specific nudge (e.g. a sign by the dispenser: "Shake, then one towel is enough — most people do!") linked to 'normal' or 'timely' with a reason.
- **Differentiation:** support: example nudges to sort into the four ways / stretch: explain why information posters alone often fail to change behaviour.
- **Materials:** nudge example cards, school photos, design sheets.
- **Joy:** "Nudge lab" — prototype your nudge.

### Lesson 27 — Planning our campaign
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a group behaviour-change campaign with a clear target, a baseline measurement and a way to measure success." (Sustainability / active citizenship)
- **Hook:** "In Year 4 our class ran one project together. This time, your group chooses the problem — and must prove it made a difference."
- **Key activity:** Groups choose a target habit (food waste, lights and screens left on, litter, paper use, single-use plastics); collect or use baseline data (Lesson 24 for food waste); choose a nudge and a message; write the plan (aim, audience, actions, who, when, how we'll measure); pitch to the headteacher or school council.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is your baseline, what is your target, and how exactly will you measure whether you reached it?" → A number from real data, a realistic target (e.g. 20% less food waste) and a clear repeat measurement.
- **Differentiation:** support: a campaign-plan template with examples / stretch: plan how to rule out other explanations if the numbers change (e.g. a school trip day).
- **Materials:** campaign-plan templates, baseline data.
- **Joy:** pitching to school leadership.

### Lesson 28 — Running the campaign
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can carry out our planned campaign and use persuasion to encourage others to change a habit." (Sustainability / communication)
- **Hook:** "Campaign day!"
- **Key activity:** Groups launch their nudges and messages (posters, assembly slots, stickers by switches, a food-waste chart in the dining hall — link to English persuasive speeches and art); record what they did and how people responded.
- **Check for understanding:** "What persuasive technique or nudge did your group use, how did people respond, and what would you change?" → A named technique or nudge, an observation of the response and a sensible improvement.
- **Differentiation:** support: a defined role in the group / stretch: lead the campaign or present at assembly.
- **Materials:** campaign materials.
- **Joy:** visible change around school.

### Lesson 29 — Measuring impact
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate the impact of our campaign using before-and-after data and consider other explanations." (Sustainability / evaluation)
- **Hook:** "Did we change anything? Let's see the numbers."
- **Key activity:** Repeat the measurement; compare before and after (percentage change, chart); discuss other explanations (weather, a school event, people changing only while watched); plan how to make the change last; share results with the school.
- **Check for understanding:** "Food waste fell from 12 kg to 9 kg. What percentage decrease is that, and what else might explain the change?" → 3 ÷ 12 = 25% decrease; other explanations such as a trip day, a popular menu that week or people changing only while they knew they were being measured.
- **Differentiation:** support: a comparison chart template / stretch: write a short report to the school council with a recommendation.
- **Materials:** measurement sheets, charts.
- **Joy:** "Impact reveal" with a class celebration.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil explains why food waste matters and why climate change raises questions of fairness, designs a nudge using one of the four ways, and evaluates a campaign with before-and-after data and alternative explanations.

## Unit 6 — Fairness, justice and having a voice

**Essential question:** What makes rules and treatment fair, and how can I help make things fairer?

**Key vocabulary:** rule, law, justice, fair, consequence, restorative, stereotype, prejudice, discrimination, equality, inclusion, disability, social model, accessibility, democracy, vote, secret ballot, manifesto, representative, petition

### Lesson 30 — Rules, laws and justice
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why societies need laws, what makes a rule fair, and different ways of responding when rules are broken." (Global citizenship)
- **Hook:** "Your class is shipwrecked on an island. No adults, no rules. What happens by day three?"
- **Key activity:** Island scenario: groups write five rules and test them for fairness (Do they apply to everyone? Were people consulted? Are the consequences in proportion?); rules vs laws; responses when rules are broken — punishment vs 'restorative' justice (repairing the harm and the relationship); link to the class's own rules.
- **Check for understanding:** "A pupil breaks a younger child's model. Compare a punishment response with a restorative one — which is more likely to stop it happening again, and why?" → Punishment deals with the rule-breaking; restorative asks them to understand the harm and help repair it, which can change behaviour because they see the effect on the other person.
- **Differentiation:** support: a fairness checklist for rules / stretch: find a rule that is fair to most people but unfair to one group, and suggest how to fix it.
- **Materials:** island scenario cards, fairness checklist.
- **Joy:** "Island parliament".

### Lesson 31 — From stereotype to discrimination
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a stereotype can become prejudice and then discrimination, and how to respond safely to a prejudiced comment." (Global citizenship / SEL)
- **Hook:** "A shop has a sign: 'Only two school pupils at a time.' Is that fair? Is it a stereotype?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4: stereotypes and evidence against them. New: the chain — stereotype (a fixed idea about a group) → prejudice (a feeling or judgement about a person because of their group) → discrimination (treating them unfairly); examples in everyday life (being followed in shops, being picked last, names being mocked); many countries have equality laws protecting people from discrimination; responding to prejudiced comments (don't laugh, say calmly why it's not OK, support the person, tell an adult).
- **Check for understanding:** "Someone says 'People from that country are lazy' and later refuses to let a new pupil from there join the team. Label the stereotype, the prejudice and the discrimination." → Stereotype: the idea that 'people from that country are lazy'; prejudice: judging the new pupil because of it; discrimination: refusing to let them join the team.
- **Differentiation:** support: a three-step chain diagram with examples to sort / stretch: explain how stereotypes can affect people's own beliefs about what they can do.
- **Materials:** scenario cards, chain diagram. Handle racism and other prejudice directly and calmly; be alert to pupils' own experiences; follow the school's procedures for reporting prejudice-based incidents.
- **Joy:** "Break the chain" — role-play the upstander moment.

### Lesson 32 — Inclusion and disability
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how environments and attitudes can include or exclude disabled people, and recommend a realistic change for our school." (Global citizenship)
- **Hook:** "Is a wheelchair user disabled by their body — or by a staircase with no ramp?"
- **Key activity:** The social model of disability (people are disabled by barriers in the environment and attitudes, not only by an impairment); visible and invisible disabilities; accessibility audit of part of the school (steps, signs, noise, lighting, quiet spaces); learn about Paralympians and disabled leaders and campaigners; sign a greeting in the local sign language.
- **Check for understanding:** "Using the social model, explain why adding a ramp or a visual timetable is about removing a barrier, not 'fixing' a person." → The problem is the barrier (stairs, unclear information); removing it lets everyone take part.
- **Differentiation:** support: an accessibility checklist with pictures / stretch: write recommendations to the school (used in Lesson 34).
- **Materials:** accessibility checklists, Paralympian and campaigner profiles, sign-language video.
- **Joy:** learning to sign a greeting.

### Lesson 33 — Democracy in action
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the key features of democracy and take part in a fair class election." (Global citizenship / history link)
- **Hook:** "Who decides the rules in our school — and how could you have a say?"
- **Key activity:** Link to the campaign for women's votes (history Unit 6); features of democracy (free and fair elections, secret ballot, representation, free speech, rule of law); hold a class election for a real role (e.g. school council representative) with manifestos, hustings and a secret ballot; count and announce the result.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does it matter that votes are secret, and what would be unfair if they weren't?" → People can vote freely without pressure, bribery or fear of what others will think or do; open voting lets people be bullied or bought.
- **Differentiation:** support: a manifesto template / stretch: compare two voting systems (first past the post and ranked choice) with a class example and explain which is fairer.
- **Materials:** ballot papers, ballot box, manifesto templates.
- **Joy:** election day.

### Lesson 34 — Using my voice: writing to a decision-maker
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a persuasive, evidence-based letter to a real decision-maker about an issue I care about." (Global citizenship / English link)
- **Hook:** "Your campaign data and accessibility audit are evidence. Who has the power to act on them?"
- **Key activity:** Identify who decides (headteacher, school governors, local council, a company); features of an effective letter (polite, specific request, evidence, a personal reason, a realistic ask); draft and redraft; send the best letters (with the school's permission) — issues from the campaign (Unit 5), accessibility (Lesson 32) or the class's own choice.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'Please add a bench by the gate; 18 pupils waited over 15 minutes there last week' more likely to succeed than 'Our school is rubbish, fix it'?" → It is polite, asks for one specific realistic thing and gives evidence, so the decision-maker knows exactly what to do and why.
- **Differentiation:** support: a letter frame with sentence starters / stretch: write to two different decision-makers about the same issue, adjusting tone and evidence for each.
- **Materials:** letter frames, campaign and audit data, envelopes.
- **Joy:** posting the letters — and celebrating any replies.

### Lesson 35 — Looking back, looking forward
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can reflect on how I've grown this year across all four strands and set one intention for my final primary year." (SEL / review)
- **Hook:** "Open your journal at September. Would that pupil recognise you now?"
- **Key activity:** Journal review; a gallery of the year (campaign results, event budget, letters, charters); pairs interview each other about their biggest change; set one intention for Year 6 (linked to the leadership role planned in Lesson 16); class celebration.
- **Check for understanding:** "Name one skill from this year you've used outside the classroom, with a specific example." → A named skill (e.g. an exit line, spotting a scam, disagreeing well) with a real example.
- **Differentiation:** support: reflection prompts / stretch: write a short article for the school newsletter about the year's campaign.
- **Materials:** journals, displays.
- **Joy:** year-end celebration.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil explains what makes rules fair and compares punishment with restorative justice, labels stereotype, prejudice and discrimination in a scenario, applies the social model of disability, explains the features of democracy, and writes an evidence-based letter to a decision-maker.

## Teacher guidance

**Common misconceptions to watch for**
- *"Fitting in means changing who I am."* Adapting how you speak is normal; giving up your values is not.
- *"You can only feel one emotion at a time."* Mixed feelings are normal, especially at times of change.
- *"Any unkindness is bullying."* Distinguish conflict, one-off unkindness and bullying — all matter, but responses differ.
- *"A good friend always keeps secrets."* Never keep a secret that means someone is unsafe.
- *"Card money isn't real money."* Every tap comes out of a real balance.
- *"The biggest pack is always the best deal."* Only if you'll use it.
- *"If people know the facts, they'll change."* Making the good choice easy and normal usually works better than information alone.
- *"Treating everyone the same is always fair."* Sometimes removing a barrier for some people is what makes things fair.

**How to teach it well**
- Establish ground rules for discussion (listen, respect, right to pass, no names in examples).
- Use distancing techniques (stories, characters, scenarios) so pupils don't have to disclose personal experiences.
- Inform families before Lessons 7, 11, 14 and 15; follow safeguarding procedures if a pupil discloses something of concern.
- Make it real: real prices, a real event budget, a real campaign with real data, real letters.
- Link to the English texts (*Journey to Jo'burg*, *A Long Walk to Water*, *The Arrival*, *Skellig*) for empathy and discussion.

**Vertical connections**
- *From Year 4:* identity map and strengths, growth mindset, conflict steps and "I" statements, assertive refusals, SMART goals, earning, simple interest, borrowing, a market stall, a waste audit and class project, key rights and stereotypes. Year 5 retrieves these briefly and applies them to harder situations rather than re-teaching them.
- *Into Year 6:* a values compass and character strengths (VIA), WOOP goals and habits, emotional intelligence and thinking traps, mediation, consent and boundaries, compound interest and credit, payslips, enterprise with break-even, rights in tension, sustainable consumption, secondary transition and the Change-Maker capstone.
- *Across subjects:* maths (statements, unit prices, percentages, budgets), computing (spreadsheets, advertising and online safety), history (votes for women), geography (fair trade, water, climate), science (climate), PE & Health (puberty, sleep, mental health, first aid), English (persuasive speeches and letters).
