> Built to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Source material:

## Year overview

Year 5 geography connects **how the Earth works** with **how people live on it** — and asks pupils to
think about fairness and sustainability as global citizens. The year opens with **advanced map skills**
(six-figure grid references, contours, scale, map projections and digital mapping), which every later
unit uses. **The restless Earth** explains plate tectonics, volcanoes and earthquakes through case
studies in Iceland, Japan, Nepal, Chile and New Zealand, and asks why the same hazard harms some
places more than others. **Rivers and water** follows a river from source to sea and investigates
water scarcity (linked to the English class text *A Long Walk to Water*). **Trade and globalisation**
traces the journeys of everyday products (chocolate, a T-shirt, a phone) through Africa, Asia and the
Americas and weighs fair trade. **Cities and population** explores urbanisation and megacities such as
Lagos, Mumbai and Tokyo. The year ends with a **fieldwork enquiry** in the pupils' own area.

Across the year every inhabited continent appears in a case study, and Antarctica appears in map and
climate work. Each unit uses maps, data (maths link: graphs and percentages) and at least one enquiry
question that pupils answer with evidence.

**What this year assumes (from Year 4):** four-figure grid references, compass points, map symbols;
the water cycle; climate zones and biomes; a first fieldwork enquiry; knowledge of the continents and
oceans.

**What Year 6 relies on:** six-figure grid references and contour reading; the plate-tectonics model;
understanding of river processes; the idea of interdependence through trade; the vocabulary of
development and sustainability; and the full fieldwork cycle (question → collect → present → conclude
→ evaluate).

## Unit 1 — Maps, projections and GIS

**Essential question:** How can a flat map show a round, three-dimensional world — and what does every map leave out?

**Key vocabulary:** map projection, Mercator, equal-area, scale, grid reference, six-figure grid reference, easting, northing, contour line, relief, spot height, symbol, key, GIS, satellite image, latitude, longitude

### Lesson 1 — The world on a flat page
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why every flat world map distorts something, and compare two projections." (UK NC KS2 geography — maps)
- **Hook:** "On many classroom maps, Greenland looks about as big as Africa. Africa is actually about 14 times bigger. How is that possible?"
- **Key activity:** Peel an orange in one piece and try to flatten it. Compare the Mercator projection with an equal-area projection (e.g. Gall–Peters) and a globe; discuss what each is good for (Mercator for navigation; equal-area for comparing size).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the Mercator projection useful for sailors but misleading for comparing countries' sizes?" → It keeps directions and shapes locally accurate for navigation, but it stretches areas near the poles.
- **Differentiation:** support: compare three countries on a globe and a Mercator map / stretch: use an online "true size" comparison tool and explain one result.
- **Materials:** oranges, globe, Mercator and equal-area world maps, computers (optional).
- **Joy:** the orange-peel challenge.

### Lesson 2 — Latitude and longitude
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use latitude and longitude to locate places anywhere on Earth." (UK NC KS2 geography — locational knowledge)
- **Hook:** "A ship sends a distress call: 'We are at 64° N, 22° W.' Where do the rescuers go?" (Near Iceland.)
- **Key activity:** Revisit the Equator, Prime Meridian, tropics and polar circles; plot places with coordinates (maths link: four-quadrant coordinates); find the coordinates of pupils' family places.
- **Check for understanding:** "How are latitude and longitude like coordinates on a grid, and how are they different?" → Both use two numbers to pinpoint a place; latitude and longitude are measured in degrees on a sphere, north/south and east/west of 0°.
- **Differentiation:** support: a world map with lines labelled every 30° / stretch: explain why the Prime Meridian passes through Greenwich (an international agreement of 1884).
- **Materials:** world maps with latitude/longitude, globes, atlas.
- **Joy:** "Rescue mission" game using coordinates.

### Lesson 3 — Six-figure grid references
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can give and use six-figure grid references to locate features precisely." (UK NC KS2 geography — map skills)
- **Hook:** "A four-figure grid reference tells you the square. How do you pinpoint the church inside the square?"
- **Key activity:** Revisit four-figure references ('along the corridor, up the stairs'); divide a square into tenths to give six figures. Practise on a large-scale local topographic map.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a six-figure grid reference more useful than a four-figure one for finding a post box?" → It locates a point within about 100 m (on a 1 : 25,000 map), not just a 1 km square.
- **Differentiation:** support: a square pre-divided into tenths / stretch: give six-figure references for five features and set a route for a partner.
- **Materials:** local 1 : 25,000 or 1 : 50,000 maps, romer cards, rulers.
- **Joy:** "Treasure hunt" using six-figure grid references.

### Lesson 4 — Contours and relief
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read contour lines to describe the shape and steepness of land." (UK NC KS2 geography — map skills)
- **Hook:** "How can wavy lines on a map show a mountain?"
- **Key activity:** Build a clay 'mountain', slice it into layers of equal height (or draw lines at equal heights up the side), and trace each layer to make a contour map. Then read contours on a real map: close lines = steep; spot heights.
- **Check for understanding:** "On a map, the contour lines on one side of a hill are close together and on the other far apart. Which side is steeper, and why?" → The side with close lines — the height changes a lot over a short distance.
- **Differentiation:** support: match contour patterns to 3D pictures / stretch: sketch a cross-section of a hill from its contours (maths link: graphs).
- **Materials:** modelling clay, dental floss or wire (for slicing), tracing paper, topographic maps.
- **Joy:** make a contour model.

### Lesson 5 — Scale and distance
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a map scale to calculate real distances and plan a route." (UK NC KS2 geography / maths link to ratio)
- **Hook:** "How far is it to the nearest river as the crow flies — and by road?"
- **Key activity:** Use line and ratio scales (1 : 25,000 means 4 cm = 1 km); measure straight-line and curved distances with string; plan a walking route with timings.
- **Check for understanding:** "On a 1 : 50,000 map, a route measures 7 cm. How far is it in real life?" → 7 × 500 m = 3.5 km.
- **Differentiation:** support: a line scale to measure against / stretch: compare distances on maps with two different scales and explain which is better for walkers.
- **Materials:** local maps, rulers, string, calculators.
- **Joy:** "Route planner" challenge for a class walk.

### Lesson 6 — GIS and satellite images
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use digital mapping to compare layers of information and explain what GIS is." (UK NC KS2 geography — digital mapping)
- **Hook:** "How can a satellite show where a forest has disappeared?"
- **Key activity:** Use a free online mapping tool (such as Google Earth or OpenStreetMap) to compare satellite images and map layers of the local area and a far-away place; overlay layers (roads, rivers, land use) on acetate to model GIS.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is layering maps (GIS) useful for planners?" → It shows how different features relate (e.g. homes near a flood zone), helping decisions.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided worksheet with specific places to find / stretch: compare satellite images of one place from different years and describe the change.
- **Materials:** computers or tablets, acetate overlays, local base map.
- **Joy:** "Fly" over your own school on the digital globe.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil gives six-figure grid references for three features, describes relief from contours, calculates a distance from a scale, and explains one limitation of a world map projection.

## Unit 2 — The restless Earth

**Essential question:** Why do earthquakes and volcanoes happen where they do, and why do they affect some people more than others?

**Key vocabulary:** crust, mantle, core, tectonic plate, plate boundary, convergent, divergent, transform, magma, lava, volcano, earthquake, epicentre, magnitude, tsunami, Ring of Fire, hazard, risk, preparedness, resilience

### Lesson 7 — Inside the Earth
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the layers of the Earth and explain that the crust is broken into moving plates." (UK NC KS2 geography — physical)
- **Hook:** Cut a hard-boiled egg in half (shell cracked). "How is this like the Earth?"
- **Key activity:** Label Earth's layers; fit a plate-map jigsaw; notice how South America and Africa's coastlines fit (Alfred Wegener's continental drift idea, 1912).
- **Check for understanding:** "What evidence made Wegener think the continents were once joined?" → Coastlines that fit together, and matching fossils and rocks on continents now separated by oceans.
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled cross-section to complete / stretch: explain why Wegener's idea wasn't accepted until evidence about the sea floor was found decades later.
- **Materials:** hard-boiled eggs, plate-map jigsaw, cross-section diagrams.
- **Joy:** the Pangaea jigsaw race.

### Lesson 8 — Plate boundaries
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe what happens at the three main kinds of plate boundary." (UK NC KS2 geography — physical)
- **Hook:** "Iceland is slowly being pulled apart. Mount Everest is still rising. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Model boundaries with crackers on a layer of jam or with foam blocks: divergent (Iceland, Mid-Atlantic Ridge), convergent (the Himalayas, from India pushing into Eurasia; the Andes), transform (the San Andreas Fault in California). Map examples.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are the Himalayas still getting higher?" → The Indian plate is still pushing into the Eurasian plate, crumpling the crust upwards.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards to match boundary types / stretch: explain why earthquakes occur at all three types but volcanoes mainly at two.
- **Materials:** crackers, jam or icing, foam blocks, world plate map.
- **Joy:** edible plate boundaries.

### Lesson 9 — Volcanoes: Iceland case study
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how volcanoes form and describe their effects, positive and negative, using Iceland." (UK NC KS2 geography — volcanoes)
- **Hook:** "In 2010 an Icelandic volcano, Eyjafjallajökull, grounded flights across Europe. How?"
- **Key activity:** Structure of a volcano; types of eruption; Iceland's benefits from volcanic activity (geothermal energy heats most homes, tourism) and the costs (ash clouds, floods from melting ice).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do people choose to live near volcanoes?" → Fertile soils, geothermal energy, tourism and jobs — the benefits can outweigh the risks.
- **Differentiation:** support: benefits/costs sorting table / stretch: evaluate whether Iceland is a 'winner' or 'loser' from its volcanoes.
- **Materials:** volcano diagrams, Iceland photos and data, benefits/costs cards.
- **Joy:** model eruption (bicarbonate and vinegar in a clay volcano — science link).

### Lesson 10 — Earthquakes and tsunamis: Japan
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what causes earthquakes and tsunamis and how Japan prepares for them." (UK NC KS2 geography — earthquakes)
- **Hook:** "In 2011, a magnitude 9 earthquake struck off Japan and a tsunami followed. What does a country do to prepare?"
- **Key activity:** Model a tsunami in a water tray; learn about epicentre and magnitude; study Japan's preparation (building design, drills, warning systems, sea walls) and the limits of preparation in 2011.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can a tsunami cause damage far from the earthquake's epicentre?" → The earthquake displaces a huge volume of water, sending waves across the ocean that grow as they reach shallow coasts.
- **Differentiation:** support: sequencing cards for earthquake → tsunami / stretch: explain why even well-prepared countries can suffer great losses.
- **Materials:** water tray, blocks, earthquake data, images of Japanese drills and buildings.
- **Joy:** build and test earthquake-resistant structures on a shaking table (jelly or a tray).

### Lesson 11 — Same hazard, different impact: Nepal and New Zealand
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare the impacts of two earthquakes and explain why wealth and preparedness matter." (UK NC KS2 geography — human and physical interaction)
- **Hook:** "Two strong earthquakes: Christchurch, New Zealand (2011) and Nepal (2015). Why were the effects so different?"
- **Key activity:** Compare data (magnitude, deaths, damage, recovery) using a table; consider building standards, mountain terrain, wealth, emergency services and remoteness. Draw a conclusion with evidence.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give two reasons why an earthquake of similar strength might cause more deaths in one country than another." → e.g. building quality and codes; money for emergency response; access to remote areas; time of day and population density.
- **Differentiation:** support: a comparison table with guiding questions / stretch: suggest two realistic ways to reduce risk in Nepal and justify them.
- **Materials:** fact files, data tables, maps.
- **Joy:** "Rescue planner" — plan aid delivery to a remote mountain village.

### Lesson 12 — The Ring of Fire and Chile
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the pattern of volcanoes and earthquakes around the Pacific and describe Chile's experience." (UK NC KS2 geography — physical)
- **Hook:** "The strongest earthquake ever recorded struck Chile in 1960 (magnitude 9.5). Where on a map would you expect it?"
- **Key activity:** Plot recent earthquakes and volcanoes on a world map from data; identify the Ring of Fire and link it to plate boundaries; learn how Chile has strengthened building codes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do so many earthquakes and volcanoes happen around the edges of the Pacific Ocean?" → The Pacific plate meets several other plates there, creating active boundaries.
- **Differentiation:** support: plot 10 given points / stretch: use a live data map (e.g. USGS earthquake map) and describe the pattern this week.
- **Materials:** world outline maps, earthquake and volcano data, stickers, computers (optional).
- **Joy:** "Live earthquake watch" on a real-time map.

### Lesson 13 — Unit 2 enquiry: should people live near hazards?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a reasoned answer to a geographical enquiry question using evidence from case studies." (UK NC KS2 geography — assessment)
- **Hook:** "If you were offered a home near a volcano with free geothermal heating, would you take it?"
- **Key activity:** Pupils plan and write an answer to "Should people live in places at risk from earthquakes and volcanoes?" using at least two case studies and a map.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which piece of evidence best supports your answer, and why?" → A specific fact from a case study, explained.
- **Differentiation:** support: a writing frame with evidence cards / stretch: consider how different people (a farmer, a tourist, a government) might answer differently.
- **Materials:** case-study notes, maps, writing frames.
- **Joy:** "Estate agent" role-play selling a home near a volcano — honestly.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil explains plate boundaries with a labelled diagram, describes one volcano and one earthquake case study, and explains why impacts differ between countries.

## Unit 3 — Rivers and water

**Essential question:** How do rivers shape the land and people's lives — and why doesn't everyone have safe water?

**Key vocabulary:** source, tributary, confluence, channel, meander, ox-bow lake, floodplain, delta, estuary, mouth, erosion, transportation, deposition, drainage basin, freshwater, water scarcity, well, sanitation

### Lesson 14 — A river's journey
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the features of a river from source to mouth." (UK NC KS2 geography — rivers)
- **Hook:** "Follow a raindrop that falls on a mountain. Where will it end up?"
- **Key activity:** Label a river's long profile (upper, middle, lower course; source, tributary, confluence, meander, floodplain, delta or estuary, mouth); trace the Nile and the Amazon on maps.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a river usually narrow and fast near its source and wide and slow near its mouth?" → Near the source the land is steep and there is less water; downstream more tributaries join and the land is flatter.
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled diagram with a word bank / stretch: compare the Nile and Amazon (length, discharge, basin).
- **Materials:** river diagrams, atlases, world map.
- **Joy:** "Raindrop's journey" story map.

### Lesson 15 — Erosion, transportation and deposition
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how rivers erode, transport and deposit material using a stream-table model." (UK NC KS2 geography — physical processes)
- **Hook:** "How does water carve a canyon?"
- **Key activity:** Stream table (a tray of sand tilted with a water flow): observe erosion on the outside of bends, deposition on the inside, meander formation and a delta. Record with photos and sketches.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a river erode the outside of a bend but deposit on the inside?" → Water flows faster on the outside of the bend (more energy to erode) and slower on the inside (drops material).
- **Differentiation:** support: guided observation sheet / stretch: change one variable (slope or flow) and describe the effect.
- **Materials:** stream table or large tray, sand, water jugs, blocks, phones/tablets for photos.
- **Joy:** watching a meander form.

### Lesson 16 — Rivers and people: the Nile
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a river supports people's lives and why it can cause conflict." (UK NC KS2 geography — human geography)
- **Hook:** "Egypt gets almost all its fresh water from the Nile — but the river starts in other countries. What could go wrong?"
- **Key activity:** Map the Nile basin (countries it flows through); uses (farming, drinking water, transport, hydroelectric power); the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam and the debate between countries sharing the river.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might building a dam upstream worry countries downstream?" → It could reduce or control the water that reaches them for farming and drinking.
- **Differentiation:** support: map with countries labelled and use cards / stretch: role-play a meeting between upstream and downstream countries.
- **Materials:** Nile basin map, fact cards, photographs.
- **Joy:** "River summit" negotiation role-play.

### Lesson 17 — Water scarcity
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what water scarcity is, where it happens and why." (UK NC KS2 geography / sustainability; link to *A Long Walk to Water*)
- **Hook:** "Only a small fraction of Earth's water is fresh water we can easily use. Where is the rest?"
- **Key activity:** Model Earth's water with 1 litre (about 2.5% fresh, most of it frozen or underground); map regions of water scarcity using data from a reputable source (e.g. UNICEF/WHO); discuss physical and economic causes; link to Nya's walk in South Sudan.
- **Check for understanding:** "What's the difference between physical water scarcity and economic water scarcity?" → Physical: not enough water in the area; economic: water exists but people can't afford or access it (no pipes, wells or treatment).
- **Differentiation:** support: a guided data map / stretch: calculate what percentage of a day Nya spends walking for water (maths link).
- **Materials:** 1-litre jug, measuring cylinders, pipettes, data maps.
- **Joy:** the "one litre" water model.

### Lesson 18 — Solutions: wells, filters and saving water
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate solutions to water problems, including building a simple filter." (UK NC KS2 geography / science link)
- **Hook:** "Salva's organisation drills wells. What other solutions are there?"
- **Key activity:** Build and test a simple water filter (gravel, sand, charcoal, cotton) with muddy water — NOT for drinking. Compare solutions: wells, rainwater harvesting, filters, reducing waste at home; calculate household water use.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why isn't filtered water from our model safe to drink?" → Filters remove visible dirt but not invisible germs; water must also be disinfected (e.g. boiled or treated).
- **Differentiation:** support: a step-by-step filter guide / stretch: design an improved filter and explain why each layer is there.
- **Materials:** plastic bottles cut in half, gravel, sand, activated charcoal, cotton wool, muddy water, cups.
- **Joy:** the filter challenge — clearest water wins (not for drinking!).

### Lesson 19 — Unit 3 enquiry: our local river or water supply
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find out where our water comes from and present my findings with a map." (UK NC KS2 geography — local area)
- **Hook:** "When you turn on the tap at school, where has that water been?"
- **Key activity:** Research the local water source (river, reservoir, groundwater, desalination) using a local water company or council website; map its route from source to tap; present findings.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where does our water come from, and what happens to it before it reaches the tap?" → A specific local source and the treatment steps (e.g. filtered, disinfected, pumped).
- **Differentiation:** support: a research guide with questions and a map outline / stretch: compare with where water comes from in a city in another continent.
- **Materials:** local water information, local maps, presentation materials.
- **Joy:** "From source to tap" illustrated maps.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil labels river features and explains erosion and deposition, explains a cause of water scarcity with a named example, and maps the local water supply.

## Unit 4 — Trade and globalisation

**Essential question:** How are we connected to people around the world through the things we buy — and is trade fair?

**Key vocabulary:** trade, import, export, supply chain, raw material, manufacture, globalisation, interdependence, producer, consumer, fair trade, wage, profit, sustainable, carbon footprint

### Lesson 20 — Where do our things come from?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can map where everyday products come from and explain the words import and export." (UK NC KS2 geography — trade)
- **Hook:** "Check the labels on your shoes, clothes and pencil case. How many countries are in this room?"
- **Key activity:** Class 'label hunt'; plot countries on a world map with string to the school; discuss why products come from so many places.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might a T-shirt be designed in one country, made in another and sold in a third?" → Each step happens where it is cheapest or where the skills and materials are.
- **Differentiation:** support: a list of countries to locate with an atlas / stretch: calculate the rough distance travelled by one product.
- **Materials:** product labels, world map, string, atlases.
- **Joy:** the string web across the world map.

### Lesson 21 — The journey of chocolate
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the supply chain of chocolate from cacao farm to shop." (UK NC KS2 geography — trade; human geography)
- **Hook:** Taste a square of chocolate (allergy-checked). "Where did this begin?"
- **Key activity:** Supply-chain sequencing: cacao grown mainly in West Africa (Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana together grow more than half the world's cacao), fermented, dried, shipped, processed and sold. Simulation: divide the price of a chocolate bar among the people in the chain.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do cacao farmers often receive only a small share of the price of a chocolate bar?" → Much of the price goes to processing, brands, transport and shops; farmers have little bargaining power.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards of the supply chain / stretch: suggest how the price split could be made fairer.
- **Materials:** chocolate (allergy-checked), cacao pod or photos, supply-chain cards, price cards, world map.
- **Joy:** the tasting — and the 'who gets what' reveal.

### Lesson 22 — Fair trade
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what fair trade aims to do and evaluate its benefits and limits." (UK NC KS2 geography / global citizenship)
- **Hook:** "What does this logo on a banana or chocolate bar promise?"
- **Key activity:** Learn about Fairtrade certification (minimum price, premium for community projects); case study of a cooperative (e.g. Kuapa Kokoo in Ghana, a farmer-owned cocoa cooperative); evaluate benefits and limitations.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does a fair-trade minimum price help a farmer when world prices fall?" → They still receive at least the minimum, so their income is more secure.
- **Differentiation:** support: benefits/limits sorting cards / stretch: argue whether shoppers or governments have more power to make trade fair.
- **Materials:** fair-trade products and packaging, case-study sheet.
- **Joy:** "Fair-trade shop" role-play.

### Lesson 23 — The journey of a T-shirt
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can trace a global supply chain for clothing and discuss working conditions and sustainability." (UK NC KS2 geography — globalisation)
- **Hook:** "A T-shirt can travel around the world before you wear it."
- **Key activity:** Trace cotton (grown in countries such as India or the USA) → spinning and weaving → sewing (e.g. in Bangladesh) → shops. Discuss factory safety (age-appropriately, including the 2013 Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh as a turning point) and the environmental costs of fast fashion.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is one social and one environmental cost of very cheap clothing?" → Social: low wages or unsafe factories; environmental: water use, pollution, waste when clothes are thrown away quickly.
- **Differentiation:** support: a supply-chain map with stages labelled / stretch: suggest three actions (by shoppers, companies, governments) and rank them.
- **Materials:** world map, supply-chain cards, a cotton boll (if available), T-shirt labels.
- **Joy:** "Swap shop" planning — a class clothes swap for charity.

### Lesson 24 — Interdependence and food miles
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain interdependence and weigh the environmental costs of transporting goods." (UK NC KS2 geography — sustainability)
- **Hook:** "Is a tomato grown locally in a heated greenhouse better for the planet than one grown in the sun far away?"
- **Key activity:** Map the origin of a meal's ingredients; calculate food miles; discuss that transport is only part of the environmental cost (growing conditions, season, method matter too).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are 'food miles' alone not enough to judge a food's environmental impact?" → How it's grown (heating, water, fertiliser) and how it's transported (ship vs plane) also matter.
- **Differentiation:** support: ingredient cards with origin countries / stretch: compare two versions of the same food with data.
- **Materials:** ingredient cards, world map, calculators, data sheets.
- **Joy:** plan a 'lowest-impact lunch' menu.

### Lesson 25 — Unit 4 enquiry: is global trade good for everyone?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present a balanced answer to a geographical enquiry using evidence from supply chains." (UK NC KS2 geography — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Winners and losers of trade: who are they?"
- **Key activity:** Groups prepare a short balanced presentation (benefits and problems of global trade, with examples from chocolate and T-shirts) and a conclusion.
- **Check for understanding:** "Name one group that benefits from global trade and one that can lose out, with evidence." → e.g. consumers get cheaper goods; some farmers and factory workers earn very little.
- **Differentiation:** support: a presentation frame / stretch: include a suggestion for making trade fairer and evaluate it.
- **Materials:** unit notes, presentation materials.
- **Joy:** presentations to another class.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil describes a supply chain with a map, explains interdependence, and evaluates fair trade with at least one benefit and one limitation.

## Unit 5 — Cities and population

**Essential question:** Why are so many people moving to cities, and how can cities be good places to live for everyone?

**Key vocabulary:** population, population density, urban, rural, urbanisation, migration, push factor, pull factor, megacity, informal settlement, infrastructure, land use, sustainable city, green space

### Lesson 26 — Where people live
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe patterns of population distribution and density using maps and data." (UK NC KS2 geography — human geography)
- **Hook:** "There are about 8 billion people on Earth. Are they spread out evenly?"
- **Key activity:** Read a population-density map; explain dense areas (fertile river valleys, coasts) and sparse areas (deserts, mountains, the poles); compare densities of a few countries (maths link: division).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do few people live in the Sahara or Antarctica?" → Extreme climates, little water and difficulty growing food make it hard to live there.
- **Differentiation:** support: a map with guiding questions / stretch: calculate population density for two countries from data and compare.
- **Materials:** population-density maps, data tables, calculators.
- **Joy:** "Human density" game — the class stands in marked squares to feel density.

### Lesson 27 — Why move to the city?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain push and pull factors for rural-to-urban migration." (UK NC KS2 geography — migration)
- **Hook:** "Since around 2007, more than half the world's people have lived in towns and cities. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Sort push factors (lack of jobs, drought) and pull factors (jobs, schools, hospitals); a short story of a family moving to Lagos (Nigeria); link to *The Arrival* in English.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between a push and a pull factor? Give one of each." → Push: a reason to leave (e.g. drought); pull: a reason to go somewhere (e.g. jobs).
- **Differentiation:** support: picture sort cards / stretch: explain why the reality of city life may differ from people's hopes.
- **Materials:** push/pull cards, photographs of Lagos, story text.
- **Joy:** "Should they move?" family decision role-play.

### Lesson 28 — Megacities: Tokyo, Mumbai and Lagos
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare three megacities and explain their opportunities and challenges." (UK NC KS2 geography — human geography)
- **Hook:** "Tokyo's metropolitan area has more people than many whole countries. How does such a city work?"
- **Key activity:** Jigsaw groups research one city (transport, housing, jobs, challenges); compare using a table. Include informal settlements such as Dharavi in Mumbai, which is also a place of busy small industries and recycling.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it too simple to describe an informal settlement only as a 'slum'?" → They face serious problems (overcrowding, sanitation) but are also communities with homes, businesses and enterprise.
- **Differentiation:** support: research cards with key facts / stretch: suggest one improvement for one city and justify it.
- **Materials:** city fact files, photographs, comparison tables, maps.
- **Joy:** "Megacity tour guide" presentations.

### Lesson 29 — Land use in our nearest city
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify land-use zones in a city using maps and satellite images." (UK NC KS2 geography — land use)
- **Hook:** "From a plane, a city looks like a patchwork. What is each patch for?"
- **Key activity:** Use satellite images and maps of the nearest city to identify land-use zones (centre, housing, industry, green space, transport); colour-code a map; discuss why land uses are where they are.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are city centres usually full of shops and offices rather than houses with big gardens?" → Land in the centre is expensive and easy to reach, so businesses use it; houses with gardens need more, cheaper land further out.
- **Differentiation:** support: a partly coded map with a key / stretch: compare land use in your city with one of the megacities.
- **Materials:** satellite images, city maps, coloured pencils, tracing paper.
- **Joy:** "Satellite spotter" challenge.

### Lesson 30 — Designing a sustainable city
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can design a sustainable neighbourhood and justify my choices using geography." (UK NC KS2 geography / sustainability)
- **Hook:** "Some cities are famous for bicycles, parks or clean public transport. What would your ideal city have?"
- **Key activity:** Groups design a neighbourhood on a grid (homes, schools, green space, transport, energy, water, waste) with a budget; justify choices; peer review.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain one feature of your design that helps the environment and one that helps people." → e.g. cycle lanes reduce emissions; parks improve health and wellbeing.
- **Differentiation:** support: a design kit of feature cards / stretch: include a flood-management feature linked to Unit 3.
- **Materials:** large grid paper, feature cards, coloured pens, building blocks (optional).
- **Joy:** city designs displayed as a "future city expo".

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil explains population patterns with a map, gives push and pull factors, compares two megacities, and justifies a sustainable city feature.

## Unit 6 — Fieldwork: how good is our local environment?

**Essential question:** How can we measure the quality of our local environment, and how could it be improved?

**Key vocabulary:** fieldwork, enquiry question, hypothesis, environmental quality survey, bipolar scale, traffic count, land-use survey, sample, sketch map, annotated photograph, data, conclusion, evaluation

### Lesson 31 — Planning a fieldwork enquiry
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a fieldwork enquiry with a question, hypothesis and methods." (UK NC KS2 geography — fieldwork)
- **Hook:** "Is the area around our school a good place for people and nature? How could we prove it?"
- **Key activity:** Choose an enquiry question (e.g. "Does environmental quality improve with distance from the main road?"); write a hypothesis; choose methods (environmental quality survey with bipolar scales, traffic count, noise levels, land-use map, photographs); plan sites on a map with grid references.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we use the same survey sheet at every site?" → So results are fair and can be compared.
- **Differentiation:** support: a planning frame with method choices / stretch: plan a method for measuring something new (e.g. litter per 10 m).
- **Materials:** local maps, survey templates, planning frames.
- **Joy:** designing your own survey sheet.

### Lesson 32 — Fieldwork day (1): collecting data
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can collect accurate data using fieldwork methods at several sites." (UK NC KS2 geography — fieldwork)
- **Hook:** "Geographers go outside. Clipboards ready!"
- **Key activity:** Visit 4–5 sites near school (safely supervised); complete environmental quality surveys, traffic counts (in fixed time), noise ratings (app or scale), sketch maps and annotated photographs.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we count traffic for exactly the same length of time at each site?" → To make the counts comparable — it's a fair test.
- **Differentiation:** support: roles in a group (counter, recorder, photographer) with clear instructions / stretch: record extra qualitative observations and note any problems with the method.
- **Materials:** clipboards, survey sheets, stopwatches, tally counters, tablets or cameras, maps, high-visibility vests. Safety: risk assessment, adult supervision, road safety.
- **Joy:** the fieldwork trip itself.

### Lesson 33 — Fieldwork (2): presenting data
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present fieldwork data using maps, graphs and annotated photographs." (UK NC KS2 geography — fieldwork / maths link)
- **Hook:** "How can we make our data tell a story?"
- **Key activity:** Present data: bar charts of traffic counts, radar or bar graphs of environmental quality scores, proportional symbols on a map, annotated photographs; use a spreadsheet where possible.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a map with symbols a good way to show where environmental quality is high and low?" → It shows the pattern in space — where things are — which a table cannot show at a glance.
- **Differentiation:** support: graph templates / stretch: calculate the mean score per site and plot it on a map.
- **Materials:** graph paper, spreadsheets, printed photographs, base maps.
- **Joy:** creating a display "map of our findings".

### Lesson 34 — Fieldwork (3): conclusions and evaluation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw conclusions from fieldwork data and evaluate my methods." (UK NC KS2 geography — fieldwork)
- **Hook:** "Was our hypothesis right? How sure are we?"
- **Key activity:** Answer the enquiry question with evidence; identify patterns and anomalies; evaluate (time of day, weather, sample size, subjectivity of scores); suggest improvements.
- **Check for understanding:** "Name one limitation of your data and how it could be improved." → e.g. only one day's traffic — repeat on different days and times.
- **Differentiation:** support: a conclusion and evaluation frame / stretch: suggest a follow-up enquiry.
- **Materials:** presented data, frames.
- **Joy:** "Hypothesis verdict" reveal.

### Lesson 35 — Taking action and year review
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can propose improvements to the local environment based on evidence and reflect on the year's geography." (UK NC KS2 geography — assessment / citizenship)
- **Hook:** "Your findings could change something real. What would you ask the council or school to do?"
- **Key activity:** Groups write a short proposal or letter to the school or local council with evidence-based recommendations; year review quiz linking maps, hazards, rivers, trade and cities.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which piece of your fieldwork evidence best supports your recommendation?" → A specific data point or map linked to the recommendation.
- **Differentiation:** support: a letter frame / stretch: include a map and graph in the proposal and address a possible objection.
- **Materials:** letter frames, fieldwork results, review quiz.
- **Joy:** send the proposals — and share any reply.

**End-of-unit check:** Pupil completes a full fieldwork enquiry (question, data collection, presentation with a map and graph, conclusion, evaluation) and makes an evidence-based recommendation.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Map skills** — gives six-figure grid references, reads contours and uses scale to measure distance on a local map.
2. **Projections and GIS** — explains one distortion of a world map and uses digital mapping to compare layers or dates.
3. **Tectonics** — explains plate boundaries and the global pattern of earthquakes and volcanoes.
4. **Hazards and people** — compares the impacts of a hazard in two countries and explains why they differ.
5. **Rivers** — describes river features and processes and explains a cause of water scarcity.
6. **Trade** — traces a supply chain and evaluates fair trade.
7. **Cities** — explains urbanisation using push/pull factors and compares megacities.
8. **Fieldwork** — completes and evaluates an enquiry with maps and graphs.

## Teacher guidance

**Common misconceptions to watch for**
- *"Maps show the world exactly as it is."* All projections distort; choose them for a purpose.
- *"Contour lines are paths or roads."* They join points of equal height.
- *"Volcanoes only happen in hot countries."* Iceland and Antarctica have volcanoes; location depends on plate boundaries.
- *"Poor countries have more earthquakes."* The hazard is physical; the impact depends on wealth and preparedness.
- *"Rivers flow from north to south."* Rivers flow downhill, in any direction (the Nile flows north).
- *"Everything in a developing city is poor."* Avoid single stories; show enterprise, culture and diversity.

**How to teach it well**
- Use maps every lesson; keep atlases and a globe within reach.
- Use real, current data (from USGS, UN, WHO/UNICEF, national statistics offices) and name sources.
- Present people in case studies as active agents, not just victims; include voices from the places studied.
- Adapt fieldwork and the local water study to your own location; every school can do a meaningful environmental quality survey.
- Cross-curricular: maths (scale, coordinates, graphs, means), science (volcanoes, water filters, climate), English (*A Long Walk to Water*, *The Arrival*, persuasive letters), life skills (fair trade, sustainability).

**Vertical connections**
- *From Year 4:* the water cycle, climate zones and biomes, four-figure grid references and the first fieldwork cycle.
- *Into Year 6:* more complex map analysis (cross-sections, GIS), deeper study of development and inequality, climate and ecosystems, and an independent fieldwork investigation.
