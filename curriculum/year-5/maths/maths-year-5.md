## Year overview

Year 5 is the capstone of the primary maths spine before the move to secondary-style content in Year 6.
It assumes the Year 4 foundations: place value to 1,000,000, factors, multiples, primes, squares and
cubes, long multiplication by a 2-digit number, short division by a 1-digit number, adding and
subtracting fractions whose denominators are related, multiplying a fraction by a whole number,
decimals to three places, a first meeting with percentages, measuring angles with a protractor,
composite rectilinear area, and line graphs. Children should arrive knowing all times tables to 12 × 12
by instant recall.

The year moves in three arcs. **Term 1 (Units 1–4)** secures number: place value to 10,000,000,
negative numbers across zero, the full set of written methods including **long division by a 2-digit
number**, highest common factor and lowest common multiple, **order of operations**, and adding and
subtracting any fractions and mixed numbers. **Term 2 (Units 5–9)** is the heart of the year and the
bridge to Year 6: **multiplying and dividing fractions**, all four operations with decimals,
**fraction–decimal–percentage equivalence**, a full unit of **ratio and proportion** (sharing in a
ratio, scale factor, unit rate, recipes, similar shapes) and a full unit of **algebra** (sequences,
expressions, substitution, formulae, one- and two-step equations). **Term 3 (Units 10–15)** applies
number to space and data: converting units (including time zones and miles/kilometres), area of
triangles and parallelograms, volume of cuboids, angle rules and polygons, circles and nets,
coordinates in all four quadrants, pie charts and the mean — and closes with three extended
investigations and a year-end review.

Every lesson follows **concrete → pictorial → abstract**: a manipulative (base-10, place-value
counters, fraction strips, Cuisenaire rods, multilink cubes, algebra tiles, geoboards, protractors,
compasses) introduces the idea, a **bar model**, number line, area model or ratio table pictures it,
and only then is it written symbolically. Each unit runs **introduce → practise → apply → retrieve**
and ends with a mastery lesson; each term contains retrieval lessons, and daily lessons start with a
5-minute mixed retrieval warm-up (not counted as separate lessons). Children who master early go
**deeper, not faster** — richer problems in the same idea (NRICH-style), not next year's content.

**What Year 6 relies on from this year:** instant fraction/decimal/percentage conversion, confident
multiplication and division of fractions and decimals, the meaning of ratio and unit rate, order of
operations with brackets and powers, writing and solving simple equations, substitution into
formulae, negative numbers on a number line and coordinate grid, area of triangles and
parallelograms, volume of cuboids, and calculating the mean. Year 6 extends these to operations with
negative numbers, dividing a fraction by a fraction, percentage change, algebraic manipulation, and
circle area.

## Unit 1 — Place value to 10,000,000 and negative numbers

**Essential question:** How does our base-ten system let us write any number — however large, and even below zero — with just ten digits?

**Key vocabulary:** million, ten million, place value, digit, value, partition, compare, order, round, estimate, negative, positive, integer, interval, across zero, base ten

### Lesson 1 — How big is a million?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how 1,000,000 is built from ten hundred-thousands and read numbers up to 10,000,000." (UK NC Y6 place value / CCSS 5.NBT.1)
- **Hook:** "If you counted one number every second without stopping, how long would it take to reach one million?" (About 11.6 days.)
- **Key activity:** Concrete: a 1,000 base-10 cube; the class estimates how many cubes would fill the room to make a million. Pictorial: extend the place-value chart leftwards in groups of three (ones, thousands, millions), showing each column is ten times the one to its right. Abstract: I do — read 3,405,020 group by group; we do — read five population figures from real cities; you do — write numbers dictated in words.
- **Check for understanding:** "Nadia says 1,000,000 has six zeros so it is six times bigger than 10. Is she right? Explain." → No: each extra zero is another ×10, not +1 step; 1,000,000 is 100,000 times bigger than 10.
- **Differentiation:** support: use a place-value chart with the three-digit groups coloured and place-value counters (1, 10, 100, 1,000) to build numbers to 100,000 first / stretch: estimate how many millimetres long a line of one million 1 cm cubes would be (10,000,000 mm = 10 km) and justify.
- **Materials:** base-10 blocks (including a 1,000 cube), place-value counters, large place-value chart to ten millions, a stopwatch.
- **Joy:** the "million-second" reveal — calculate together that a million seconds is about 11½ days and a billion seconds is nearly 32 years.

### Lesson 2 — Reading, writing and partitioning numbers to 10,000,000
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read, write and partition any number up to 10,000,000 in more than one way." (UK NC Y6 place value / CCSS 5.NBT.1)
- **Hook:** A mystery number clue card: "I have 7 millions, no hundred-thousands, 40 thousands and 25 ones. Who am I?" (7,040,025)
- **Key activity:** Concrete: build numbers with place-value counters on a Gattegno-style chart. Pictorial: part–whole models showing standard partition (4,000,000 + 300,000 + 20,000 + 5) and non-standard partition (4,320,005 = 4,300,000 + 20,005). Abstract: write numbers in words and digits, including those with zeros as placeholders.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which digit has the greater value: the 5 in 5,061,200 or the 5 in 1,650,000? How many times greater?" → The 5 in 5,061,200 (5 million) is 100 times the 5 in 1,650,000 (50,000).
- **Differentiation:** support: partition 6-digit numbers first, using counters and a chart with column headings / stretch: find three different non-standard partitions of 2,008,000 using exactly three parts.
- **Materials:** place-value counters, Gattegno chart (printed), digit cards 0–9, mini-whiteboards.
- **Joy:** "Number detective" — children write clue cards for a partner's mystery number and try to stump them.

### Lesson 3 — Comparing and ordering large numbers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare and order numbers up to 10,000,000 and explain which digit decides." (UK NC Y6 place value / CCSS 5.NBT.1)
- **Hook:** Five country populations on cards with the names hidden — can we order them before we find out which country is which?
- **Key activity:** Concrete: build two close numbers side by side with counters and find the first column that differs. Pictorial: position numbers on a 0–10,000,000 number line. Abstract: use <, > and = ; order sets of numbers with different numbers of digits; I do → we do → you do with real data (populations of Lagos, Lima, Seoul, Sydney, Cairo from a current almanac).
- **Check for understanding:** "Leo says 999,999 is bigger than 1,000,001 because 9 is bigger than 1. Explain his mistake." → Compare digits only when numbers are aligned by place: 999,999 has 6 digits, 1,000,001 has 7, so 1,000,001 is larger.
- **Differentiation:** support: numbers presented in a place-value grid so columns line up / stretch: find all numbers that fit 4,5_7,_00 < 4,560,000 and explain the constraints.
- **Materials:** place-value counters, number-line strip 0–10,000,000, population fact cards (teacher sources current figures from an almanac or UN data), digit cards.
- **Joy:** "Population Top Trumps" card game using real city data.

### Lesson 4 — Rounding to any degree of accuracy
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can round any whole number to the nearest 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000, 100,000 or 1,000,000 and say when rounding is useful." (UK NC Y6 place value / CCSS 5.NBT.4 extended)
- **Hook:** A news headline says "Nearly 3 million visitors" — the real figure was 2,648,310. Is the headline honest?
- **Key activity:** Concrete/pictorial: place the number on a number line between the two nearest multiples and see which it is closer to; midpoint rule (5 or more rounds up). Abstract: round the same number to every degree in a table and notice what stays the same. Discuss when rounding helps (headlines, estimating) and when it misleads.
- **Check for understanding:** "Round 4,965,000 to the nearest 100,000. Why does the millions digit change?" → 5,000,000; it lies between 4,900,000 and 5,000,000 and 65,000 is past the midpoint 50,000, so it rounds up, which carries into the millions.
- **Differentiation:** support: blank number lines with the two multiples already marked / stretch: "A number rounds to 350,000 to the nearest 10,000. What are the smallest and largest whole numbers it could be?" → 345,000 and 354,999.
- **Materials:** blank number lines, place-value counters, highlighters, newspaper or website headlines with rounded figures.
- **Joy:** "Headline writers" — pupils write two headlines for the same real figure, one that rounds fairly and one that exaggerates.

### Lesson 5 — Negative numbers in context
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can place, order and compare negative numbers using real contexts." (UK NC Y6 place value / CCSS 6.NS.5–6 preview)
- **Hook:** The Dead Sea shore is about 430 m below sea level and Mount Everest is 8,849 m above it. How can one number line hold both?
- **Key activity:** Concrete: a vertical number line on the floor (masking tape) from −10 to 10; children stand on temperatures called out. Pictorial: a thermometer and a cross-section of land and sea. Abstract: order sets like −8, 3, −1, 0, −12 and use < and >, explaining that −8 < −1 because it is further below zero.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which is colder, −15 °C or −5 °C? Explain using a number line." → −15 °C, because it is 15 degrees below zero, further from zero on the negative side.
- **Differentiation:** support: work only between −10 and 10 with a large vertical thermometer / stretch: order elevations of five real places (e.g. Death Valley −86 m, Dead Sea shore ≈ −430 m) and explain what "below sea level" means.
- **Materials:** masking-tape floor number line, large demonstration thermometer, temperature cards, world elevation fact cards.
- **Joy:** "Human thermometer" — children physically move up and down the floor line as the teacher reads a world weather report.

### Lesson 6 — Calculating intervals across zero
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find the difference between two numbers when one is negative, and count on or back across zero." (UK NC Y6 place value / CCSS 6.NS.7 preview)
- **Hook:** "In Yakutsk, Russia, it can be −40 °C in January and +20 °C in July. How big is that swing?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: hop along the floor number line from −4 to 5, counting 4 hops to zero and 5 more. Pictorial: jumps on an empty number line split at zero. Abstract: solve temperature, elevation and bank-balance problems; start at −3, rise 8 → 5; start at 2, fall 7 → −5.
- **Check for understanding:** "The temperature was −6 °C and rose by 9 degrees. Sam says it is now −15 °C. What did he do wrong?" → He moved the wrong way (fell instead of rose); −6 + 9 = 3 °C.
- **Differentiation:** support: counting in single hops on a labelled number line / stretch: find two temperatures 13 degrees apart where one is negative and their total is 5 → 9 and −4.
- **Materials:** floor number line, individual number lines −20 to 20, thermometer images, world temperature data table.
- **Joy:** "Weather reporter" — present a two-city temperature swing report to the class, from Antarctica to the Sahara.

### Lesson 7 — Estimating and checking with large numbers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can round numbers to estimate answers and decide whether a calculated answer is sensible." (UK NC Y6 place value / CCSS 5.NBT.4)
- **Hook:** "A stadium sold 48,915 tickets on Saturday and 51,204 on Sunday. The website says 1,001,190 were sold altogether. Instantly — is that right?"
- **Key activity:** Model rounding to one significant digit to estimate (≈ 50,000 + 50,000 = 100,000). Children judge a list of "claims" — possible, impossible or needs checking — and justify with an estimate before calculating any exactly.
- **Check for understanding:** "Estimate 3,982,000 − 1,015,500. Is 2,966,500 a reasonable exact answer?" → Estimate 4,000,000 − 1,000,000 = 3,000,000, so yes, 2,966,500 is reasonable.
- **Differentiation:** support: numbers already placed on a number line to help rounding / stretch: create an incorrect claim that an estimate would NOT catch (e.g. a small digit error) and explain why estimation has limits.
- **Materials:** claim cards, place-value charts, calculators (for checking only), mini-whiteboards.
- **Joy:** "Fact or Fake?" — the class votes on outrageous number claims, then a calculator verdict is revealed.

### Lesson 8 — Number systems of the world
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare our base-ten place-value system with Roman, Mayan and Babylonian numerals and explain why place value and zero matter." (UK NC Y6 place value / enrichment)
- **Hook:** "The Maya counted in twenties and drew zero as a shell. Why would someone count in twenties?" (Fingers and toes.)
- **Key activity:** Concrete: build Mayan numerals with shells (0), dots (1) and sticks (5) in vertical place-value levels of 1 and 20. Pictorial: compare Roman numerals (no place value, no zero) with Babylonian base-60 marks (which survive in our 60 minutes and 360°). Abstract: convert numbers up to 399 into Mayan form and years into Roman numerals (e.g. 2026 = MMXXVI).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it much harder to do column addition with Roman numerals than with our digits?" → Roman numerals have no place value and no zero, so the columns don't line up by value.
- **Differentiation:** support: Mayan numbers to 19 only, with a symbol key / stretch: write 2,026 in Mayan base-20 (5 × 400 + 1 × 20 + 6) and explain each level.
- **Materials:** dried beans or counters (dots), lolly sticks (bars), small shells or shell drawings (zero), Roman-numeral reference cards, a clock face (for base 60).
- **Joy:** write your birth year and age in Mayan, Roman and Babylonian symbols on a decorated "ancient number card".

### Lesson 9 — Retrieval and reasoning: place value
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use place value, rounding and negatives to solve reasoning puzzles." (UK NC Y6 place value — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "I am a 7-digit number. Rounded to the nearest million I am 3,000,000. All my digits are different, and my ones digit is the largest. What could I be?"
- **Key activity:** Mixed retrieval quiz (low stakes, self-marked) then paired reasoning challenges: digit-card puzzles, "always, sometimes, never" statements (e.g. "Rounding to the nearest 1,000 gives a bigger number"), and a negative-number temperature puzzle.
- **Check for understanding:** "Always, sometimes or never: a number with more digits is bigger. Explain." → Always for whole numbers without leading zeros (with decimals or negatives it changes, e.g. −1,000 < 5).
- **Differentiation:** support: digit-card puzzles with fewer constraints and a place-value grid / stretch: write your own "Who am I?" 7-digit puzzle with exactly one answer.
- **Materials:** digit cards 0–9 per pair, place-value grids, retrieval quiz sheet, number lines −20 to 20.
- **Joy:** puzzle swap — pairs trade their created puzzles and race to solve them.

### Lesson 10 — Unit 1 mastery check
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show what I have mastered about large numbers and negative numbers, and fix any gaps." (UK NC Y6 place value — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Today you are the teacher: find and correct three mistakes in 'Alex's homework'."
- **Key activity:** Short independent mastery task (below), then error-analysis of worked examples with deliberate mistakes; the teacher confers one-to-one and regroups for a 10-minute re-teach or extension.
- **Check for understanding:** "Alex rounded 6,459,999 to the nearest million as 7,000,000. Correct or not? Why?" → Incorrect: the midpoint between 6,000,000 and 7,000,000 is 6,500,000, and 6,459,999 is below it, so it rounds to 6,000,000.
- **Differentiation:** support: mastery task with a place-value chart and number line available / stretch: after the task, design a 3-question quiz that would reveal a classmate's misconception.
- **Materials:** mastery task sheet, place-value counters, number lines, red "teacher pens".
- **Joy:** "Teacher's marking" — children mark Alex's work with ticks and helpful comments in red pen.

**End-of-unit check:** (1) Write 4,030,207 in words. (2) Order 2,099,999; 2,100,000; 2,010,999. (3) Round 7,452,801 to the nearest 10,000 and the nearest million (7,450,000; 7,000,000). (4) The temperature falls from 4 °C to −7 °C — by how much? (11 °C). (5) Explain why 0 matters in 3,006,000.

## Unit 2 — Four operations: mental and written methods

**Essential question:** How do I choose the most efficient method — mental, written or estimated — and be sure my answer is right?

**Key vocabulary:** efficient, compensation, partition, column method, exchange, inverse, product, long multiplication, placeholder, dividend, divisor, quotient, remainder, long division, short division, estimate, multi-step, bar model

### Lesson 11 — Mental addition and subtraction strategies
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose a mental strategy (compensation, bridging, partitioning) to add and subtract large numbers efficiently." (UK NC Y6 addition & subtraction / CCSS 4.NBT.4 extended)
- **Hook:** "Can you do 45,000 − 19,999 in your head faster than a friend with a calculator?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: place-value counters show why 19,999 is 1 less than 20,000. Pictorial: empty number lines for compensation (subtract 20,000, add 1 back) and bridging. Abstract: sort calculations into "mental" or "written" and justify; race the calculator.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 63,000 − 29,998 easier as 63,000 − 30,000 + 2 and not − 2?" → Taking away 30,000 removes 2 too many, so you add 2 back: 33,002.
- **Differentiation:** support: compensation with 2- and 3-digit numbers first (83 − 29) using a number line / stretch: find a calculation where compensation is slower than column method and explain why.
- **Materials:** place-value counters, blank number lines, calculators (for the race), sorting cards.
- **Joy:** "Beat the Calculator" — a pupil versus a calculator race on smart-strategy calculations.

### Lesson 12 — Column addition and subtraction with large numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add and subtract numbers with up to 7 digits using column methods and check with the inverse." (UK NC Y6 addition & subtraction / CCSS 4.NBT.4)
- **Hook:** "Mount Everest is 8,849 m tall; Mauna Kea is 10,211 m from its base on the sea floor. Which is 'tallest'?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: place-value counters for exchanging across zeros (e.g. 4,000,002 − 1,356,748). Pictorial: exchanges drawn in a place-value grid. Abstract: column method; check subtraction by adding the answer to the number subtracted.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does 6,000,000 − 1 need exchanges in every column?" → There are no ones to take from, so a million must be exchanged down through every column until there are ten ones: 5,999,999.
- **Differentiation:** support: 5-digit calculations with counters alongside / stretch: missing-digit column subtractions with 7-digit numbers, solved by reasoning about exchanges rather than guessing.
- **Materials:** place-value counters, squared paper, place-value grids.
- **Joy:** "Tallest mountain debate" — calculate both heights' differences and hold a 2-minute vote-and-justify.

### Lesson 13 — Multi-step addition and subtraction problems with bar models
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw a bar model to decide which operations a multi-step problem needs." (UK NC Y6 addition & subtraction / CCSS 4.OA.3)
- **Hook:** A charity target thermometer: target 250,000, raised 87,450 in week 1 and 64,980 in week 2. How much more is needed?
- **Key activity:** Concrete: strips of paper cut to represent parts and whole. Pictorial: bar models (part–whole and comparison). Abstract: write the number sentences from the model; I do one, we do one, you do three with increasing steps.
- **Check for understanding:** "Draw the bar model and explain which part is unknown before you calculate." → A whole of 250,000 split into 87,450, 64,980 and ? — the unknown part is 250,000 − 152,430 = 97,570.
- **Differentiation:** support: bar models partly drawn with known values labelled / stretch: write your own three-step problem whose answer is exactly 100,000.
- **Materials:** paper strips, Cuisenaire rods (for modelling parts), bar-model templates, squared paper.
- **Joy:** plan a "real" class fundraising target using a bar-model poster that fills in as the lesson goes.

### Lesson 14 — Mental multiplication and division
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply and divide mentally using known facts, place value, doubling and halving, and the distributive law." (UK NC Y6 multiplication & division / CCSS 5.NBT.2)
- **Hook:** "How can you work out 25 × 48 in your head in ten seconds?" (25 × 4 = 100, so 25 × 48 = 100 × 12 = 1,200.)
- **Key activity:** Concrete: arrays of counters show that halving one factor and doubling the other keeps the product. Pictorial: area models split 7 × 398 into 7 × 400 − 7 × 2. Abstract: ×/÷ by 10, 100, 1,000 as digits moving places; derived facts (from 6 × 7 = 42, find 60 × 700, 4,200 ÷ 6).
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain why 16 × 25 = 8 × 50 = 4 × 100." → Halving one factor and doubling the other keeps the product the same: all equal 400.
- **Differentiation:** support: doubling/halving with arrays of counters and 2-digit numbers / stretch: find three different mental routes to 35 × 18 and decide which is fastest.
- **Materials:** counters, squared paper for arrays, place-value slider (digits moving across a fixed decimal point).
- **Joy:** "Mental maths magician" — pupils perform a quick mental trick (e.g. ×25 via ÷4 × 100) for the class.

### Lesson 15 — From the area model to long multiplication
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply a 3-digit number by a 2-digit number and explain each row of long multiplication using the area model." (UK NC Y6 multiplication / CCSS 5.NBT.5)
- **Hook:** "A school orders 124 boxes of 36 pencils. How many pencils?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: base-10 blocks in an array for 24 × 13. Pictorial: area (grid) model for 124 × 36 split into 124 × 30 and 124 × 6. Abstract: record the same two partial products as rows in long multiplication (744 + 3,720 = 4,464), making the link explicit.
- **Check for understanding:** "In 124 × 36, why does the second row start with a 0?" → Because we are multiplying by 30, not 3; every product is ten times bigger, so the zero holds the ones place.
- **Differentiation:** support: 2-digit × 2-digit using base-10 blocks and a pre-drawn grid / stretch: 3-digit × 2-digit missing-digit problems.
- **Materials:** base-10 blocks, grid (area-model) templates, squared paper.
- **Joy:** "Pencil crate" — count real boxes of pencils to check the answer for a small case.

### Lesson 16 — Long multiplication: 4-digit × 2-digit
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use long multiplication to multiply a 4-digit number by a 2-digit number accurately." (UK NC Y6 multiplication / CCSS 5.NBT.5)
- **Hook:** "A plane seats 1,245 passengers a week on one route. How many in a year (52 weeks)?"
- **Key activity:** I do: 1,245 × 52 with place-value language (1,245 × 2 = 2,490; 1,245 × 50 = 62,250; total 64,740). We do: two more with estimates first. You do: practice set with an estimate column and a "does it make sense?" column.
- **Check for understanding:** "Estimate 3,021 × 48 first. Priya's answer is 14,508. Is she right?" → Estimate ≈ 3,000 × 50 = 150,000, so 14,508 is too small by a factor of 10; she probably forgot the placeholder zero (correct: 145,008).
- **Differentiation:** support: 3-digit × 2-digit on squared paper with column headings / stretch: which digits 1–6 used once each make the greatest product in ☐☐☐☐ × ☐☐? Justify.
- **Materials:** squared paper, place-value counters (for the first example), calculators (checking only).
- **Joy:** digit-card challenge — make the largest possible product with your cards and see whose is biggest.

### Lesson 17 — Estimating products and spotting errors
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can estimate products, check with inverse or estimation, and find errors in long multiplication." (UK NC Y6 multiplication / CCSS 5.NBT.5)
- **Hook:** "Here are five 'finished' long multiplications from Robot Marker 3000. Two are wrong. Can you find them without redoing everything?"
- **Key activity:** Teach rounding factors to estimate; the last-digit check (ones digits multiply to give the answer's ones digit); error-hunting in worked examples (missing placeholder, carry errors, misaligned rows).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must 347 × 26 end in 2?" → 7 × 6 = 42, and only the ones digits affect the ones digit of the product, so the answer ends in 2 (it is 9,022).
- **Differentiation:** support: errors in 2-digit × 2-digit examples with an area model to compare / stretch: invent an error that passes the last-digit check but fails the estimate check.
- **Materials:** error-hunt cards, squared paper, highlighters.
- **Joy:** "Fix the robot" — pupils diagnose and write a repair note for the malfunctioning Robot Marker.

### Lesson 18 — Short division including 2-digit divisors
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide a 4-digit number by a 1- or 2-digit number using short division." (UK NC Y6 division / CCSS 5.NBT.6)
- **Hook:** "8,736 football stickers are shared equally between 12 friends. How many each?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: place-value counters grouped for 8,736 ÷ 4 (review). Pictorial: exchanges between columns. Abstract: short division by 11 and 12 using known tables (8,736 ÷ 12 = 728), then by 15 or 25 with a multiples list written first.
- **Check for understanding:** "In 8,736 ÷ 12, why do we start with 87 hundreds instead of 8 thousands?" → 8 thousands can't be shared into 12 groups of whole thousands, so we exchange them into 87 hundreds.
- **Differentiation:** support: 1-digit divisors with place-value counters to exchange / stretch: find a 4-digit number that divides exactly by 11, 12 and 15.
- **Materials:** place-value counters, squared paper, multiples lists (11–25).
- **Joy:** sticker-sharing challenge with a real bag of stickers for a small version.

### Lesson 19 — Long division: meaning and method
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use long division to divide a 3-digit number by a 2-digit number and explain each step." (UK NC Y6 division / CCSS 5.NBT.6)
- **Hook:** "432 students go on a trip in groups of 12. How many groups?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: place-value counters: 4 hundreds can't make a group of 12 hundreds, so exchange; 43 tens → 3 groups of 12 tens with 7 tens left; 72 ones → 6 groups. Pictorial: record each step. Abstract: long division layout (divide, multiply, subtract, bring down) with a multiples list: 432 ÷ 12 = 36; check 36 × 12 = 432.
- **Check for understanding:** "In 432 ÷ 12, what does the 72 represent?" → 7 tens left over exchanged into 70 ones, plus the 2 ones: 72 ones still to share.
- **Differentiation:** support: long division by 11 or 12 only with a printed multiples list and counters / stretch: divisions where the quotient has a zero digit (7,272 ÷ 12 = 606; 6,060 ÷ 15 = 404) — explain why the zero must be written.
- **Materials:** place-value counters, squared paper, multiples-list cards.
- **Joy:** "Division detectives" — decode a message where each letter is the answer to a division.

### Lesson 20 — Long division: 4-digit by 2-digit
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide a 4-digit number by a 2-digit number using long division." (UK NC Y6 division / CCSS 5.NBT.6)
- **Hook:** "A farm packs 7,335 eggs into boxes of 15. How many boxes?"

### Lesson 21 — Remainders: what do they mean?

- **Hook:** "800 fans travel in coaches that hold 53 people. The calculator says 15.09… How many coaches do we book?"

### Lesson 22 — Remainders as fractions and decimals

### Lesson 23 — Multiplication and division problems

## Unit 3 — Factors, primes and order of operations

**Essential question:** What hidden structure do numbers have, and why do we need an agreed order for doing calculations?

**Key vocabulary:** factor, multiple, common factor, highest common factor (HCF), common multiple, lowest common multiple (LCM), prime, composite, prime factor, factor tree, square number, cube number, index, power, brackets, order of operations

### Lesson 29 — Factors and multiples revisited
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find all factor pairs of a number systematically and list multiples." (UK NC Y6 multiplication & division / CCSS 4.OA.4)
- **Hook:** "A baker wants to arrange 36 cakes in a rectangle on a tray. How many different rectangles are possible?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: arrange 36 counters or squares into every rectangle. Pictorial: record each as a factor pair on a "rainbow" diagram. Abstract: find factors systematically by testing 1, 2, 3… until pairs repeat; notice square numbers have an odd number of factors.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do you only need to test up to 6 to find all factors of 36?" → 6 × 6 = 36; after that, the partners of factors are ones you've already found.
- **Differentiation:** support: numbers up to 24 with square tiles / stretch: find the number under 100 with the most factors (60, 72, 84, 90 and 96 each have 12).
- **Materials:** square tiles or counters, squared paper, coloured pencils for factor rainbows.
- **Joy:** "Cake tray designer" — draw your favourite arrangement as a bakery display.

### Lesson 30 — Common factors and the highest common factor
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find common factors and the highest common factor of two numbers and use it to solve problems." (UK NC Y6 multiplication & division / CCSS 6.NS.4)
- **Hook:** "A florist has 24 roses and 36 tulips. She wants identical bunches with nothing left over. What is the greatest number of bunches?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: sort counters of two colours into equal groups. Pictorial: Venn diagram of factors of 24 and 36 (common: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12). Abstract: HCF = 12 → 12 bunches of 2 roses and 3 tulips.
- **Check for understanding:** "Could the florist make 8 bunches? Why or why not?" → No: 8 is a factor of 24 but not of 36, so the tulips wouldn't share equally.
- **Differentiation:** support: pairs of numbers under 30 with counters / stretch: find the HCF of three numbers (e.g. 48, 72, 120 → 24).
- **Materials:** two-colour counters, Venn diagram hoops or templates, flower picture cards.
- **Joy:** make paper "bouquets" to prove the HCF answer works.

### Lesson 31 — Common multiples and the lowest common multiple
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find the lowest common multiple of two numbers and use it in problems." (UK NC Y6 multiplication & division / CCSS 6.NS.4)
- **Hook:** "Two lighthouses flash every 6 and every 8 seconds. They flash together now. When will they next flash together?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: Cuisenaire rods — lay trains of 6s and 8s until they line up (24). Pictorial: number lines with hops of 6 and 8. Abstract: list multiples to find the LCM; link to finding common denominators.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 48 a common multiple of 6 and 8 but not the lowest?" → Both divide 48, but 24 comes first in both lists.
- **Differentiation:** support: Cuisenaire trains with small numbers (2 and 3, 4 and 6) / stretch: when is the LCM of two numbers just their product? (When they have no common factor other than 1.)
- **Materials:** Cuisenaire rods, number lines, two torches (for the lighthouse demo).
- **Joy:** lighthouse simulation — two pupils flash torches on their counts; the class shouts when they coincide.

### Lesson 32 — Prime numbers and prime factors
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify primes up to 100 and write a number as a product of its prime factors." (UK NC Y6 multiplication & division / CCSS 6.NS.4 preview)
- **Hook:** "Over 2,000 years ago, Eratosthenes in Alexandria found a 'sieve' that catches every prime. Let's use it."
- **Key activity:** Concrete/pictorial: the Sieve of Eratosthenes on a 100 square (cross out multiples of 2, 3, 5, 7 — 25 primes remain). Abstract: factor trees (60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5); notice every tree for 60 ends with the same primes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 1 not a prime number?" → A prime has exactly two different factors (1 and itself); 1 has only one factor.
- **Differentiation:** support: primes to 30 with arrays — a prime can only make one rectangle (1 row) / stretch: explain why we only need to sieve with primes up to 7 for a 100 square (because 11 × 11 = 121 > 100).
- **Materials:** 100 squares, coloured pencils, square tiles, factor-tree templates.
- **Joy:** "Prime hunt" — each pupil gets a large number and races to break it into primes.

### Lesson 33 — Square and cube numbers and index notation
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise square and cube numbers and use the notation ² and ³." (UK NC Y5–6 / CCSS 6.EE.1)
- **Hook:** "Which is bigger: 2⁵ or 5²?" (32 vs 25.)
- **Key activity:** Concrete: build squares from tiles (1, 4, 9, 16…) and cubes from multilink (1, 8, 27, 64). Pictorial: sketch them. Abstract: notation, squares to 15² and cubes to 5³; 5² means 5 × 5, not 5 × 2.
- **Check for understanding:** "Mo says 4³ = 12. Show him why he's wrong with cubes." → 4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 — a 4 × 4 × 4 cube, not three 4s added.
- **Differentiation:** support: squares to 10² using tiles / stretch: find a number that is both a square and a cube (64 = 8² = 4³; also 1 and 729).
- **Materials:** square tiles, multilink cubes, squared paper.
- **Joy:** build the biggest cube number the class's cubes allow and calculate how many were used.

### Lesson 34 — Why we need an order of operations
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why multiplication and division are done before addition and subtraction." (UK NC Y6 order of operations / CCSS 5.OA.1)
- **Hook:** Two calculators give different answers to 2 + 3 × 4 (a simple one gives 20, a scientific one gives 14). Which is right?
- **Key activity:** Concrete: shopping context — 2 apples plus 3 bags of 4 oranges — model with counters to show 2 + 12 = 14. Abstract: rules: brackets, powers (indices), × and ÷ left to right, + and − left to right. Practice with mixed expressions.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 20 − 6 ÷ 2 equal to 7 or 17? Explain." → 17: division first (6 ÷ 2 = 3), then 20 − 3.
- **Differentiation:** support: expressions with only two operations and a counters model for each / stretch: 48 ÷ 6 × 2 — explain why left-to-right gives 16, not 4.
- **Materials:** counters, a basic calculator and a scientific calculator, expression cards.
- **Joy:** "Calculator courtroom" — the class judges which calculator is 'guilty' of the wrong answer.

### Lesson 35 — Brackets and powers in the order of operations
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate expressions with brackets and powers using the correct order." (UK NC Y6 order of operations / CCSS 5.OA.1, 6.EE.1)
- **Hook:** "Add one pair of brackets to 3 + 4 × 5 − 2 to make the answer 33."

- **Check for understanding:** "Where are the brackets in 3 + 4 × 5 − 2 = 33? Explain." → (3 + 4) × 5 − 2 = 35 − 2 = 33.

- **Materials:** expression cards, bracket cut-outs, mini-whiteboards.
- **Joy:** bracket puzzles race — the first team to hit all target numbers wins.

### Lesson 36 — The four fours challenge
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use order of operations creatively to make target numbers." (UK NC Y6 order of operations / CCSS 5.OA.1)
- **Hook:** "Using exactly four 4s and any operations, can we make every number from 0 to 20?"
- **Key activity:** Model: 4 ÷ 4 + 4 ÷ 4 = 2; 4 + 4 + 4 ÷ 4 = 9; (4 + 4 + 4) ÷ 4 = 3. Pairs build a class chart; each solution must be checked by another pair using the order of operations.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 44 ÷ 4 − 4 = 7? Check using the order of operations." → Yes: 44 ÷ 4 = 11, 11 − 4 = 7.

### Lesson 37 — Retrieval and reasoning: number structure
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use factors, multiples, primes and order of operations to solve reasoning puzzles." (UK NC Y6 — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "I am a 2-digit number. I am a multiple of 5, one more than a square number, and my digits add to 11. Who am I?" (65 = 64 + 1.)
- **Key activity:** Mixed retrieval quiz, then a carousel of four puzzle stations: number riddles, factor/multiple Venn sorts, prime-or-not debate, bracket puzzles.

### Lesson 38 — Unit 3 mastery check
- **Duration:** 35 min

- **Key activity:** Independent mastery task, then conferencing and targeted re-teach or deeper problems.
- **Check for understanding:** "Find the HCF and LCM of 12 and 18, and explain what each means." → HCF 6 (largest number dividing both); LCM 36 (smallest number both divide into).

## Unit 4 — Fractions I: equivalence, adding and subtracting

**Essential question:** How can fractions with different denominators be compared and combined?

**Key vocabulary:** numerator, denominator, equivalent, simplify, simplest form, common denominator, lowest common denominator, improper fraction, mixed number, compare, order, unit fraction, Egyptian fraction

### Lesson 39 — Equivalent fractions revisited
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can generate equivalent fractions and explain why multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number keeps the value." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.1)
- **Hook:** "Would you rather have 3⁄4 of a pizza or 9⁄12 of the same pizza?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: fraction strips and paper folding — fold a quarter strip into thirds to see 3⁄4 = 9⁄12. Pictorial: fraction wall and area models. Abstract: × the numerator and denominator by the same number (a form of ×1).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 2⁄3 = 4⁄6 but 2⁄3 ≠ 3⁄4 (adding 1 to top and bottom)?" → Equivalent fractions come from multiplying both parts by the same number (splitting every part equally); adding changes the size.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction wall and strips for halves, quarters and eighths / stretch: find a fraction equivalent to 3⁄5 whose numerator and denominator add up to 40 (15⁄25).
- **Materials:** fraction strips, fraction wall, paper for folding, coloured pencils.
- **Joy:** pizza-choice debate with paper pizzas cut into different slices.

### Lesson 40 — Simplifying fractions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can simplify fractions to their simplest form using the highest common factor." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 6.NS.4 link)
- **Hook:** "A football team won 18 of their 24 games. How could we say that more simply?"
- **Key activity:** Pictorial: area models regrouped into bigger parts. Abstract: divide numerator and denominator by common factors; do it in one step using the HCF (18⁄24 ÷ 6 = 3⁄4).
- **Check for understanding:** "Jin simplified 12⁄18 to 6⁄9. Is it in its simplest form? How do you know?" → No — 3 is still a common factor; 2⁄3 is simplest because the HCF of 2 and 3 is 1.
- **Differentiation:** support: fractions with small denominators using fraction strips to see the regrouping / stretch: simplify 84⁄126 (2⁄3) in as few steps as possible.
- **Materials:** fraction strips, square tiles for area models, factor lists.
- **Joy:** "Sports statistician" — simplify real win records of famous teams.

### Lesson 41 — Comparing and ordering fractions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare and order fractions using common denominators, common numerators or benchmarks." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 4.NF.2 extended)
- **Hook:** "Is 5⁄8 or 7⁄12 of a chocolate bar more?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: fraction strips side by side. Pictorial: number lines 0–1. Abstract: three strategies — common denominator (15⁄24 vs 14⁄24), common numerator (for 3⁄7 vs 3⁄8 — bigger pieces win), benchmark ½ (4⁄9 < ½ < 5⁄9).
- **Check for understanding:** "Which is bigger, 3⁄7 or 3⁄8? Explain without calculating a common denominator." → 3⁄7: same number of parts, but sevenths are bigger than eighths.
- **Differentiation:** support: compare using fraction strips only / stretch: order 2⁄3, 5⁄8, 7⁄10, 11⁄16 and explain the most efficient strategy for each pair.
- **Materials:** fraction strips, number lines 0–1, chocolate-bar grids (squared paper).
- **Joy:** "Fraction battle" card game — each player flips a fraction card; the larger fraction wins, with justification.

### Lesson 42 — Improper fractions and mixed numbers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can convert between improper fractions and mixed numbers and place them on a number line." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 4.NF.3)
- **Hook:** "A recipe needs 11⁄4 cups of flour. How many full cups is that?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: quarter pieces of fraction circles grouped into wholes (11 quarters = 2 wholes and 3 quarters). Pictorial: number line in quarters to 4. Abstract: 11⁄4 = 2¾ by division (11 ÷ 4 = 2 r 3); 3⅖ = 17⁄5.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does 11 ÷ 4 = 2 r 3 tell us that 11⁄4 = 2¾?" → 2 groups of 4 quarters make 2 wholes; 3 quarters remain.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction circles for halves, thirds, quarters / stretch: which is greater, 23⁄7 or 3⅓? (23⁄7 = 3 2⁄7 = 3 6⁄21 and 3⅓ = 3 7⁄21, so 3⅓ is greater.)
- **Materials:** fraction circles, number lines, measuring cups.
- **Joy:** bake-off planning — convert a recipe's improper fractions to real measuring cups.

### Lesson 43 — Adding and subtracting fractions with related denominators
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add and subtract fractions where one denominator is a multiple of the other." (UK NC Y5–6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.1)
- **Hook:** "Ana drinks 1⁄2 of a bottle and Ben drinks 3⁄8. How much is gone?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: fraction strips — swap the ½ strip for 4⁄8. Pictorial: bar models split into eighths. Abstract: convert to the common denominator, then add numerators only: 4⁄8 + 3⁄8 = 7⁄8; 5⁄6 − 1⁄3 = 5⁄6 − 2⁄6 = 3⁄6 = ½.
- **Check for understanding:** "Kai says 1⁄2 + 3⁄8 = 4⁄10. Use fraction strips to show why he is wrong." → Adding denominators changes the size of the parts; halves and eighths must be the same size first: 7⁄8.
- **Differentiation:** support: halves/quarters/eighths with strips / stretch: 7⁄12 + 5⁄6 − 1⁄4 (7⁄12 + 10⁄12 − 3⁄12 = 14⁄12 = 1⅙).
- **Materials:** fraction strips, bar-model templates, water bottles marked in eighths.
- **Joy:** "Water bottle challenge" — pour coloured water to check predictions.

### Lesson 44 — Adding and subtracting any fractions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add and subtract fractions with unrelated denominators by finding a common denominator." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.1)
- **Hook:** "A third of a garden is vegetables and a quarter is flowers. What fraction is left for the pond?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete/pictorial: fold paper into thirds one way and quarters the other to see twelfths. Abstract: common denominator via LCM: 1⁄3 + 1⁄4 = 4⁄12 + 3⁄12 = 7⁄12; left for pond 5⁄12.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 12 a good common denominator for thirds and quarters? Would 24 also work?" → 12 is the LCM of 3 and 4; 24 also works but gives larger numbers to simplify later.
- **Differentiation:** support: paper-folding for each calculation with denominators up to 12 / stretch: 2⁄5 + 3⁄7 (14⁄35 + 15⁄35 = 29⁄35) and invent a pair whose sum is exactly 1.
- **Materials:** squares of paper for folding, coloured pencils, fraction strips.
- **Joy:** design a garden plan on squared paper divided in given fractions.

### Lesson 45 — Adding mixed numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add mixed numbers, exchanging fractions greater than 1 into wholes." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.1)
- **Hook:** "A runner does 2¾ km before school and 1⅔ km after. How far altogether?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: fraction circles for wholes and parts. Pictorial: number line jumps. Abstract: add wholes (3), add fractions (9⁄12 + 8⁄12 = 17⁄12 = 1 5⁄12), total 4 5⁄12 km. Compare with converting to improper fractions.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the answer 4 5⁄12 and not 3 17⁄12?" → 17⁄12 is more than a whole; 12⁄12 is exchanged for 1 whole, leaving 5⁄12.
- **Differentiation:** support: mixed numbers with the same denominator first, using fraction circles / stretch: 3⅝ + 2⅚ + 1¼ (wholes 6; 15⁄24 + 20⁄24 + 6⁄24 = 41⁄24 = 1 17⁄24; total 7 17⁄24).

### Lesson 46 — Subtracting mixed numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can subtract mixed numbers, including when I need to exchange a whole." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.1)
- **Hook:** "There were 3¼ litres of juice. The class drank 1⅔ litres. How much is left?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: fraction circles — exchange one whole for 12⁄12. Pictorial: bar model. Abstract: two methods: exchange (3 3⁄12 → 2 15⁄12; minus 1 8⁄12 = 1 7⁄12) or improper fractions (39⁄12 − 20⁄12 = 19⁄12 = 1 7⁄12).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can't you just do 3 − 1 = 2 and 1⁄4 − 2⁄3 separately here?" → 1⁄4 is smaller than 2⁄3, so you need to exchange a whole first (or the fractional part would be negative).
- **Differentiation:** support: subtractions with no exchange and a common denominator given / stretch: find two mixed numbers with different denominators whose difference is exactly ⅚.

### Lesson 47 — Fraction problems with bar models

### Lesson 48 — Fractions as division
- **Duration:** 35 min

- **Hook:** "3 pizzas are shared equally by 4 friends. How much does each get?"

- **Joy:** real (or paper) pizza party — share and eat the evidence.

### Lesson 49 — Fractions on the number line and between fractions

## Unit 5 — Fractions II: multiplying and dividing

**Essential question:** What does it mean to multiply or divide by a fraction, and why can multiplying make something smaller?

**Key vocabulary:** of, product, unit fraction, non-unit fraction, area model, scaling, simplify, mixed number, divide, share, how many … in, reciprocal (stretch only)

### Lesson 53 — Fractions of amounts
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find a fraction of an amount and explain it with a bar model." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.4)
- **Hook:** "A game costs 40 dollars. It's reduced by 3⁄5. How much do you save?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: 40 counters shared into 5 equal groups; take 3 groups. Pictorial: bar model split into fifths (each 8). Abstract: ÷ by the denominator, × by the numerator: 3⁄5 of 40 = 24. Link to "fraction × whole number": 3⁄5 × 40.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 3⁄5 of 40 the same as 3 × (40 ÷ 5)?" → 40 ÷ 5 finds one fifth (8); three fifths is three of those (24).
- **Differentiation:** support: unit fractions of amounts with counters first / stretch: 5⁄8 of 360 (225) and 7⁄12 of 3.6 kg in grams (2,100 g).
- **Materials:** counters, bar-model templates, price tags, Cuisenaire rods.
- **Joy:** "Sale detective" — calculate savings on a mock shop's fraction-off signs.

### Lesson 54 — Finding the whole from a fraction
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find the whole amount when I know the value of a fraction of it." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.6)
- **Hook:** "2⁄5 of the class walk to school. That's 12 children. How many are in the class?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: 12 counters placed in 2 of 5 cups (6 each), then fill the other cups. Pictorial: bar model — 2 parts = 12, so 1 part = 6, 5 parts = 30. Abstract: practise "part known, whole unknown" and mixed problems.
- **Check for understanding:** "3⁄4 of a number is 45. Rosa says the number is 60. Show whether she is right." → 45 ÷ 3 = 15 per quarter; 4 × 15 = 60 — right.
- **Differentiation:** support: unit-fraction problems (1⁄4 of a number is 9) with cups and counters / stretch: "After spending 3⁄8 of her money, Lena has 45 left. How much did she start with?" (72).
- **Materials:** cups, counters, bar-model templates.
- **Joy:** "Reverse riddles" — pupils write "I'm thinking of a number…" fraction riddles for a partner.

### Lesson 55 — Multiplying unit fractions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply two unit fractions and explain the answer with paper folding." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.4)
- **Hook:** "What is half of a third of a chocolate bar?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: fold paper into thirds, shade one, then fold in half the other way — the double-shaded part is 1⁄6 of the sheet. Pictorial: area-model grids. Abstract: 1⁄2 × 1⁄3 = 1⁄6; notice numerator × numerator, denominator × denominator.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 1⁄2 × 1⁄3 smaller than both 1⁄2 and 1⁄3?" → You are taking only part of a part — half of a third must be less than a third.
- **Differentiation:** support: paper-folding for each calculation / stretch: 1⁄3 × 1⁄4 × 1⁄2 (1⁄24) — explain with a folded strip.
- **Materials:** paper squares, rulers, coloured pencils, chocolate-bar picture grids.
- **Joy:** fold and shade "fraction art" squares that become a class quilt.

### Lesson 56 — Multiplying any two fractions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply two fractions, simplifying my answer." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.4)
- **Hook:** "3⁄4 of a field is planted. Wheat covers 2⁄3 of the planted part. What fraction of the whole field is wheat?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete/pictorial: area model — shade 3⁄4 one way, 2⁄3 of that the other way; 6 of 12 parts double-shaded. Abstract: 2⁄3 × 3⁄4 = 6⁄12 = 1⁄2. Show simplifying before multiplying as a shortcut for stretch.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why don't we need a common denominator to multiply fractions?" → Multiplying finds a part of a part; the area model's rows and columns make the new denominator automatically.
- **Differentiation:** support: area-model grids drawn in advance / stretch: simplify first: 4⁄9 × 3⁄8 = 1⁄6, and explain why it works.
- **Materials:** squared paper, transparent overlays or coloured pencils, fraction strips.
- **Joy:** "Field planner" — design a farm on squared paper and calculate each crop's fraction.

### Lesson 57 — Multiplication as scaling
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can predict whether a product will be bigger or smaller than a factor by comparing the other factor with 1." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.5)
- **Hook:** "Is it possible to multiply 250 by something and get a smaller answer?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: stretch and shrink Cuisenaire trains or elastic bands to show ×2, ×1 and ×½. Pictorial: number lines showing 250 × 7⁄8, 250 × 1, 250 × 1¼. Abstract: sort products as "bigger than", "equal to", "smaller than" without calculating.
- **Check for understanding:** "Without calculating, is 7⁄8 × 250 more or less than 250? Why?" → Less, because 7⁄8 is less than 1, so it shrinks 250.
- **Differentiation:** support: sort using fraction strips alongside a 1-whole strip / stretch: order 5 × 3⁄4, 5 × 5⁄4, 5 × 1 and 3⁄4 × 5⁄4 without calculating, explaining each.
- **Materials:** elastic bands, Cuisenaire rods, number lines, sorting cards.
- **Joy:** "Shrink ray" — design a machine that shrinks objects by a fraction and calculate new sizes of classroom objects.

### Lesson 58 — Multiplying mixed numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply a mixed number by a whole number, and (stretch) by another mixed number." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.4, 5.NF.6)
- **Hook:** "A lemonade recipe needs 2¼ cups of sugar. How much sugar for 3 batches?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: fraction circles for 3 lots of 2¼. Pictorial: partition 2¼ × 3 = 2 × 3 + ¼ × 3 = 6 + ¾ = 6¾. Abstract: compare with improper fractions (9⁄4 × 3 = 27⁄4 = 6¾).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 2¼ × 3 not 6¼?" → The quarter must be multiplied by 3 as well as the 2: 6 + ¾.
- **Differentiation:** support: mixed number × 2 with fraction circles / stretch: 1½ × 2½ using an area model (2 + ½ + 1 + ¼ = 3¾).
- **Materials:** fraction circles, measuring cups, squared paper.
- **Joy:** scale a real lemonade recipe for the class and make it.

### Lesson 59 — Dividing a fraction by a whole number
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide a fraction by a whole number and explain it by sharing." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.7)
- **Hook:** "Half a pizza is left. Three friends share it. What fraction of the whole pizza does each get?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: cut a paper half-pizza into 3 equal slices — each is 1⁄6 of the whole. Pictorial: bar model. Abstract: 1⁄2 ÷ 3 = 1⁄6; 4⁄5 ÷ 2 = 2⁄5 (the numerator divides neatly); 3⁄4 ÷ 2 = 3⁄8 (split each quarter in two).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 3⁄4 ÷ 2 equal to 3⁄8 and not 3⁄2?" → Sharing makes each piece smaller; halving each quarter makes eighths, so 3 eighths. 3⁄2 is bigger than what we started with.
- **Differentiation:** support: unit fraction ÷ whole with paper strips / stretch: 2⁄3 ÷ 4 two ways (2⁄12 = 1⁄6, or split each third into 4) and explain both.
- **Materials:** paper circles and strips, scissors, bar-model templates.
- **Joy:** real (or paper) pizza sharing with fraction name tags on each slice.

### Lesson 60 — How many fit? Dividing a whole number by a unit fraction
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide a whole number by a unit fraction by asking 'how many … fit into …?'" (CCSS 5.NF.7 — bridge to Year 6)
- **Hook:** "How many quarter-hour episodes can you watch in 3 hours?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: 3 whole fraction strips covered with quarter strips — count 12. Pictorial: number line 0–3 marked in quarters. Abstract: 3 ÷ ¼ = 12; 5 ÷ ⅓ = 15; notice dividing by a unit fraction multiplies by its denominator.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 2 ÷ ⅓ bigger than 2?" → You are counting how many thirds fit into 2; each is small, so 6 fit.
- **Differentiation:** support: only halves and quarters with fraction strips / stretch: 3 ÷ ⅔ — how many two-thirds fit into 3? (4½) Explain with a number line.
- **Materials:** fraction strips, number lines, a clock face.
- **Joy:** "Movie marathon" — plan how many short films of different fractional lengths fit in an afternoon.

### Lesson 61 — Fraction problems with all four operations
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose the right operation for a fraction problem and solve it with a bar model." (UK NC Y6 fractions / CCSS 5.NF.6)
- **Hook:** "In a class of 30, 2⁄5 walk to school and 1⁄3 of the rest cycle. How many come another way?"
- **Key activity:** Model: walkers 12, rest 18, cyclists 6, others 12. Children sort problems by operation before solving; discuss the words "of", "share", "how many fit", "altogether".
- **Check for understanding:** "A ¾ m ribbon is cut into 3 equal pieces. Which calculation finds each length: ¾ × 3 or ¾ ÷ 3? Why?" → ¾ ÷ 3 = ¼ m; we are sharing the ribbon, so each piece is smaller.
- **Differentiation:** support: one-step problems with operation hints / stretch: write a problem for each operation using the same numbers (⅔ and 4).
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, fraction strips, ribbon and scissors.
- **Joy:** "Problem factory" — the class's invented problems become next week's warm-ups.

### Lesson 62 — Unit 5 mastery check
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show I have mastered multiplying and dividing with fractions." (UK NC Y6 fractions — assessment)
- **Hook:** "The fraction kitchen: scale recipes and share the results."
- **Key activity:** Independent mastery task, then teacher conferencing and targeted regrouping.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which is larger: ⅔ × 12 or 12 ÷ ⅔? Explain before calculating." → 12 ÷ ⅔ (18) is larger than ⅔ × 12 (8): dividing by a number less than 1 makes the answer bigger; multiplying by it makes it smaller.

- **Materials:** mastery task, fraction strips, squared paper.
- **Joy:** the class recipe book — each pupil contributes one scaled recipe.

## Unit 6 — Decimals: all four operations

**Essential question:** How do decimals extend place value, and how do we calculate with them as confidently as with whole numbers?

**Key vocabulary:** decimal point, tenth, hundredth, thousandth, decimal place, round, compare, order, exchange, product, quotient, recurring (stretch), convert, equivalent

### Lesson 63 — Decimal place value to three places
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read, write and partition decimals with up to three decimal places." (UK NC Y6 decimals / CCSS 5.NBT.3)
- **Hook:** "In a sprint final, the winner won by 0.008 seconds. How small is that?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: base-10 blocks re-labelled (the 1,000 cube as 1, flat as 0.1, rod as 0.01, unit as 0.001) and decimal place-value counters. Pictorial: place-value chart with tenths, hundredths, thousandths. Abstract: 3.452 = 3 + 0.4 + 0.05 + 0.002.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which is larger, 0.7 or 0.65? Explain using place value." → 0.7 (7 tenths = 0.70), which is more than 6 tenths and 5 hundredths.
- **Differentiation:** support: two decimal places with money (dollars and cents) / stretch: write the number 4 thousandths more than 2.999 (3.003) and explain the exchanges.
- **Materials:** base-10 blocks, decimal place-value counters, place-value chart, stopwatch.
- **Joy:** "Photo finish" — time pupils' reactions with a stopwatch to hundredths and compare.

### Lesson 64 — Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100 and 1,000
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply and divide decimals by 10, 100 and 1,000 by moving digits across the place-value chart." (UK NC Y6 decimals / CCSS 5.NBT.2)
- **Hook:** "Jun says to multiply by 10 you just add a zero. So 2.5 × 10 = 2.50. Is he right?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: a place-value slider — digits on a strip that moves while the decimal point stays fixed. Abstract: 3.45 × 100 = 345; 72 ÷ 1,000 = 0.072; link to metric conversions.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain why 'add a zero' fails for 2.5 × 10." → 2.50 equals 2.5; each digit must move one place left: 25.
- **Differentiation:** support: × and ÷ by 10 only with the slider / stretch: missing-number statements like 0.36 × ☐ = 360 and 4.2 ÷ ☐ = 0.042.
- **Materials:** place-value sliders (card strip in a chart), digit cards, metre stick.
- **Joy:** "The Shrinking Machine" — digits slide through a cardboard machine.

### Lesson 65 — Comparing, ordering and rounding decimals
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can order decimals with different numbers of places and round to the nearest whole, tenth or hundredth." (UK NC Y6 decimals / CCSS 5.NBT.3–4)
- **Hook:** Four long-jump distances: 4.5 m, 4.45 m, 4.405 m, 4.54 m. Who won gold?
- **Key activity:** Pictorial: number lines zooming from 4 to 5, then 4.4 to 4.5. Abstract: fill blank places with zeros to compare (4.500, 4.450, 4.405, 4.540); round 3.456 to 1 dp (3.5) and to the nearest whole (3).
- **Check for understanding:** "Mia says 0.45 > 0.5 because 45 > 5. Explain her mistake." → She compared digits as whole numbers; 0.5 = 0.50, which is 50 hundredths, larger than 45 hundredths.
- **Differentiation:** support: decimals to 2 places with a zoomed number line / stretch: a number rounds to 7.3 to 1 dp — what are the smallest and largest 2-dp numbers it could be? (7.25 and 7.34).
- **Materials:** number lines at three zoom levels, metre sticks, tape measures.
- **Joy:** class long-jump in the playground, measured to the centimetre and ordered.

### Lesson 66 — Adding and subtracting decimals
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add and subtract decimals with different numbers of decimal places." (UK NC Y6 decimals / CCSS 5.NBT.7)
- **Hook:** "A parcel weighs 4.5 kg. You remove a book weighing 1.26 kg. What's the new weight?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: decimal counters, exchanging a tenth for ten hundredths. Pictorial: place-value grids. Abstract: line up decimal points; use placeholder zeros: 4.50 − 1.26 = 3.24; 12 − 3.75 = 8.25.
- **Check for understanding:** "Tom lined up 4.5 and 1.26 by their right-hand digits. Explain why that is wrong." → He lined up digits, not place values; the decimal points must line up (4.50 − 1.26 = 3.24).
- **Differentiation:** support: money amounts with play money / stretch: missing-digit decimal subtractions and "make exactly 10 with three decimals of different lengths".
- **Materials:** decimal place-value counters, play money, kitchen scales.
- **Joy:** weigh real parcels and check calculated weights on the scales.

### Lesson 67 — Decimals and money around the world
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve money problems with decimals and explain how different currencies are written." (UK NC Y6 decimals / CCSS 5.NBT.7)
- **Hook:** "In Japan, everyday prices are whole numbers of yen. In Mexico there are pesos and centavos. Why do some currencies need decimals?"
- **Key activity:** Compare a shopping list in three currencies (e.g. euros and cents, Indian rupees, US dollars and cents). Add totals and calculate change using counting on and column methods; discuss that 100 cents = 1 dollar, so two decimal places.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we write 3 dollars and 5 cents as 3.05, not 3.5?" → 5 cents is 5 hundredths of a dollar; 3.5 would be 3 dollars 50 cents.
- **Differentiation:** support: whole-number and 1-dp money with coins / stretch: complete a shop receipt with a 7.5% sales tax, rounded to the nearest cent.
- **Materials:** play money in several currencies (or printed notes/coins), shop price cards, receipts.
- **Joy:** "World market" — role-play shopkeepers and customers from different countries.

### Lesson 68 — Multiplying decimals by whole numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply a decimal by a whole number and check with an estimate." (UK NC Y6 decimals / CCSS 5.NBT.7)
- **Hook:** "A bottle holds 1.75 litres. How much do 6 bottles hold?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: decimal counters for 0.3 × 4 (12 tenths = 1.2). Pictorial: bar models. Abstract: 2.45 × 3 = 7.35 (use 245 × 3 = 735, then ÷ 100); estimate first (≈ 2.5 × 3 = 7.5).
- **Check for understanding:** "Leila says 0.6 × 5 = 0.30. Explain her error." → 6 tenths × 5 = 30 tenths = 3.0, not 30 hundredths.
- **Differentiation:** support: one-decimal-place numbers with counters / stretch: 1.75 × 6 two ways (10.5) and explain why both work.
- **Materials:** decimal place-value counters, measuring jugs, bottles.
- **Joy:** fill a bucket with measured bottles to verify the answer.

### Lesson 69 — Multiplying decimals by decimals
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply tenths by tenths using an area model and explain the size of the product." (CCSS 5.NBT.7)
- **Hook:** "A garden bed is 0.3 m by 0.2 m. What is its area?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete/pictorial: on a hundred square (1 m by 1 m), shade 3 columns and 2 rows — 6 small squares overlap = 0.06 m². Abstract: 1.2 × 0.4 = 0.48 (12 × 4 = 48; tenths × tenths = hundredths).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 0.3 × 0.2 smaller than both 0.3 and 0.2?" → We are taking 0.2 (less than 1) of 0.3, so the product is smaller.
- **Differentiation:** support: area models with tenths × whole numbers first / stretch: 2.5 × 1.4 via an area model (3.5).
- **Materials:** hundred squares, coloured pencils, metre sticks, masking tape (to mark a 1 m² square on the floor).
- **Joy:** mark a real 1 m² square on the floor and tape out the 0.3 m × 0.2 m bed inside it.

### Lesson 70 — Dividing decimals by whole numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide a decimal by a whole number using short division." (UK NC Y6 decimals / CCSS 5.NBT.7)
- **Hook:** "7.2 metres of rope is cut into 3 equal pieces. How long is each?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: share decimal counters — 7 ones and 2 tenths into 3 groups; exchange the leftover 1 one for 10 tenths. Pictorial: grouping in a chart. Abstract: short division keeping the decimal point aligned: 7.2 ÷ 3 = 2.4; 1.35 ÷ 5 = 0.27; 8.4 ÷ 12 = 0.7.
- **Check for understanding:** "In 1.35 ÷ 5, why does the answer start with 0.?" → 1 one can't be shared into 5 whole ones, so we exchange it for 10 tenths: 13 tenths ÷ 5 = 2 tenths remainder 3 tenths, and so on.
- **Differentiation:** support: dividing money amounts with coins / stretch: 9.36 ÷ 12 (0.78).
- **Materials:** decimal place-value counters, rope and metre stick, squared paper.
- **Joy:** cut a real rope into equal lengths and measure to check.

### Lesson 71 — Division with decimal answers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can continue a division past the decimal point to give an exact decimal answer." (UK NC Y6 decimals / CCSS 5.NBT.7)
- **Hook:** "17 dollars shared between 4 friends. Can we share it exactly?"
- **Key activity:** Model with play money: 17 ÷ 4 = 4 r 1; exchange the dollar for 100 cents: 25 c each → 4.25. Abstract: write 17 as 17.00 and continue short division; 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can we write 17 as 17.00 without changing its value?" → Adding zero tenths and zero hundredths adds nothing; it just lets us keep dividing.
- **Differentiation:** support: divide by 2, 4 or 5 with play money / stretch: 10 ÷ 3 — what happens? (3.333… recurring.) Why does it never end?
- **Materials:** play money, squared paper, calculators (for checking the recurring decimal).
- **Joy:** "Never-ending division" — race the calculator to predict the next digit of 1 ÷ 7.

### Lesson 72 — Converting fractions to decimals
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can convert fractions to decimals by using equivalent fractions or division." (UK NC Y6 decimals / CCSS 5.NF.3, 6.NS.3 preview)
- **Hook:** "Which is bigger: ⅜ or 0.4?"

### Lesson 73 — Converting decimals to fractions

### Lesson 74 — Multi-step decimal problems

## Unit 7 — Percentages and FDP equivalence

**Essential question:** Why do we have three ways — fractions, decimals and percentages — to say the same amount, and when is each most useful?

**Key vocabulary:** per cent, percentage, hundredth, equivalent, convert, discount, increase, decrease, sale price, original price, proportion, out of, of

### Lesson 77 — Per cent means "out of one hundred"
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what a percentage means and show it on a hundred grid, a bar and a number line." (UK NC Y6 percentages / CCSS 6.RP.3c)
- **Hook:** "Your tablet battery says 37%. What does that actually mean?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: 100-bead strings; move 37 beads. Pictorial: hundred grids and 0–100% bars. Abstract: percentages as hundredths: 37% = 37⁄100 = 0.37; the whole is always 100%.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 50% the same as ½ whatever the whole is?" → 50 out of every 100 is half of every 100, so half of the whole.
- **Differentiation:** support: shade hundred grids with multiples of 10% / stretch: can a percentage be more than 100%? Give a real example (a price rising to 150% of the original).
- **Materials:** bead strings of 100, hundred grids, percentage bars.
- **Joy:** "Battery race" — pupils estimate percentages of full containers (water bottles, jars), then measure.

### Lesson 78 — Key equivalences: fractions, decimals and percentages
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall and explain the key fraction, decimal and percentage equivalents." (UK NC Y6 FDP / CCSS 6.RP.3c)
- **Hook:** "Which is the best deal: ¼ off, 0.2 off, or 30% off?"
- **Key activity:** Pictorial: a triple number line (fractions above, decimals and percentages below). Learn and derive: ½ = 0.5 = 50%, ¼ = 0.25 = 25%, ¾ = 0.75 = 75%, ⅕ = 0.2 = 20%, ⅒ = 0.1 = 10%, ⅛ = 0.125 = 12.5%, ⅓ = 0.333… ≈ 33.3%.
- **Check for understanding:** "Nico says ⅓ = 0.3 = 30%. What's wrong?" → ⅓ = 0.333… (recurring) ≈ 33.3%; 0.3 is only 3 tenths, less than a third.
- **Differentiation:** support: halves, quarters and tenths with a hundred grid / stretch: derive ⅝, 3⁄20 and 7⁄25 as decimals and percentages (0.625 = 62.5%; 0.15 = 15%; 0.28 = 28%).
- **Materials:** triple number lines, hundred grids, FDP matching cards.
- **Joy:** "FDP dominoes" — a class domino chain linking equivalent forms.

### Lesson 79 — Converting any fraction to a percentage
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can convert fractions to percentages using equivalent fractions or division." (UK NC Y6 FDP / CCSS 6.RP.3c)
- **Hook:** "You scored 18 out of 25 on a quiz and your friend scored 34 out of 50. Who did better?"
- **Key activity:** Scale to hundredths (18⁄25 = 72⁄100 = 72%; 34⁄50 = 68%). When the denominator doesn't scale easily, divide (3⁄8 = 0.375 = 37.5%).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it easier to compare these scores as percentages?" → Percentages rescale every score to 'out of 100', so different totals become comparable.
- **Differentiation:** support: denominators 2, 4, 5, 10, 20, 25, 50 with hundred grids / stretch: 7⁄40 (17.5%) and 11⁄16 (68.75%).
- **Materials:** hundred grids, calculators (for divisions in stretch), score cards.
- **Joy:** "Quiz-show judges" — rank contestants who took different-length quizzes.

### Lesson 80 — Ordering fractions, decimals and percentages
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can order a mixed set of fractions, decimals and percentages by converting to one form." (UK NC Y6 FDP / CCSS 6.RP.3c)
- **Hook:** "Four juices: 45% fruit, 0.4 fruit, ⅜ fruit, and 2⁄5 fruit. Which is fruitiest?"
- **Key activity:** Convert all to percentages (45%, 40%, 37.5%, 40%) and order; place on a 0–100% line; discuss when decimals are easier.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are 0.4 and 2⁄5 at exactly the same point?" → Both equal 40%, i.e. 40 hundredths.
- **Differentiation:** support: sets of three with benchmark values / stretch: sets including values above 1 (1.2, 115%, 6⁄5).
- **Materials:** percentage number lines, juice cartons with labels, conversion cards.
- **Joy:** blind taste-test of juices after ranking their fruit content.

### Lesson 81 — Percentages of amounts: 50%, 25%, 10% and 1%
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find 50%, 25%, 10% and 1% of an amount and explain why each method works." (UK NC Y6 percentages / CCSS 6.RP.3c)
- **Hook:** "A 250-dollar bike is 10% off. How much is saved?"
- **Key activity:** Pictorial: bar models divided into 10 or 100 equal parts. Abstract: 50% = ÷2; 25% = ÷4; 10% = ÷10; 1% = ÷100. 10% of 250 = 25; 1% of 250 = 2.5.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does dividing by 10 find 10%?" → 10% is 10⁄100 = ⅒, one of ten equal parts.
- **Differentiation:** support: whole-hundred amounts with bar models / stretch: find 1% of 3.6 kg (36 g) and 10% of 0.8 (0.08).
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, place-value sliders, price tags.
- **Joy:** "Discount dash" — price tags on classroom items; race to calculate sale prices.

### Lesson 82 — Any percentage of an amount
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate any whole-number percentage of an amount by building from 10% and 1%." (UK NC Y6 percentages / CCSS 6.RP.3c)
- **Hook:** "How could you find 35% of 60 in your head?"
- **Key activity:** Bar model: 10% = 6, so 30% = 18, 5% = 3, 35% = 21. Build 17% of 300 from 10% + 5% + 2% (30 + 15 + 6 = 51). Compare with multiplying by the decimal (0.35 × 60).
- **Check for understanding:** "Find 15% of 80 two different ways." → 10% (8) + 5% (4) = 12; or 0.15 × 80 = 12.
- **Differentiation:** support: multiples of 5% only, with a bar model / stretch: 12.5% of 64 (8) and 99% of 450 (445.5) with clever methods.
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, hundred grids, mini-whiteboards.
- **Joy:** "Percentage mind-reader" — pupils challenge the teacher (and each other) to mental percentages.

### Lesson 83 — Discounts and sale prices
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate a sale price after a percentage discount and compare offers." (UK NC Y6 percentages / CCSS 7.RP.3 preview)
- **Hook:** "Shop A: 25% off an 80-dollar coat. Shop B: 15 dollars off a 75-dollar coat. Where would you buy?"
- **Key activity:** Two methods: find the discount then subtract (25% of 80 = 20; 80 − 20 = 60); or find the remaining percentage (75% of 80 = 60). Compare: Shop B = 60 too — the same price.
- **Check for understanding:** "A 45-dollar game is 20% off. Explain two ways to find the sale price." → 20% = 9, 45 − 9 = 36; or 80% of 45 = 36.
- **Differentiation:** support: 10%, 25% and 50% discounts with bar models / stretch: is 20% off then a further 10% off the same as 30% off? (No: 100 → 80 → 72, which is 28% off.)
- **Materials:** catalogue pages, price tags, bar-model templates, calculators (checking).
- **Joy:** "Smart shopper" — spend a pretend budget across sale offers to get the most items.

### Lesson 84 — Percentage increase in real life
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate a new amount after a percentage increase, such as tax or growth." (UK NC Y6 percentages / CCSS 7.RP.3 preview)
- **Hook:** "A plant is 60 cm tall and grows 10% in a month. How tall is it now?"
- **Key activity:** Bar model: the original 100% plus an extra 10% bar: 60 + 6 = 66 cm. Contexts: sales tax (8% on 25 = 2; total 27), a restaurant tip of 15%, population growth.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'increase by 10%' the same as finding 110%?" → The new amount is the original 100% plus 10% more.
- **Differentiation:** support: 10% and 50% increases with a bar model / stretch: a price goes up 10% then down 10% — is it back to the start? (No: 100 → 110 → 99.)
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, plant growth chart, receipts.
- **Joy:** "Tip calculator" — pupils design a laminated tip chart for a pretend restaurant.

### Lesson 85 — Expressing one amount as a percentage of another
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write one number as a percentage of another." (UK NC Y6 percentages / CCSS 6.RP.3c)
- **Hook:** "13 of the 20 children in our survey chose football. What percentage is that?"

- **Check for understanding:** "Is 18 out of 30 more or less than 50%? How much exactly?" → More: 18⁄30 = 3⁄5 = 60%.
- **Differentiation:** support: totals of 10, 20, 50, 100 / stretch: totals such as 40, 80 and 120 requiring simplifying first (e.g. 42 out of 120 = 35%).
- **Materials:** class survey data, hundred grids, calculators (checking).
- **Joy:** survey the class on a favourite topic and present the results as percentages.

### Lesson 86 — Finding the whole from a percentage

## Unit 8 — Ratio and proportion

**Essential question:** How do ratios help us compare, share, scale and make things "the same, only bigger or smaller"?

**Key vocabulary:** ratio, for every, part, whole, proportion, equivalent ratio, simplest form, ratio table, scale factor, enlargement, scale, map scale, unit rate, unitary method, similar, direct proportion

### Lesson 89 — What is a ratio?
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe a relationship between two quantities using 'for every' language and ratio notation." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 6.RP.1)
- **Hook:** "For every 3 red beads there are 2 blue beads on this necklace. If I make it longer, what stays the same?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: build repeating patterns with multilink cubes (3 red : 2 blue). Pictorial: draw the pattern in groups. Abstract: write 3 : 2 and read it aloud; order matters (3 : 2 ≠ 2 : 3).
- **Check for understanding:** "A tower has 12 red and 8 blue cubes built in the 3 : 2 pattern. Why is the ratio still 3 : 2?" → It's 4 groups of (3 red, 2 blue); each group keeps the same 'for every' relationship.
- **Differentiation:** support: build ratios with cubes up to 5 groups / stretch: write a ratio of three quantities (e.g. a recipe 2 : 3 : 1) and describe it.
- **Materials:** multilink cubes, beads and string, pattern cards.
- **Joy:** make a beaded bracelet in a chosen ratio pattern.

### Lesson 90 — Ratio and fractions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the link between a ratio and the fractions of the whole." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 6.RP.1)
- **Hook:** "Boys to girls in a class is 4 : 5. Is ⅘ of the class boys?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: 4 blue and 5 yellow counters make 9 parts. Pictorial: bar model with 9 equal parts. Abstract: boys = 4⁄9, girls = 5⁄9; a ratio compares part to part, a fraction compares part to whole.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain why the fraction of girls is 5⁄9, not 5⁄4." → There are 9 parts altogether; a fraction compares a part to the whole, not to the other part.
- **Differentiation:** support: ratios with small totals and counters / stretch: a bag has red : blue = 2 : 7. After adding 3 red beads, ⅓ of the beads are red. How many beads were there at first? (18: 4 red, 14 blue.)
- **Materials:** two-colour counters, bar-model templates, class data.
- **Joy:** "Class census" — ratio and fraction facts about the class (e.g. wears glasses : doesn't).

### Lesson 91 — Equivalent ratios and simplest form
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find equivalent ratios and simplify a ratio using common factors." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 6.RP.3a)
- **Hook:** "Two paint mixes: 6 yellow : 4 blue, and 3 yellow : 2 blue. Will the greens match?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: mix drops of food colouring in water (or model with cubes). Pictorial: group the cubes. Abstract: divide both parts by the HCF: 6 : 4 = 3 : 2; 12 : 18 = 2 : 3.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 2 : 3 equivalent to 4 : 5? Explain." → No: adding 2 to each part changes the relationship; equivalent ratios come from multiplying or dividing both parts by the same number.
- **Differentiation:** support: simplify with cubes, grouping physically / stretch: simplify 45 : 60 : 75 (3 : 4 : 5).
- **Materials:** multilink cubes, food colouring, water, pipettes, clear cups.
- **Joy:** colour-mixing lab — test whether "equivalent" mixes really give the same shade.

### Lesson 92 — Ratio tables and double number lines
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use ratio tables and double number lines to find equivalent ratios." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 6.RP.3a)
- **Hook:** "A lemonade recipe uses 2 lemons for every 5 cups of water. How many lemons for 30 cups?"
- **Key activity:** Pictorial: double number line (lemons above, cups below). Abstract: ratio table with rows ×2, ×3, ×6: 2 : 5 → 12 : 30. Show adding rows (4 : 10 + 2 : 5 = 6 : 15).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can you add two rows of a ratio table but not add the same number to both parts?" → Adding rows combines two batches of the same recipe; adding the same number to both parts changes the recipe.
- **Differentiation:** support: tables with some values filled in / stretch: find the lemons needed for 17.5 cups (7).
- **Materials:** ratio-table templates, double number lines, lemons and jugs.
- **Joy:** make real lemonade using the class ratio table.

### Lesson 93 — Ratio problems: one part known
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve ratio problems when I know the size of one part." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 6.RP.3)
- **Hook:** "In a fruit bowl, apples : oranges = 2 : 3. There are 12 apples. How many oranges?"
- **Key activity:** Bar model: 2 bars = 12, so 1 bar = 6; oranges = 3 bars = 18; total 30. Practise with different known quantities (given the total, given one part).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we find the value of one bar first?" → Each bar is one 'part'; once we know one part we can build any other quantity.
- **Differentiation:** support: problems with the bar model drawn / stretch: 3-part ratios (flour : sugar : butter = 4 : 2 : 3 with 300 g butter → 400 g flour, 200 g sugar).
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, Cuisenaire rods, fruit.
- **Joy:** "Fruit-stall owner" — stock your stall in a given ratio and calculate orders.

### Lesson 94 — Sharing in a ratio
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can share an amount in a given ratio." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 6.RP.3)
- **Hook:** "Two sisters share 40 dollars in the ratio 3 : 5 because one worked longer. How much does each get?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: share 40 counters in rounds of 3 and 5. Pictorial: bar with 8 parts; 1 part = 5; shares 15 and 25. Abstract: total parts → value of one part → each share. Also 56 in 2 : 5 → 16 and 40.
- **Check for understanding:** "Kofi shares 40 in 3 : 5 and gets 13⅓ and 26⅔. What did he do?" → He divided by 3 instead of by the total number of parts (8).
- **Differentiation:** support: share with counters in rounds / stretch: share 150 in 2 : 3 : 5 (30, 45, 75) and explain.
- **Materials:** counters, play money, bar-model templates.
- **Joy:** "Treasure split" — divide a pirate treasure (chocolate coins) by contribution ratios.

### Lesson 95 — Ratio problems with a difference
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve ratio problems when I know the difference between the parts." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 6.RP.3)
- **Hook:** "Ali and Ben collect stickers in the ratio 5 : 2. Ali has 12 more than Ben. How many do they each have?"
- **Key activity:** Bar model: Ali 5 bars, Ben 2; the difference is 3 bars = 12, so 1 bar = 4; Ali 20, Ben 8. Mixed practice: total known, one part known, difference known — which is which?
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the difference 3 bars here?" → Ali has 5 bars and Ben 2; the extra 3 bars are what make Ali's amount bigger.
- **Differentiation:** support: difference problems with bars pre-drawn / stretch: "The ratio of cats to dogs is 3 : 7. There are 28 more dogs than cats. How many animals?" (70).
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, Cuisenaire rods, sticker sheets.
- **Joy:** "Problem-type sort" — a relay game identifying the type before solving.

### Lesson 96 — Scale factor and enlargement
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can enlarge a shape by a scale factor and find a scale factor from two similar shapes." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 7.G.1 preview)
- **Hook:** "How do you make a giant version of a drawing without it looking stretched?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: build a small shape with square tiles and enlarge it by ×2 and ×3. Pictorial: squared-paper enlargements (a 2 × 3 rectangle → 6 × 9 with scale factor 3). Abstract: every length is multiplied; angles are unchanged.
- **Check for understanding:** "A rectangle 2 cm × 3 cm is changed to 4 cm × 5 cm. Is that an enlargement? Why?" → No: 2 → 4 is ×2 but 3 → 5 is not ×2; all lengths must be multiplied by the same scale factor.
- **Differentiation:** support: enlarging simple rectilinear shapes on squared paper by ×2 / stretch: what happens to the area when lengths are ×3? (×9) — explain with tiles.
- **Materials:** square tiles, squared paper, rulers, a small picture to enlarge.
- **Joy:** enlarge a cartoon character by the grid method to make a poster.

### Lesson 97 — Scale drawings and maps
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a scale to calculate real distances from a map or plan." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 7.G.1 preview)
- **Hook:** "On this town map, the walk from the station to the museum is 6 cm. Can we get there in 10 minutes?"
- **Key activity:** Plans: 1 cm : 5 m — a 4 cm wall is 20 m. Maps: 1 : 50,000 means 1 cm on the map is 50,000 cm = 500 m in real life. Measure routes on a real local map.
- **Check for understanding:** "On a 1 : 50,000 map, why is 1 cm equal to 500 m?" → 50,000 cm ÷ 100 = 500 m.
- **Differentiation:** support: simple scales (1 cm : 1 m) on a classroom plan / stretch: draw the classroom to a scale of 1 : 50 and justify the choice.
- **Materials:** local map with a ratio scale, rulers, string (for curved routes), trundle wheel.
- **Joy:** plan a real walking route on the map, then walk it and compare.

### Lesson 98 — Direct proportion and recipes
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use the unitary method to scale quantities in direct proportion." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 6.RP.3)
- **Hook:** "This pancake recipe serves 4. We need to feed 6. How much flour?"
- **Key activity:** Unitary method: 300 g for 4 → 75 g for 1 → 450 g for 6. Ratio-table alternative (×1.5). Scale a whole recipe from a real cookbook.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might we find the amount for 1 person first?" → From 1 we can reach any number of people; there is no easy whole-number jump from 4 to 6 without it.
- **Differentiation:** support: doubling and halving recipes / stretch: scale a recipe for 4 to serve 10 when eggs can't be split — what's a sensible decision?
- **Materials:** real recipe cards (e.g. from different countries), kitchen scales, measuring jugs.
- **Joy:** make pancakes (or another simple recipe) scaled for the class.

### Lesson 99 — Unit rates and best value
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate unit rates to compare value and speed." (CCSS 6.RP.2–3b)
- **Hook:** "Pack A: 6 pens for 4.20. Pack B: 10 pens for 6.50. Which is better value?"
- **Key activity:** Find the price per pen (0.70 vs 0.65 → Pack B). Other unit rates: km per hour, words per minute, price per 100 g.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the bigger pack not always better value?" → Only the cost per item tells you; a bigger pack could have a higher unit price.
- **Differentiation:** support: whole-number unit prices / stretch: compare 750 g for 3.60 with 1.2 kg for 5.40 (0.48 per 100 g vs 0.45 per 100 g).
- **Materials:** supermarket packaging with prices, calculators (checking), stopwatches for words-per-minute.
- **Joy:** "Supermarket sweep" — find the best-value items from real packaging.

### Lesson 100 — Similar shapes and missing lengths
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find missing lengths in similar shapes using a scale factor." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 7.G.1 preview)
- **Hook:** "How tall is the school flagpole? We can't climb it — but we can measure its shadow."
- **Key activity:** Similar triangles: sides 4 and 6; a similar triangle has 10 in place of 4 (×2.5), so the other side is 15. Outdoors: a 1 m stick's shadow and the flagpole's shadow give the height by the same scale factor.
- **Check for understanding:** "A 1 m stick casts a 0.5 m shadow. The flagpole's shadow is 4 m. How tall is the flagpole? Explain." → Every height is 2 × its shadow at the same moment, so 8 m.
- **Differentiation:** support: whole-number scale factors on squared paper / stretch: find the scale factor when it is a fraction (6 cm → 4 cm is ×⅔).
- **Materials:** metre stick, tape measure, chalk, squared paper, a sunny day (or a torch and toys indoors).
- **Joy:** measure something too tall to reach using only shadows — as Thales is said to have done with the Great Pyramid.

### Lesson 101 — Proportional or not?
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can decide whether a situation is in direct proportion and explain why." (UK NC Y6 ratio / CCSS 7.RP.2 preview)
- **Hook:** "It takes 3 minutes to boil one egg. How long to boil 3 eggs in the same pan?"
- **Key activity:** Sort situations: proportional (cost of apples by weight), not proportional (boiling eggs; age and height; a taxi with a fixed charge). Test with a table: does doubling one quantity double the other?
- **Check for understanding:** "A taxi charges 3 plus 2 per km. Is cost proportional to distance? How can you tell?" → No: 1 km costs 5 but 2 km costs 7, not 10 — doubling distance doesn't double cost.
- **Differentiation:** support: sort with tables already completed / stretch: invent an inverse-proportion situation (more workers, less time) and explain the difference.
- **Materials:** situation cards, table templates, eggs and a pan (optional demonstration).
- **Joy:** "Trick question tournament" — pupils write proportion trick questions for the class.

### Lesson 102 — Unit 8 mastery check
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show I have mastered ratio, scale and proportion." (UK NC Y6 ratio — assessment)
- **Hook:** "You are the architect: design a small park using ratio and scale."
- **Key activity:** Independent mastery task (ratio problems, map scale, recipe scaling, unit price), then conferencing and targeted regrouping.
- **Check for understanding:** "On a 1 : 25,000 map, two villages are 4 cm apart. How far apart are they in real life?" → 4 × 25,000 = 100,000 cm = 1 km.
- **Differentiation:** support: ratio tables and bar-model templates / stretch: design a scale plan of the park that fits on A4 and justify the scale.
- **Materials:** mastery task, squared paper, rulers, ratio tables.
- **Joy:** park designs are displayed as a "city planning exhibition".

**End-of-unit check:** (1) Simplify 15 : 25 (3 : 5). (2) Share 72 in the ratio 5 : 3 (45 and 27). (3) 250 g of rice serves 5 — how much for 8? (400 g). ## Unit 9 — Algebra

**Essential question:** How can letters help us describe patterns, write rules and find unknown numbers?

**Key vocabulary:** sequence, term, term-to-term rule, position-to-term rule, nth term, variable, unknown, expression, formula, substitute, equation, solve, balance, inverse operation, generalise

### Lesson 103 — Sequences and term-to-term rules
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe and continue sequences, including those with fractions, decimals and negative numbers." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 5.OA.3)
- **Hook:** "5, 3, 1, −1, … What comes next, and will the sequence ever reach −100?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: build growing and shrinking patterns with cubes. Pictorial: number lines with jumps. Abstract: term-to-term rules (−2; +0.25; ×2) and deciding whether a number belongs in the sequence.
- **Check for understanding:** "Will −100 appear in 5, 3, 1, −1, …? Explain." → No: every term is odd (it starts odd and changes by 2), and −100 is even.
- **Differentiation:** support: whole-number sequences with cubes / stretch: create two different sequences that share exactly one term.
- **Materials:** multilink cubes, number lines −20 to 20, sequence cards.
- **Joy:** "Sequence secret agents" — encode a message as a sequence for others to crack.

### Lesson 104 — Position-to-term rules: matchstick patterns
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find a rule linking a term's position to its value and use it to find any term." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 5.OA.3, 6.EE.2)
- **Hook:** "How many sticks would you need to make 100 squares in a row?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: build 1, 2, 3 squares in a row with lolly sticks (4, 7, 10). Pictorial: a table of position and sticks. Abstract: the rule "3 × position + 1", written 3n + 1; the 100th term is 301.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it 3n + 1 and not n + 3?" → Each new square adds 3 sticks (so 3 × n), plus the one extra stick that starts the row.
- **Differentiation:** support: find the 10th term by extending the table with sticks / stretch: triangles in a row (3, 5, 7, … → 2n + 1) and a two-row pattern of squares.
- **Materials:** lolly sticks, squared paper, table templates.
- **Joy:** build the biggest pattern the class's sticks allow and predict the total before counting.

### Lesson 105 — Function machines and unknowns
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use function machines forwards and backwards to find inputs and outputs." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 6.EE.2)
- **Hook:** "A mystery machine turned 4 into 14 and 6 into 20. What does it do?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: a cardboard function machine with cards fed in. Pictorial: two-step machines drawn as boxes. Abstract: ×3 then +2; reverse with inverse operations (output 20 → 18 → 6).
- **Check for understanding:** "The machine is '× 3 then + 2'. The output is 29. What was the input? Explain the order of your steps." → 9: undo the last step first (29 − 2 = 27), then ÷ 3.
- **Differentiation:** support: one-step machines / stretch: find two different two-step machines that both turn 4 into 14.
- **Materials:** cardboard function machine, input/output cards, mini-whiteboards.
- **Joy:** "Guess my machine" — pairs design secret machines and challenge others.

### Lesson 106 — Writing expressions with letters
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write algebraic expressions for situations and use algebraic conventions." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 6.EE.2a)
- **Hook:** "I have n sweets. My friend has 5 more than me. My sister has twice as many as me. Can we write that without knowing n?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: cups (unknown number) and counters; algebra tiles. Abstract: n + 5, 2n, n − 3, n⁄4, 3n + 1; conventions: 3n means 3 × n; n × 3 is written 3n.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 3n the same as n + 3? Test with n = 5." → No: 3n = 15 but n + 3 = 8; 3n is three lots of n.
- **Differentiation:** support: expressions modelled with cups and counters before writing / stretch: write expressions with two variables (the cost in cents of a apples at 30 c and b bananas at 25 c: 30a + 25b).
- **Materials:** cups, counters, algebra tiles, mini-whiteboards.
- **Joy:** "Algebra charades" — act out an expression for the class to write.

### Lesson 107 — Substitution
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can substitute values into expressions, respecting the order of operations." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 6.EE.2c)
- **Hook:** "a = 4 and b = 7. Which is bigger: 3a + 2 or 2(a + b)?"
- **Key activity:** Replace letters with numbers carefully (3a + 2 = 3 × 4 + 2 = 14; 2(a + b) = 22; a² + b = 23). Game: each letter of the alphabet has a value — find the value of your name.
- **Check for understanding:** "If a = 4, Ellie says 3a = 34. Explain her mistake." → 3a means 3 × a = 12, not the digits 3 and 4 side by side.
- **Differentiation:** support: one-operation expressions with values written on counters / stretch: find values of a and b that make 3a + 2 = 2(a + b) true (e.g. a = 2, b = 2; a = 4, b = 3).
- **Materials:** letter-value cards, counters, mini-whiteboards.
- **Joy:** "Worth of a word" — find the most "valuable" word in the class dictionary.

### Lesson 108 — Using formulae
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a formula written in words or letters to calculate an answer." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 6.EE.2c)
- **Hook:** "A taxi charges 3 to start plus 2 per km: C = 3 + 2m. How much for an 8 km ride?"
- **Key activity:** Formulae from everyday life: P = 2(l + w) for a rectangle (l = 7, w = 3 → 20); taxi C = 3 + 2m (m = 8 → 19); a quick rule to estimate °F from °C (F ≈ 2C + 30) — discuss why it is only approximate.
- **Check for understanding:** "Using P = 2(l + w), why do we add l and w before multiplying?" → The brackets say so: the perimeter is two lots of (length + width).
- **Differentiation:** support: word formulae before letter formulae / stretch: compare F ≈ 2C + 30 with the exact rule F = 1.8C + 32 at 10 °C (50 and 50) and 30 °C (90 and 86).
- **Materials:** formula cards, rectangles on squared paper, thermometers with both scales.
- **Joy:** test the "quick weather formula" against a real thermometer.

### Lesson 109 — Writing formulae from contexts
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a formula to describe a real situation and use it." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 6.EE.2a, 6.EE.9)
- **Hook:** "Cinema tickets cost 8 each plus a booking fee of 5 per order. Write a rule for any number of tickets."
- **Key activity:** From tables to formulae: C = 8t + 5 (t = 4 → 37). Other contexts: phone plans, a border of tiles around a garden, paper chains.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it 8t + 5 and not 13t?" → The fee is charged once per order, not once per ticket.
- **Differentiation:** support: tables to complete before writing the formula / stretch: compare two phone plans with formulae and find when they cost the same.
- **Materials:** context cards, table templates, square tiles for the border problem.
- **Joy:** "Business planner" — invent a lemonade stall and write the formula for its profit.

### Lesson 110 — One-step equations with the balance model
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve one-step equations by keeping both sides balanced." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 6.EE.5, 6.EE.7)
- **Hook:** "A bag with a mystery number of marbles plus 7 loose marbles balances 15 marbles. How many are in the bag?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: a real balance scale with a closed bag and marbles (or cups and counters). Pictorial: balance diagrams. Abstract: x + 7 = 15 → x = 8; 4y = 36 → y = 9; do the same to both sides.
- **Check for understanding:** "To solve x + 7 = 15, why do we subtract 7 from both sides, not just one?" → The two sides must stay equal (balanced); changing one side only would tip the scale.
- **Differentiation:** support: equations modelled physically with cups and counters / stretch: x ÷ 4 = 2.5 and 12 = 20 − x.
- **Materials:** balance scales, marbles or cubes, opaque bags or cups, algebra tiles.
- **Joy:** "Mystery bag" — pupils prepare bags for classmates to solve with the balance.

### Lesson 111 — Two-step equations
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve two-step equations and check my solution by substitution." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 6.EE.7 extended)
- **Hook:** "Three identical bags plus 4 marbles balance 19 marbles. How many in each bag?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: cups and counters — remove 4 from both sides, then share 15 among 3 cups. Abstract: 3x + 4 = 19 → 3x = 15 → x = 5; 2y − 3 = 11 → y = 7. Always check by substituting.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we deal with the + 4 before dividing by 3?" → To get the 3 bags alone first; we undo operations in reverse order.
- **Differentiation:** support: equations with cups and counters and step prompts / stretch: 5x − 7 = 2x + 8 using the balance model (x = 5).
- **Materials:** cups and counters, balance scales, algebra tiles.
- **Joy:** "Equation escape room" — each solved equation opens a padlock number.

### Lesson 112 — Equations with two unknowns
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find all whole-number solutions to an equation with two unknowns, working systematically." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 6.EE.6)
- **Hook:** "a + b = 10. How many answers are there?"
- **Key activity:** Systematic lists and tables for a + b = 10 and 2a + b = 12 with positive whole numbers (a = 1…5 gives b = 10, 8, 6, 4, 2). Discuss why working systematically prevents missed answers.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can't a be 6 in 2a + b = 12 if a and b must be positive whole numbers?" → 2 × 6 = 12 leaves b = 0, which isn't positive.
- **Differentiation:** support: list pairs using cubes in two colours / stretch: two equations at once: a + b = 10 and a − b = 4 (a = 7, b = 3).
- **Materials:** two-colour cubes, table templates.
- **Joy:** "How many ways?" — a class poster recording every solution found.

### Lesson 113 — Think of a number: why tricks work
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use algebra to explain why a 'think of a number' trick always works." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 6.EE.3–4 preview)
- **Hook:** "Think of a number. Double it. Add 10. Halve it. Subtract the number you first thought of. Your answer is… 5!"
- **Key activity:** Model with a cup (the number) and counters: 2 cups + 10 → 1 cup + 5 → 5. Then algebra: (2n + 10) ÷ 2 − n = 5. Children design their own trick and prove it.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain, with cups and counters, why the starting number disappears." → Halving 2 cups leaves 1 cup, and subtracting the original number removes that cup, leaving only counters.
- **Differentiation:** support: perform and model tricks with cups and counters / stretch: design a trick whose answer is always the person's starting number plus 3.
- **Materials:** cups and counters, algebra tiles, trick cards.
- **Joy:** perform the trick on a family member at home — and explain it.

### Lesson 114 — Generalising: always, sometimes, never
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use examples and simple algebra to test and explain general statements." (UK NC Y6 algebra / CCSS 6.EE.3–4 preview)
- **Hook:** "The sum of two odd numbers is always even. Can we prove it for every odd number, even ones we'll never write?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: odd numbers as pairs of cubes plus one extra — the two extras pair up. Algebra: (2a + 1) + (2b + 1) = 2(a + b + 1). Test other statements: "the product of two consecutive numbers is even" (always), "a multiple of 4 is a multiple of 8" (sometimes).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is testing five examples not enough to prove 'always'?" → Examples can show 'sometimes' or find a counterexample, but only a general argument covers every case.
- **Differentiation:** support: test with multilink models / stretch: prove the sum of any three consecutive numbers is a multiple of 3 using n − 1, n, n + 1.
- **Materials:** multilink cubes, statement cards, algebra tiles.
- **Joy:** "Maths court" — groups argue the case for 'always', 'sometimes' or 'never'.

### Lesson 115 — Retrieval: algebra
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall and connect sequences, expressions, formulae and equations." (UK NC Y6 algebra — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "Algebra bingo: every number on your card is the solution to an equation."
- **Key activity:** Bingo with equations, substitution and nth-term questions; then pupils sort common errors into categories and correct them.
- **Check for understanding:** "The nth term is 4n − 1. Is 45 in the sequence? Explain." → No: 4n − 1 = 45 gives 4n = 46, n = 11.5, which is not a whole-number position.
- **Differentiation:** support: bingo card with one-step equations / stretch: create a bingo question for each number on your card.
- **Materials:** bingo cards, cups and counters, mini-whiteboards.
- **Joy:** algebra bingo, with the prize of choosing tomorrow's warm-up.

### Lesson 116 — Unit 9 mastery check
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show I have mastered expressions, formulae, sequences and equations." (UK NC Y6 algebra — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Secret agent training: decode, generalise and solve."
- **Key activity:** Independent mastery task, then conferencing and targeted regrouping.
- **Check for understanding:** "Books cost 6 each plus 4 postage per order. Write a formula for the cost C of b books, and find b when C = 40." → C = 6b + 4; 6b + 4 = 40 → b = 6.
- **Differentiation:** support: cups and counters, formula word banks / stretch: find a formula for the number of tiles in the nth pattern of a growing L-shape.
- **Materials:** mastery task, algebra tiles, cups and counters.
- **Joy:** passing all "agent missions" earns a certificate of algebraic clearance.

**End-of-unit check:** (1) Find 5n − 2 when n = 6 (28). (2) Solve 4x + 9 = 37 (7). (3) Next term and nth term of 5, 9, 13, … (17; 4n + 1). (4) Write a formula for the cost of b books at 6 each plus 4 postage. (5) Find all positive whole-number pairs for 3a + b = 13.

### Lesson 117 — The metric system: one idea, many units

### Lesson 119 — Measurement problems with conversions

### Lesson 120 — Time: durations and time zones

## Unit 11 — Area, perimeter and volume

**Essential question:** How can formulae help us measure the space inside and around shapes — and why do they work?

**Key vocabulary:** perimeter, area, square centimetre (cm²), square metre (m²), formula, base, perpendicular height, right-angled triangle, parallelogram, compound shape, volume, cubic centimetre (cm³), cubic metre (m³), cuboid, capacity, surface area

### Lesson 125 — Same area, different perimeter

### Lesson 126 — Area of rectangles and compound rectilinear shapes

## Unit 12 — Geometry: angles, shapes, circles and nets

**Essential question:** What rules do angles and shapes always obey — and how can we use them to find what we can't measure?

**Key vocabulary:** angle, degree, acute, obtuse, reflex, straight line, around a point, vertically opposite, interior angle, polygon, regular, isosceles, equilateral, scalene, quadrilateral, parallel, perpendicular, construct, compasses, circle, radius, diameter, circumference, pi (π), face, edge, vertex, net, polyhedron, tessellation

### Lesson 139 — Angles on a straight line and around a point
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use the facts that angles on a straight line total 180° and angles around a point total 360° to find missing angles." (UK NC Y6 geometry / CCSS 7.G.5 preview)
- **Hook:** "A pizza is cut into 5 slices from the centre. Four angles are 70°, 80°, 60° and 90°. What is the fifth — without a protractor?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: measure angles on a straight line with a protractor and see they total 180°; paper-plate "angle wheels". Abstract: missing-angle problems with reasons written in words ("angles around a point add to 360°").
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must the missing pizza angle be 60°?" → Angles around a point total 360°; 70 + 80 + 60 + 90 = 300, so 360 − 300 = 60°.
- **Differentiation:** support: two-angle problems with protractors to check / stretch: multi-step diagrams needing both facts.
- **Materials:** protractors, paper plates, split pins, rulers.
- **Joy:** "Pizza angle challenge" — cut paper pizzas and verify the missing angles.

### Lesson 140 — Vertically opposite angles
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why vertically opposite angles are equal and use it to find angles." (UK NC Y6 geometry / CCSS 7.G.5 preview)
- **Hook:** "Cross two rulers like scissors. As you open them, which angles stay equal?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: two strips joined with a split pin; measure all four angles. Pictorial: diagrams of crossing lines. Abstract: prove using straight-line angles (a + b = 180 and b + c = 180, so a = c).
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain, without measuring, why vertically opposite angles must be equal." → Each one makes 180° with the same neighbouring angle, so they must be the same size.
- **Differentiation:** support: measure crossing lines with protractors first / stretch: find every angle in a diagram of three lines crossing at one point, given one angle of 50° and another of 70°.
- **Materials:** card strips and split pins, protractors, rulers.
- **Joy:** "Scissor spy" — use the rule to find angles in photos of scissors, bridges and road junctions.

### Lesson 141 — Angles in a triangle
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show that the angles in a triangle total 180° and use this to find missing angles." (UK NC Y6 geometry / CCSS 8.G.5 preview)
- **Hook:** "Draw any triangle, tear off its three corners and put them together. What do you notice?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: tear-and-arrange corners on a straight line. Abstract: missing angles in scalene, isosceles (two equal angles) and equilateral (60° each) triangles; right-angled triangles.
- **Check for understanding:** "Could a triangle have two obtuse angles? Explain." → No: two obtuse angles would total more than 180° before adding the third.
- **Differentiation:** support: find one missing angle with two given, using a protractor to check / stretch: an isosceles triangle has one angle of 40° — find two possible sets of angles (40, 70, 70 or 40, 40, 100).
- **Materials:** paper triangles, scissors, protractors, rulers.
- **Joy:** "Triangle impossible" — try to draw triangles that break the rule and explain why they fail.

### Lesson 142 — Angles in quadrilaterals and polygons
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find the angle sum of any polygon by splitting it into triangles." (UK NC Y6 geometry / CCSS 7.G.5 extension)
- **Hook:** "What do the angles of a stop sign add up to?" (An octagon: 1,080°.)
- **Key activity:** Pictorial: split polygons into triangles from one vertex (quadrilateral 2 triangles = 360°; pentagon 3 = 540°; hexagon 4 = 720°). Abstract: the table leads to (n − 2) × 180°; interior angle of a regular hexagon = 720 ÷ 6 = 120°.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a pentagon split into 3 triangles, not 5?" → Drawing diagonals from one vertex makes n − 2 triangles; the two sides next to that vertex don't make new triangles.
- **Differentiation:** support: quadrilaterals only, verified by tearing corners / stretch: find the interior angle of a regular octagon (135°) and of a regular decagon (144°).
- **Materials:** polygon cut-outs, rulers, protractors, table template.
- **Joy:** "Road-sign detectives" — calculate the angles of real sign shapes from around the world.

### Lesson 143 — Classifying triangles and quadrilaterals
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can classify triangles and quadrilaterals by their properties and explain family relationships (e.g. every square is a rectangle)." (UK NC Y6 geometry / CCSS 5.G.3–4)
- **Hook:** "True or false: a square is a rectangle."
- **Key activity:** Concrete: geostrips to build and deform shapes (a rectangle pushed becomes a parallelogram). Pictorial: a hierarchy (family tree) diagram and a Venn diagram. Abstract: list properties (sides, angles, parallel sides, diagonals). - **Check for understanding:** "Explain why every square is a rhombus but not every rhombus is a square." → A square has four equal sides (so it is a rhombus) and also four right angles; a rhombus need not have right angles.
- **Differentiation:** support: sort shapes by one property at a time / stretch: write a "Guess my quadrilateral" riddle with the fewest clues that identify exactly one shape.

### Lesson 144 — Constructing triangles
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can construct a triangle accurately from given sides and angles using a ruler, protractor and compasses." (UK NC Y6 geometry / CCSS 7.G.2 preview)
- **Hook:** "Can you draw a triangle with sides 3 cm, 4 cm and 8 cm?"
- **Key activity:** I do: construct a triangle given two sides and the angle between them (ruler + protractor); given three sides (ruler + compasses: arcs meeting). We do / you do: accuracy checked by measuring the unused parts. Discover that 3, 4, 8 fails (the two short sides can't reach).

### Lesson 145 — Parts of a circle
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can name and draw the radius, diameter and circumference and use d = 2r." (UK NC Y6 geometry / CCSS 7.G.4 preview)
- **Hook:** "How do you draw a perfect circle on the playground with only chalk and string?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: string-and-chalk circles outdoors (the string is the radius); compasses indoors. Pictorial: label parts. Abstract: d = 2r; r = d ÷ 2.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is every diameter exactly twice the radius?" → A diameter goes from the edge through the centre to the other edge — two radii end to end.
- **Differentiation:** support: measure radius and diameter on drawn circles / stretch: find the centre of a circle traced from a plate using folding (two diameters cross at the centre).

### Lesson 146 — Circumference and pi
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can investigate the link between the circumference and diameter of a circle and explain what pi is." (CCSS 7.G.4 preview — bridge to Year 6)
- **Hook:** "Is there a secret number hidden inside every circle?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: measure the circumference (with string) and diameter of many round objects (tins, plates, bicycle wheels). Record circumference ÷ diameter in a table — always a little over 3. Name it π ≈ 3.14; mention that Archimedes estimated it between 3 10⁄71 and 3 1⁄7.
- **Check for understanding:** "A wheel has a diameter of 50 cm. Roughly how far does it roll in one turn? Explain." → About 3 × 50 = 150 cm (more precisely about 157 cm), because the circumference is a bit more than 3 diameters.
- **Differentiation:** support: measure 3 objects with a partner, using calculators for the division / stretch: calculate class mean of C ÷ d and explain why measurements aren't exactly 3.14159…
- **Materials:** round objects, string, tape measures, rulers, calculators.
- **Joy:** "Pi hunt" — measure the biggest circle in school (a roundabout or a netball circle).

### Lesson 147 — 3D shapes and Euler's rule
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe 3D shapes by faces, edges and vertices and test Euler's rule." (UK NC Y6 geometry / CCSS 6.G.4 preview)
- **Hook:** "Count the faces, edges and vertices of a cube. Is there a hidden connection?"

### Lesson 149 — Tessellation and angles: geometry in world art
- **Duration:** 40 min

- **Hook:** "Where would your reflection be if the y-axis were a mirror?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: mirrors and tracing paper on the grid. Abstract: reflecting (3, 5) in the y-axis gives (−3, 5); in the x-axis gives (3, −5); spot the pattern (one sign changes).
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain why reflecting in the y-axis changes the sign of x but not y." → The point moves across the vertical axis to the same distance on the other side, at the same height.
- **Differentiation:** support: reflect single points using a mirror / stretch: reflect a shape in the x-axis and then the y-axis — what single transformation is the same? (A half-turn about the origin.)
- **Materials:** small mirrors, tracing paper, four-quadrant grids.
- **Joy:** "Symmetry art" — create a four-way symmetric design by reflecting in both axes.

### Lesson 155 — Reasoning with coordinates: missing vertices
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use shape properties to find missing coordinates." (UK NC Y6 position / CCSS 6.G.3)
- **Hook:** "Three corners of a treasure map square are at (1, 1), (4, 1) and (4, 4). Where's the treasure (the fourth corner)?"
- **Key activity:** Use properties (equal sides, parallel sides) to find missing vertices; find (1, 4) for the square; for a parallelogram (0, 0), (4, 0), (5, 3) the fourth vertex is (1, 3).
- **Check for understanding:** "How do you know the fourth corner of the parallelogram is (1, 3)?" → Opposite sides are parallel and equal: from (0, 0) move the same as from (4, 0) to (5, 3) — 1 right, 3 up.
- **Differentiation:** support: squares and rectangles only, on squared paper / stretch: find all possible fourth vertices for a parallelogram with three given vertices (there are three).
- **Materials:** grids, rulers, geoboards.
- **Joy:** treasure-map puzzles with shape clues.

### Lesson 156 — Unit 13 mastery check
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show I have mastered four-quadrant coordinates, translations and reflections." (UK NC Y6 position — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Mission control: guide the rover across the four-quadrant map."
- **Key activity:** Independent mastery task; conferencing; then a whole-class four-quadrant Battleships tournament.
- **Check for understanding:** "Triangle A has vertices (1, 2), (3, 2) and (1, 5). Reflect it in the x-axis. Give the new vertices." → (1, −2), (3, −2), (1, −5).
- **Differentiation:** support: large grids and tracing paper / stretch: describe a sequence of two transformations that maps one shape onto another.
- **Materials:** mastery task, grids, tracing paper, Battleships sheets.
- **Joy:** Battleships tournament final.

**End-of-unit check:** (1) Plot (−4, 3), (2, −1), (0, −5). (2) Name the quadrant of (−2, −7). (3) Translate (−1, 4) 5 right and 6 down ((4, −2)). (4) Reflect (6, −2) in the y-axis ((−6, −2)). (5) Find the fourth vertex of the rectangle (−2, 1), (3, 1), (3, −2) ((−2, −2)).

## Unit 14 — Statistics: line graphs, pie charts and the mean

**Essential question:** How can data help us answer questions — and how can graphs mislead us?

**Key vocabulary:** data, survey, frequency, line graph, pie chart, sector, proportion, mean, average, range, outlier, axis, scale, misleading

### Lesson 157 — Line graphs: comparing two lines
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read, interpret and compare data on line graphs with two lines." (UK NC Y6 statistics / CCSS 5.G.2 link)
- **Hook:** "Why is January hot in Sydney but cold in Oslo?"
- **Key activity:** Read a two-line graph of monthly average temperatures for a Southern and a Northern Hemisphere city (teacher sources current climate averages). Interpret: when is the difference biggest? When do the lines cross? Link to Earth's tilt (science connection).
- **Check for understanding:** "What does it mean where the two lines cross?" → In that month both cities have the same average temperature.
- **Differentiation:** support: one-line graphs with a ruler to read across / stretch: sketch a line graph for a city on the equator and explain its shape.
- **Materials:** printed line graphs, rulers, climate data tables, a globe.
- **Joy:** "Holiday planner" — use the graphs to pick the best month to visit each city.

### Lesson 158 — Reading pie charts
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can interpret pie charts using fractions and percentages of the whole." (UK NC Y6 statistics / CCSS 6.SP link)
- **Hook:** "Two pie charts: school A and school B. Charlie says more children walk at school A because its sector is bigger. Is he right?"
- **Key activity:** Pictorial: pie charts on fraction circles (half, quarter, tenth sectors). Abstract: if 120 children were surveyed and ¼ walk, then 30 walk. Discuss that a pie chart shows proportion, not totals.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can't you compare sector sizes across two pie charts with different totals?" → A pie chart shows fractions of its own total; ¼ of 400 is more than ½ of 100.
- **Differentiation:** support: pie charts divided into halves, quarters, eighths with the total given / stretch: pie charts marked with angles (e.g. 90° of 360° of 60 children = 15).
- **Materials:** fraction circles, printed pie charts, protractors.
- **Joy:** "Chart detectives" — hunt for pie charts in newspapers and check if they add up.

### Lesson 159 — Calculating angles for pie charts
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate the angle for each sector of a pie chart from data." (UK NC Y6 statistics)
- **Hook:** "30 children voted for their favourite fruit. How many degrees does one vote get?"
- **Key activity:** 360° ÷ 30 = 12° per vote; 8 votes → 96°. Tables of data → angles; check angles total 360°. Link to percentages (a quarter = 90°).
- **Check for understanding:** "If 36 people are surveyed, why is each person worth 10°?" → The whole circle is 360°, shared equally among 36 people.
- **Differentiation:** support: totals that divide 360 easily (12, 36, 60, 90) with a ratio table / stretch: totals such as 24 or 40, and a data set given as percentages.
- **Materials:** data tables, calculators (checking), ratio tables.
- **Joy:** survey the class and prepare the angles for tomorrow's pie chart.

### Lesson 160 — Drawing pie charts
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw an accurate pie chart with a protractor and compasses." (UK NC Y6 statistics)
- **Hook:** "Is the class favourite-fruit chart right? Let's draw it accurately and see."
- **Key activity:** I do: draw a circle with compasses, a radius as start line, measure each angle in turn, label sectors. We do / you do: draw from the class survey data (from Lesson 159).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we measure each new angle from the last line drawn, not from the start line?" → Each sector starts where the previous one ended; measuring from the start line would overlap sectors.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-drawn circles with a start line and angles that are multiples of 30° / stretch: draw a pie chart from percentage data (convert to degrees first).
- **Materials:** safety compasses, 360° protractors, rulers, coloured pencils.
- **Joy:** the class's pie charts go into a "Who We Are" data display.

### Lesson 161 — The mean as levelling out
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the mean as sharing out equally and calculate it." (UK NC Y6 statistics / CCSS 6.SP.3, 6.SP.5c)
- **Hook:** "Three towers of cubes: 3, 5 and 7. How can we make them fair?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: level the towers by moving cubes (all become 5). Pictorial: bar charts levelled with a line. Abstract: mean = total ÷ number of values (15 ÷ 3 = 5).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does adding the values and dividing by how many there are 'level out' the towers?" → Adding finds the total cubes; dividing shares them equally between the towers.
- **Differentiation:** support: level physical towers before calculating / stretch: add a 4th tower of 13 — how does the mean change? (7) Why does one big value pull the mean up?
- **Materials:** multilink cubes, bar-chart templates, calculators (checking).
- **Joy:** "Fair shares" — level out snacks (or counters) in teams.

### Lesson 162 — Using the mean
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate the mean, find a missing value from the mean, and decide when the mean is a useful average." (UK NC Y6 statistics / CCSS 6.SP.5c)
- **Hook:** "The mean of four scores is 6. Three of them are 5, 7 and 8. What was the fourth?"
- **Key activity:** Total = mean × number (6 × 4 = 24); missing = 24 − 20 = 4. Real data: rainfall, reaction times, class jumps; spot an outlier and see how it distorts the mean.
- **Check for understanding:** "Five friends' pocket money: 5, 5, 6, 6 and 50. Is the mean (14.40) a fair 'typical' value? Explain." → No: the 50 is an outlier that pulls the mean up; most receive 5 or 6.
- **Differentiation:** support: whole-number means with cubes / stretch: create a set of 5 numbers with mean 10 and range 20.
- **Materials:** multilink cubes, data cards, calculators.
- **Joy:** "Reaction-time lab" — ruler-drop tests, then the mean of three tries.

### Lesson 163 — Choosing graphs and spotting misleading ones
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose an appropriate graph for data and explain how a graph can mislead." (UK NC Y6 statistics / CCSS 6.SP.4 link)
- **Hook:** Two bar charts of the same sales data: one looks like sales tripled, one like they barely changed. How?
- **Key activity:** Examine misleading features: axes not starting at zero, uneven scales, 3D pie charts, missing labels. Match data types to graphs (line graph for change over time, pie chart for parts of a whole, bar chart for comparing categories).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a line graph wrong for showing favourite colours?" → The categories aren't connected or ordered in time, so points between them mean nothing.
- **Differentiation:** support: match three data sets to three graphs / stretch: redraw a misleading graph honestly and write the headline it should have had.
- **Materials:** examples of misleading graphs, squared paper, rulers.
- **Joy:** "Graph crimes" — pupils present the worst graph they found and fix it.

### Lesson 164 — Statistical investigation and unit mastery check
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can carry out a mini statistical investigation — question, data, graph, conclusion — and show my mastery of statistics." (UK NC Y6 statistics — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Do Year 5 children sleep enough? Let's find out."
- **Key activity:** Short mastery task (reading a line graph, pie-chart angles, mean), then groups pose a question, collect class data, choose and draw a graph, calculate a mean and write a conclusion.
- **Check for understanding:** "The mean sleep time of 6 children is 9 hours. Five slept 9, 10, 8, 9 and 9 hours. How long did the sixth sleep?" → 54 − 45 = 9 hours.
- **Differentiation:** support: a structured investigation template / stretch: compare two groups using both mean and range.
- **Materials:** mastery task, investigation template, compasses, protractors, squared paper.
- **Joy:** present findings at a class "Data Summit".

**End-of-unit check:** (1) In a pie chart of 60 people, what angle represents 15 people? (90°). (2) Mean of 12, 15, 9, 20, 14 (14). (3) The mean of 3 numbers is 8; two are 6 and 7 — find the third (11). (4) Read a two-line graph and describe the biggest difference. (5) Explain one way a graph could mislead.

## Unit 15 — Year-end investigations and bridge to Year 6

**Essential question:** How do mathematicians combine everything they know to solve real problems?

**Key vocabulary:** investigate, plan, budget, estimate, justify, model, conclusion, integer, negative, zero pair

### Lesson 165 — Investigation 1: plan a class trip
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a trip within a budget using ratio, percentages, time and money." (UK NC Y6 — applied)
- **Hook:** "You have 1,200 to take 28 children and the required adults to a museum. Can you do it?"
- **Key activity:** Groups use a pack (adult-to-child ratio of 1 : 7, coach prices, ticket prices with group discounts, a timetable) to build a costed plan and a schedule; present with a bar model of the budget.
- **Check for understanding:** "With a ratio of 1 adult to 7 children, how many adults are needed for 28 children, and why?" → 4 adults: 28 ÷ 7 = 4 groups, each with 1 adult.
- **Differentiation:** support: a structured planning sheet with steps / stretch: add a 15% discount on entry for groups over 30 people — does inviting more children save money per person?
- **Materials:** trip-planning pack, calculators, timetables, bar-model templates.
- **Joy:** the class votes on the best plan (and, where possible, the trip happens).

### Lesson 166 — Investigation 2: design a playground to scale
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can design a playground to scale, calculating area, perimeter and cost." (UK NC Y6 — applied)
- **Hook:** "The school has a 30 m × 20 m space and a budget. What would you build?"
- **Key activity:** Draw a scale plan (1 cm : 2 m) with at least three zones (including a triangle or parallelogram); calculate areas, fencing and surfacing costs from a price list; present a costed proposal.
- **Check for understanding:** "On your 1 cm : 2 m plan, a sandpit is 3 cm × 2 cm. What is its real area?" → 6 m × 4 m = 24 m² — convert each length to real metres first, then multiply.
- **Differentiation:** support: rectangular zones only and a scale of 1 cm : 1 m / stretch: include a circular zone and estimate its perimeter using π ≈ 3.
- **Materials:** squared paper, rulers, compasses, price list, calculators.
- **Joy:** proposals are shared with the school leadership or parents' group.

### Lesson 167 — Investigation 3: a statistics project
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan and carry out a statistical investigation on a question I care about." (UK NC Y6 — applied)
- **Hook:** "What would you like to know about our school? Let's find out with data."
- **Key activity:** Pupils choose a question (e.g. "Do older children read more?"), design a fair data-collection method, collect data, present it with an appropriate graph and a mean, and write a conclusion with limitations.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might asking only your friends give a misleading answer?" → The sample isn't representative; friends may share the same habits.
- **Differentiation:** support: choose from three prepared questions with a recording sheet / stretch: compare two groups and discuss whether the difference is big enough to matter.
- **Materials:** survey templates, squared paper, compasses, protractors, calculators.
- **Joy:** a "Data Fair" where pupils present posters to another class.

### Lesson 168 — Year retrieval: number, fractions, ratio and algebra
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall and apply the key number, fraction, ratio and algebra ideas from this year." (UK NC Y6 — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "The Year 5 maths escape room: five locks, five topics."
- **Key activity:** Team escape-room: each lock needs a correct answer (long division, fraction operations, FDP, ratio sharing, equation solving); a reflection on which ideas feel secure.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which is greater: ⅗ of 45 or 65% of 40? Show your method." → ⅗ of 45 = 27; 65% of 40 = 26; so ⅗ of 45 is greater.
- **Differentiation:** support: hint envelopes with a worked example for each lock / stretch: a bonus lock requiring a two-step equation built from a ratio problem.
- **Materials:** escape-room lock cards (or combination padlocks), counters, fraction strips, bar-model templates.
- **Joy:** the escape-room race (with teamwork, not speed alone, rewarded).

### Lesson 169 — Year retrieval: geometry, measure and statistics
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall and apply the key geometry, measurement and statistics ideas from this year." (UK NC Y6 — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "Maths trail: the answers are hidden around the school."
- **Key activity:** A school-grounds maths trail: measure and calculate area and volume, find missing angles on real structures, use coordinates on a map, calculate a mean from data at a station.
- **Check for understanding:** "At the trail's flowerbed (a right-angled triangle with legs 3 m and 4 m), what is its area?" → ½ × 3 × 4 = 6 m².
- **Differentiation:** support: trail done in mixed-ability pairs with formula cards / stretch: an extra station on circles (estimate a circumference with π ≈ 3.14).
- **Materials:** trail map, tape measures, trundle wheel, protractors, clipboards.
- **Joy:** the outdoor maths trail itself.

### Lesson 170 — Celebration and bridge to Year 6: operating with negative numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use zero pairs to add simple negative numbers, and I can describe my growth as a mathematician this year." (CCSS 7.NS.1 preview — bridge to Year 6)
- **Hook:** "What is −3 + 5? Two-colour counters can tell us."
- **Key activity:** Concrete: two-colour counters (yellow +1, red −1); a yellow and a red make a zero pair. Model −3 + 5 = 2 and 4 + (−6) = −2. Then a reflection: each pupil chooses their proudest piece of work from the year and writes a letter to their Year 6 self.
- **Check for understanding:** "Using counters, explain why 4 + (−6) = −2." → 4 yellow and 6 red counters make 4 zero pairs, leaving 2 red: −2.
- **Differentiation:** support: model only with counters and a number line / stretch: predict the answer to −2 + (−5) and explain with counters (−7).
- **Materials:** two-colour counters, number lines −10 to 10, maths journals.
- **Joy:** a "Maths Oscars" celebration of the year's best problem-solving moments.

**End-of-unit check:** Presentation of one investigation, with the pupil explaining their method, their mathematics, and one thing they would do differently.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Place value and negatives** — reads, orders and rounds any number to 10,000,000 and finds intervals across zero (e.g. explains the rise from −7 °C to 5 °C on a number line).
2. **Written methods** — accurately completes 4-digit × 2-digit long multiplication and 4-digit ÷ 2-digit long division, interpreting remainders in context (seen in a timed-free set of 6 calculations and 2 word problems).
3. **Order of operations** — evaluates expressions with brackets and powers and explains why 2 + 3 × 4 = 14.
4. **Fractions** — adds and subtracts any fractions and mixed numbers; multiplies fractions and divides a fraction by a whole number, explaining with an area or bar model.
5. **FDP** — converts instantly between key fractions, decimals and percentages and finds any percentage of an amount (e.g. 35% of 80 mentally).
6. **Ratio and proportion** — shares in a ratio, scales a recipe, uses a map scale and compares unit prices, drawing a bar model or ratio table to justify.
7. **Algebra** — writes an expression or formula for a situation, substitutes values, finds an nth term of a linear sequence and solves a two-step equation, checking by substitution.
8. **Measure and geometry** — calculates the area of triangles and parallelograms and the volume of cuboids; finds missing angles using angle facts with reasons; constructs a triangle.
9. **Statistics** — draws and interprets a pie chart and calculates and critiques a mean.
10. **Reasoning** — explains a generalisation (e.g. why the sum of two odd numbers is even) using an example and a general argument.

Seen through a one-to-one "maths conversation" (15 minutes per pupil) plus the portfolio of unit mastery tasks — never a single high-stakes test.

## Teacher guidance

**Common misconceptions to watch for**
- *"Multiplication makes bigger, division makes smaller."* Directly confront this in Units 5–6: ⅔ × 12 = 8 and 12 ÷ ⅔ = 18. Always ask, "Is the other number more or less than 1?"
- *Decimals as two separate whole numbers* (0.45 > 0.5 "because 45 > 5"). Keep decimal counters and zoomed number lines available all year.
- *Adding denominators* (½ + ⅓ = 2⁄5). Return to fraction strips: parts must be the same size before they can be counted together.
- *BIDMAS read as strict order* (division always before multiplication). Teach "× and ÷ are equal partners, left to right; so are + and −".
- *Ratio confused with fraction* (4 : 5 read as ⅘). Draw the bar with all 9 parts every time.
- *Slanted side used as height* in triangle and parallelogram areas. Insist on marking the right angle.
- *3a read as 30 + a or as digits 3 and a*; *3n confused with n + 3*. Use cups and counters: 3n is three cups.
- *Percentage change reversible* (up 10% then down 10% returns to the start). Show 100 → 110 → 99.

**How to teach it well**
- Run the daily five-minute mixed retrieval warm-up (three questions from earlier units, one from last year) — spacing is what makes this year stick.
- Always start a new idea concretely, even with confident pupils; the manipulative is what makes the explanation convincing.
- Insist on bar models for all ratio, fraction and percentage word problems. They carry directly into Year 6 algebra.
- Use the unit mastery lessons diagnostically: regroup pupils for the next week, and keep a list of pupils needing a pre-teach before each new unit.
- Keep a class "misconception wall": when a common error appears, name it, show why it's tempting, and show the fix.

**Vertical connections**
- *From Year 4:* place value to 1,000,000, long multiplication, short division, related-denominator fractions, decimals to 3 dp and first percentages — Unit 1 and the Term 1 warm-ups quickly re-secure these.
- *Into Year 6:* ratio and unit rate become proportional reasoning and percentage change; algebra becomes simplifying, expanding and solving with unknowns on both sides; negative numbers (Lesson 170) become all four operations with integers; dividing a whole number by a unit fraction (Lesson 60) becomes dividing a fraction by a fraction; circumference (Lesson 146) becomes circle area; area of triangles becomes area of trapezia and surface area.
- *Across subjects:* science (line graphs, means of repeat readings, unit conversions), geography (map scales, coordinates, climate graphs), computing (coordinates in Scratch and Python, spreadsheet formulae), art (Islamic geometric patterns, enlargement), life skills (budgets, percentages, best-value shopping).
