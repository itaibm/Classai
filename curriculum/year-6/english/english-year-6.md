> Built to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Aligned to **US CCSS ELA Grade 6**

## Year overview

Year 6 English is built on a **class-text spine of six whole books, a Shakespeare play and a poetry
thread**, read in full — never as extracts only. Each unit is anchored in one text: children read
it closely, talk about it, analyse it in writing, and use it as a model for their own writing. Grammar,
vocabulary and spelling are taught **inside** the units, in service of the writing each text inspires.

**Class texts, term by term**

| Term | Class texts | Writing focus | Speaking focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ***Holes*** — Louis Sachar; ***Brown Girl Dreaming*** — Jacqueline Woodson (with an identity poetry anthology); ***A Christmas Carol*** — Charles Dickens | analytical paragraphs, narrative with interwoven timelines, memoir-in-verse, the first analytical essay | book talk, poetry recital |
| 2 | Great speeches (Malala Yousafzai, Martin Luther King Jr., Severn Cullis-Suzuki, John F. Kennedy, Nelson Mandela) and spoken-word poetry; ***A Long Walk to Water*** — Linda Sue Park; short stories from around the world (Bradbury, Hughes, Cisneros, Maupassant, O. Henry, Ruskin Bond) | persuasive speech, research report, balanced argument, short story | speech delivery, formal debate |
| 3 | ***A Midsummer Night's Dream*** — William Shakespeare; ***The Giver*** — Lois Lowry (with Frost's "The Road Not Taken" and Shakespeare's Sonnet 18) | analytical essay on a play, argument essay, dystopian narrative, graduation portfolio | scene performance, graduation speech |

**Why these books.** They are among the most widely taught, most loved books for 11–12-year-olds
worldwide; together they span five continents of setting or origin, classic and modern, prose, verse,
drama and non-fiction; and each carries a big question worth arguing about at this age: fate and
justice (*Holes*), identity and belonging (*Brown Girl Dreaming*), change and responsibility (*A
Christmas Carol*), survival and hope (*A Long Walk to Water*), love and illusion (*A Midsummer Night's
Dream*), freedom and choice (*The Giver*).

**What we assume from Year 5 (ages 10–11, UK Y6 / US Grade 5):** fluent, expressive reading of
long novels; inference, summary and "point–evidence–explain" answers; the full KS2 grammar and
punctuation toolkit (semicolons, colons, dashes, passive voice, formal register, the subjunctive);
the Year 5/6 statutory spelling words and morphology; planning, drafting, editing and proof-reading
for audience and purpose. **What Year 6 adds:** analysis of *how* writers create meaning (language,
structure, form) in extended analytical writing; argument built on evidence and rhetoric; independent
research with sources evaluated and credited; a first full Shakespeare play; formal speaking to real
audiences. **What secondary relies on:** the analytical essay (thesis → evidence → analysis →
context), confident handling of heritage texts and verse, and independent, critical reading habits.

**Every week:** 5 lessons of 40–45 minutes, plus 20+ minutes of **daily independent reading** of a
self-chosen book (see Books & resources) with a reading journal, and a weekly spelling/vocabulary
focus drawn from the class text (Greek and Latin roots, commonly confused words, academic vocabulary).

## Unit 1 — Holes: reading like a writer

**Essential question:** How does a writer weave several stories together so that the ending feels both surprising and inevitable?

**Key vocabulary:** narrative structure, plot, subplot, flashback, interwoven timelines, foreshadowing, irony, characterisation, motif, theme, inference, evidence, analysis, protagonist, antagonist, analytical paragraph

### Lesson 1 — Opening a novel: first impressions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the opening chapters of *Holes* create questions and mood, using quotations as evidence." (CCSS RL.6.1, RL.6.5; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** Read aloud the first line: "There is no lake at Camp Green Lake." — "What questions does this sentence make you ask?"
- **Key activity:** Teacher reads Chapters 1–3 aloud with expression (I do); class lists the questions the opening raises and the words that create a dry, harsh mood (we do); each child writes three questions they want answered and one quotation that creates mood (you do).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might Sachar start with a place that contradicts its own name?" → It creates irony and mystery, warns us that things at the camp are not what they seem, and makes us want to read on.
- **Differentiation:** support: listen with the text in front of them; choose quotations from a pre-highlighted page / stretch: compare with the opening of another novel they know and explain which hooks the reader more
- **Materials:** a copy of *Holes* per child (Louis Sachar), reading journals, sticky notes
- **Joy:** Start a "Mystery wall" of questions that gets answered, one by one, as the class reads

### Lesson 2 — Characterisation: meeting Stanley Yelnats
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can infer what Stanley is like from what he says, does and thinks, and from how others treat him." (CCSS RL.6.3; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** "Stanley's name is a palindrome. What else about him is a pattern that keeps repeating?"
- **Key activity:** Read Chapters 4–7; build a "character iceberg" (surface actions above the line, inferred feelings and beliefs below) with quotations; discuss the family "curse" and Stanley's attitude to it.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is Stanley unlucky or does he give up too easily? Use one quotation to support your view." → A reasoned opinion supported by an accurate quotation (e.g. his family always blaming the "no-good-dirty-rotten-pig-stealing-great-great-grandfather").
- **Differentiation:** support: iceberg template with sentence starters ("Stanley says… which suggests…") / stretch: track how Sachar uses humour to make a sad situation bearable
- **Materials:** *Holes*, iceberg templates, highlighters
- **Joy:** "Hot-seat Stanley" — one child answers the class's questions in role

### Lesson 3 — Two stories, one book: interwoven timelines
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Sachar switches between the present and the past, and why." (CCSS RL.6.5; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** "Suddenly we're in Latvia, more than a hundred years ago. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Read Chapter 7 (Elya Yelnats and Madame Zeroni); create a three-strand timeline (Stanley's present, Elya in Latvia, Green Lake 110 years ago) on a long strip of paper that the class adds to throughout the novel.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does the story of Elya and Madame Zeroni explain about the present-day story?" → It explains the origin of the Yelnats family "curse", because Elya broke his promise to carry Madame Zeroni up the mountain.
- **Differentiation:** support: timeline with the three strands colour-coded and some events already placed / stretch: predict how the strands might connect and record the prediction to test later
- **Materials:** *Holes*, long paper strip for the class timeline, coloured markers
- **Joy:** The "Three-Strand Timeline" becomes a growing wall display

### Lesson 4 — Inference: reading between the lines at Camp Green Lake
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make inferences about the camp and its adults and support them with evidence." (CCSS RL.6.1; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** "The Warden never says she's looking for something. How do we know she is?"
- **Key activity:** Read Chapters 8–15; model "I notice… I infer… because…" with Mr. Sir and the Warden; pairs gather clues that the digging isn't about "building character".
- **Check for understanding:** "What evidence suggests the boys are digging for a reason other than building character?" → The Warden gets excited when Stanley finds the gold tube, gives rewards for anything interesting, and makes them dig in a specific area.
- **Differentiation:** support: clue cards to sort into "evidence" and "not evidence" / stretch: explain how Sachar uses dramatic irony — what the reader suspects before Stanley does
- **Materials:** *Holes*, inference frames, clue cards
- **Joy:** "Detective's notebook" — collect clues like an investigator

### Lesson 5 — Grammar for writers: varying sentences for effect
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use short sentences, long sentences and fragments on purpose to create pace and emphasis." (CCSS L.6.3a; UK KS3 Writing/Grammar)
- **Hook:** Sachar's short chapters and short sentences: "Why does 'He dug.' hit harder than a long sentence?"
- **Key activity:** Analyse sentence lengths in a digging passage; rewrite a flat paragraph using a mixture of simple, compound and complex sentences and one deliberate fragment; read aloud to hear the rhythm.
- **Check for understanding:** "When would a very short sentence be more effective than a long one? Give an example." → To create tension, emphasise a key moment or end a paragraph with impact, e.g. "Then the shovel hit something hard."
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence-combining cards to build complex sentences / stretch: vary sentence openings (subordinate clause, adverbial, participle phrase) and explain each choice
- **Materials:** *Holes*, sentence-combining cards, mini-whiteboards
- **Joy:** "Rhythm remix": read the same paragraph in two versions and let the class vote

### Lesson 6 — The analytical paragraph (What–How–Why)
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write an analytical paragraph that makes a point, embeds a quotation, and analyses how a word or phrase creates meaning." (CCSS W.6.9, W.6.2; UK KS3 Writing)
- **Hook:** Two answers to "How does Sachar present the heat?" — one retells, one analyses. Which is better and why?
- **Key activity:** Model the What (point) – How (evidence, embedded quotation, zooming in on a word) – Why (effect on the reader, link to the writer's purpose) paragraph; co-write one; children write one on the heat or the lizards.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between retelling and analysing?" → Retelling says what happens; analysing explains how the writer's choices create an effect and why.
- **Differentiation:** support: paragraph frame with sentence starters and a chosen quotation / stretch: analyse two words from the same quotation and link to a theme
- **Materials:** *Holes*, What–How–Why paragraph frames, model paragraphs
- **Joy:** "Zoom lens": pick one word and write as much as you can about why it was chosen

### Lesson 7 — Kissin' Kate Barlow: a story within a story
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the story of Katherine Barlow and Sam changes our understanding of Green Lake." (CCSS RL.6.3, RL.6.5; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** "How did a kind schoolteacher become a feared outlaw?"
- **Key activity:** Read Chapters 23 and 25–26; discuss prejudice and injustice in the town of Green Lake; add to the three-strand timeline; write a short analytical paragraph on how the town changed.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does Sachar tell us that it never rained on Green Lake after Sam's death?" → It suggests the land itself was cursed by the town's injustice, linking the present-day dry lake to a past wrong.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided questions and a summary card of the chapters / stretch: compare Katherine's transformation with Stanley's
- **Materials:** *Holes*, timeline strip, discussion prompts
- **Joy:** Add the "Kate Barlow" strand with a mystery "KB" lipstick-tube symbol

### Lesson 8 — Friendship and power: Stanley and Zero
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the friendship between Stanley and Zero develops and what it shows about power and respect." (CCSS RL.6.3; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** "Everyone thinks Zero is 'nothing'. What does the novel think?"
- **Key activity:** Read Chapters 18–29 (in shared and independent reading); trace key moments in the friendship on a relationship graph; discuss the reading lessons Stanley gives Zero.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it significant that Stanley teaches Zero to read?" → It shows respect and equality, gives Zero power the adults denied him, and reverses how others treat him.
- **Differentiation:** support: relationship graph with key chapters marked / stretch: explore how Mr. Pendanski's treatment of Zero reveals prejudice, with evidence
- **Materials:** *Holes*, relationship graph template
- **Joy:** "Friendship graph" — plot the ups and downs visually

### Lesson 9 — Vocabulary: word roots and precise words
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use Greek and Latin roots to work out unfamiliar words and choose precise vocabulary in my writing." (CCSS L.6.4b; UK KS3 Vocabulary)
- **Hook:** "*Desolate*, *dehydrated*, *parched* — all mean 'dry' in some way. Why does the choice matter?"
- **Key activity:** Root study (hydr = water, therm = heat, sol = sun, des-/de- = away/without); build word webs; rank synonyms for "hot" and "tired" by intensity; use three in a paragraph describing the lake.
- **Check for understanding:** "Using the root 'hydr', explain what 'dehydrated' means and why it fits Green Lake." → Without water — the boys and the land have lost their water.
- **Differentiation:** support: a root-and-meaning card with pictures / stretch: find five more words from the same roots and use two in a descriptive sentence
- **Materials:** root cards, dictionaries and thesauruses, *Holes*
- **Joy:** "Word-root detectives": decode invented words made from real roots

### Lesson 10 — Spelling: commonly confused words
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can spell and use commonly confused words correctly (affect/effect, their/there/they're, whose/who's, lose/loose, practice/practise in British spelling)." (CCSS L.6.2b; UK KS3 Spelling)
- **Hook:** "Loose lips or lose lips? One letter changes everything."
- **Key activity:** Teach the rule or memory aid for each pair; sort sentences; proof-read a passage about Camp Green Lake seeded with ten errors; note US/UK spelling differences as equally correct conventions (color/colour).
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose affect or effect: 'The heat had a terrible ___ on the boys.' Explain." → Effect: it is a noun here (a result); "affect" is usually the verb.
- **Differentiation:** support: three pairs only, with picture memory aids / stretch: explain the verb "effect" (to bring about) as in "effect change"
- **Materials:** spelling cards, error passage, personal spelling logs
- **Joy:** "Error hunt" race against the clock

### Lesson 11 — Symbols and motifs: onions, peaches, lizards and holes
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a motif gathers meaning as a novel goes on." (CCSS RL.6.2, RL.6.4; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** A bag of onions on the table: "Why do onions matter so much in this book?"
- **Key activity:** Track four motifs (onions, holes, lizards, water/peaches) across the novel in a table with page references; discuss what each might represent.
- **Check for understanding:** "What might the holes represent by the end of the novel?" → Punishment, but also buried truth and the past being uncovered; digging becomes a way of finding the truth and righting old wrongs.
- **Differentiation:** support: motif table with page numbers supplied / stretch: argue which motif is most important to the theme of justice
- **Materials:** *Holes*, motif tables, an onion (sensory hook)
- **Joy:** "Symbol museum": bring or draw an object and write its museum label

### Lesson 12 — Climbing God's Thumb: the climax
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the climax brings the three storylines together." (CCSS RL.6.5; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** "Stanley carries Zero up the mountain. Where have we seen this before?"
- **Key activity:** Read Chapters 30–39; complete the three-strand timeline; discuss how Stanley unknowingly fulfils Elya's broken promise.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does Stanley carrying Zero up the mountain connect the past and present?" → Zero is a descendant of Madame Zeroni; by carrying him up the mountain Stanley keeps the promise Elya broke, which breaks the curse.
- **Differentiation:** support: a family-tree diagram of the Yelnats and Zeroni families / stretch: identify three earlier moments that foreshadowed the climb
- **Materials:** *Holes*, family-tree template, timeline strip
- **Joy:** Reveal the fully completed Three-Strand Timeline

### Lesson 13 — Foreshadowing and structure
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find and explain examples of foreshadowing and how they make the ending satisfying." (CCSS RL.6.5; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** "Go back to Chapter 1. Which clues were hiding in plain sight?"
- **Key activity:** Groups re-read early chapters hunting for clues (the lizards, the sneakers, Sploosh, the lullaby); present them as "planted clue → payoff" pairs.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a surprise ending more satisfying when it was foreshadowed?" → The reader feels the ending was earned, not random, and can look back and see the pattern.
- **Differentiation:** support: a list of chapters where clues are hidden / stretch: explain how the lullaby about "the wolf" and "the moon" connects the families
- **Materials:** *Holes*, clue-and-payoff cards
- **Joy:** "Clue hunt" race with a prize for the most surprising find

### Lesson 14 — Themes: fate, justice and friendship
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can state a theme of the novel as a full sentence and support it with evidence from across the book." (CCSS RL.6.2; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** "Is the book saying our lives are decided by fate — or by what we choose?"
- **Key activity:** Distinguish a topic ("fate") from a theme ("Our actions can break the patterns we inherit"); gather evidence across the book; debate "fate or choice?" in a structured discussion.
- **Check for understanding:** "Turn the topic 'justice' into a theme statement for *Holes*." → e.g. "Injustice done in the past can harm future generations until someone has the courage to put it right."
- **Differentiation:** support: theme-statement frames / stretch: write two competing theme statements and weigh which is better supported
- **Materials:** *Holes*, theme cards, evidence grid
- **Joy:** "Fate vs choice" debate with a vote at the start and at the end

### Lesson 15 — Book talk: discussion roles
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can take part in a book discussion by building on others' ideas and supporting my views with evidence." (CCSS SL.6.1; UK KS3 Spoken English)
- **Hook:** "A great discussion isn't taking turns to talk — it's building something together."
- **Key activity:** Teach discussion moves ("I'd like to build on…", "I see it differently because…", "Can you point to where…?"); groups hold a 15-minute book talk on *Holes* with rotating roles (questioner, evidence-finder, summariser, connector).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is one way you built on someone else's idea today?" → A specific example using a discussion move.
- **Differentiation:** support: discussion-move cards to hold / stretch: act as chair, drawing in quieter voices and summarising the group's view
- **Materials:** discussion-move cards, role cards, *Holes*
- **Joy:** A "fishbowl" discussion where observers score great moves

### Lesson 16 — Writing: planning a narrative with two timelines
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a short story that alternates between a present-day plot and a past event that explains it." (CCSS W.6.3; UK KS3 Writing)
- **Hook:** "An old object is found in your family's attic. What story is behind it?"
- **Key activity:** Plan using a two-strand story map (present / past) with a link object and a moment where the strands meet; decide on the reveal.
- **Check for understanding:** "How will your reader know when the story moves to the past?" → A clear signal: a section break, a change of tense or time phrase ("A hundred years earlier…"), or a new setting.
- **Differentiation:** support: a planning template with prompts and three object options / stretch: plan a third strand, as Sachar does
- **Materials:** two-strand story maps, interesting "found objects" (an old key, a letter, a coin)
- **Joy:** Choose a mystery object from a box to inspire the story

### Lesson 17 — Writing: drafting the opening and switching time
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draft an opening that hooks the reader and a smooth switch into the past." (CCSS W.6.3a, W.6.3c; UK KS3 Writing)
- **Hook:** Revisit Sachar's first line: write three possible first lines for your own story.
- **Key activity:** Draft the opening and first time switch using taught techniques: hook sentence, varied sentences, transitional phrases.
- **Check for understanding:** "Read your first line aloud. What question does it make the reader ask?" → The child names the question their line raises.
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence starters for time switches / stretch: open in the past strand and delay the present
- **Materials:** writing books, plans, transitions word bank
- **Joy:** "First-line gallery": pin up opening lines for a silent gallery walk

### Lesson 18 — Writing: drafting the middle and ending
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can complete my story so the two timelines connect in a satisfying ending." (CCSS W.6.3; UK KS3 Writing)
- **Hook:** "The best endings feel like a key turning in a lock."
- **Key activity:** Continue drafting; plant one foreshadowing clue; write the ending in which strands connect; teacher conferences.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where did you plant a clue that pays off at the end?" → The child points to a specific sentence and its payoff.
- **Differentiation:** support: a paragraph-by-paragraph checklist / stretch: an ending with a final twist or an echo of the opening line
- **Materials:** writing books, plans
- **Joy:** Share endings in pairs — does the partner say "Oh!"?

### Lesson 19 — Editing: revising for effect
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can revise my story to improve word choice, sentence variety and clarity." (CCSS W.6.5; UK KS3 Writing)
- **Hook:** "Writers don't write, they rewrite." Show a real draft page with crossings-out (e.g. a manuscript facsimile).
- **Key activity:** Revise using ARMS (Add, Remove, Move, Substitute); peer feedback with "two stars and a wish"; improve at least three sentences.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show one sentence you changed and explain why the new version is better." → Clear before/after with a reason about effect on the reader.
- **Differentiation:** support: a focused checklist (three items) / stretch: cut 10% of the words without losing meaning
- **Materials:** coloured pens for revision, ARMS card, drafts
- **Joy:** "Before and after" reveal in pairs

### Lesson 20 — Proof-reading and publishing
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can proof-read for spelling, punctuation and grammar and publish a final copy." (CCSS L.6.1, L.6.2; UK KS3 Writing)
- **Hook:** "One typo can ruin a great story. Be the editor."
- **Key activity:** Proof-read using CUPS (Capitals, Usage, Punctuation, Spelling); read aloud to catch errors; publish by hand in neat joined handwriting or type.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between revising and proof-reading?" → Revising improves the content and style; proof-reading fixes surface errors.
- **Differentiation:** support: proof-read with a partner / stretch: edit a partner's story as a professional editor with margin comments
- **Materials:** CUPS checklist, dictionaries, publishing paper or devices
- **Joy:** Stories bound into a class anthology

### Lesson 21 — Film vs book: comparing adaptations
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare a scene in the novel with its film adaptation and evaluate the choices made." (CCSS RL.6.7; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** "The film of *Holes* (2003, Disney, screenplay by Louis Sachar himself) — did it get it right?"
- **Key activity:** Watch one or two scenes (e.g. the first day of digging); compare with the text using a Venn diagram; evaluate what film adds (music, faces) and loses (inner thoughts).
- **Check for understanding:** "What can a novel do that a film finds hard? Give an example from *Holes*." → Show a character's thoughts and feelings directly, e.g. Stanley's private thoughts about his family and himself.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided comparison questions / stretch: write a critic's review comparing the two
- **Materials:** film of *Holes* (2003) — selected scenes only, Venn diagram templates
- **Joy:** "Critics' corner": star ratings with reasons

### Lesson 22 — Analytical writing: planning a response to the whole novel
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan an extended answer to 'How does Sachar show that the past affects the present?' with three points and evidence." (CCSS W.6.9, W.6.2a; UK KS3 Writing)
- **Hook:** "Your job is to prove an argument about a book — like a lawyer with evidence."
- **Key activity:** Introduce the thesis statement; brainstorm and select three points; gather quotations for each; order them logically.
- **Check for understanding:** "What makes a good thesis statement?" → It answers the question directly, makes an arguable point, and previews the main ideas.
- **Differentiation:** support: a partially completed plan with three suggested points / stretch: include a counter-point
- **Materials:** *Holes*, essay planning grid
- **Joy:** "Lawyer's case file" planning format

### Lesson 23 — Analytical writing: drafting the response
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a multi-paragraph analytical response with an introduction, three analytical paragraphs and a conclusion." (CCSS W.6.2, W.6.9; UK KS3 Writing)
- **Hook:** Read a model introduction: "What does it do in the first sentence?"
- **Key activity:** Draft independently using the plan; use connectives for analysis (furthermore, in contrast, this suggests); teacher conferences.
- **Check for understanding:** "Read one paragraph to a partner: where is the analysis, not just the quotation?" → The child identifies the sentences that explain effect and purpose.
- **Differentiation:** support: paragraph frames for each section / stretch: embed short quotations smoothly within their own sentences
- **Materials:** plans, *Holes*, analytical connectives mat
- **Joy:** "Silent writing" with calm music — the first real essay

### Lesson 24 — Unit 1 assessment and reflection
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can respond independently to an unseen extract and reflect on my progress as a reader and writer." (CCSS RL.6.1–6.5, W.6.9; UK KS3)
- **Hook:** "What do you now notice in a book that you didn't in September?"
- **Key activity:** Unseen short extract with three analytical questions (inference, language, structure); self-assess the analytical essay against criteria; set one reading and one writing target.
- **Check for understanding:** "In the extract, how does the writer create tension? Use evidence." → An answer with a quotation and analysis of a technique (short sentences, pacing, imagery).
- **Differentiation:** support: questions read aloud and a word bank / stretch: a comparative question linking the extract to *Holes*
- **Materials:** unseen extract, assessment rubric, reading journals
- **Joy:** Celebrate the class anthology and a "Holes party" snack (peaches and onion-free!)

**End-of-unit check:** A multi-paragraph analytical response on how Sachar connects the past and the present, assessed for a clear thesis, embedded evidence, analysis of language and structure, and a conclusion; plus the unseen-extract questions.

## Unit 2 — Identity & voice: Brown Girl Dreaming and poetry

**Essential question:** How do poets use form, imagery and sound to tell the truth about who they are and where they come from?

**Key vocabulary:** free verse, stanza, line break, enjambment, imagery, metaphor, simile, personification, repetition, voice, memoir, rhythm, rhyme, haiku, sonnet, tone, speaker

### Lesson 25 — What is a memoir in verse?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what a memoir in verse is and how poems can tell a life story." (CCSS RL.6.5; UK KS3 Reading — poetry)
- **Hook:** Read the opening poem of *Brown Girl Dreaming*, "february 12, 1963": "A whole book of poems that tells a true story — why would someone choose that?"
- **Key activity:** Introduce Jacqueline Woodson and the book (born in Ohio, childhood between South Carolina and Brooklyn during the US civil rights movement); read the first poems aloud; notice line breaks and white space; discuss how the form feels different from prose.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might Woodson have told her life story in short poems instead of long chapters?" → Poems capture memories as moments or snapshots, and line breaks slow us down to feel each image, as memory often works.
- **Differentiation:** support: listen along with an audio recording (Woodson reads her own audiobook) / stretch: compare a poem with how the same event might be told in prose
- **Materials:** *Brown Girl Dreaming* (Jacqueline Woodson), audio recording, a map of the USA
- **Joy:** Listening to the author read her own words

### Lesson 26 — Line breaks and white space
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how line breaks and enjambment control pace and emphasis." (CCSS RL.6.4, RL.6.5; UK KS3 Reading)
- **Hook:** Show the same sentence written as prose and broken into four lines: "Which one makes you pause — where, and why?"
- **Key activity:** Experiment by re-breaking a passage of prose into poem lines in different ways; read aloud; analyse Woodson's line breaks in two poems.
- **Check for understanding:** "What effect does ending a line on an important word have?" → It emphasises the word, making the reader pause on it before moving on.

### Lesson 41 — Victorian London: understanding the context

### Lesson 61 — What makes a speech powerful?

- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify the audience, purpose and key techniques of a famous speech." (CCSS RI.6.8, SL.6.3; UK KS3 Reading — non-fiction)

### Lesson 62 — Ethos, pathos, logos
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain Aristotle's three appeals and find each in a speech." (CCSS RI.6.8; UK KS3 Reading)

### Lesson 63 — Rhetorical devices: repetition, tricolon, rhetorical questions

### Lesson 64 — Structure of a speech

### Lesson 155 — Vocabulary and spelling review: the year's words
- **Duration:** 35 min

### Lesson 156 — Unseen reading: comparing two texts

### Lesson 157 — Unit 8 assessment
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write an argument essay under timed conditions on a question about *The Giver*." (CCSS W.6.1, W.6.9; UK KS3 Writing)

- **Check for understanding:** "How did you handle the counter-claim in your timed essay?" → The child identifies their concession and rebuttal.

- **Materials:** essay questions, rubric

### Lesson 158 — Lowry's craft: reading like a writer (reflection)
- **Duration:** 40 min

**Essential question:** Who am I as a reader, writer and speaker — and who do I want to become?

### Lesson 159 — My reading life: reflection
- **Duration:** 40 min

### Lesson 160 — Personal memoir: planning "My primary years"
- **Duration:** 45 min

## Books & resources

**Class texts (whole books)**
- ***Holes* by Louis Sachar (1998, Newbery Medal)** — a perfectly engineered plot with three interwoven timelines; the ideal text for teaching structure and foreshadowing, and irresistibly funny.
- ***Brown Girl Dreaming* by Jacqueline Woodson (2014, US National Book Award)** — a memoir in verse about growing up during the civil rights movement; teaches poetic form, voice and identity at once.
- ***A Christmas Carol* by Charles Dickens (1843)** — the most accessible great Victorian novella; a first heritage text with a clear moral arc and rich language. Use a complete edition with notes.
- ***A Long Walk to Water* by Linda Sue Park (2010)** — based on the true story of Salva Dut; a short, powerful dual narrative that launches research and argument about a global issue.
- ***A Midsummer Night's Dream* by William Shakespeare** — the most joyful first Shakespeare: comedy, magic and a play-within-a-play. Use a school edition with notes (e.g. Cambridge School Shakespeare).
- ***The Giver* by Lois Lowry (1993, Newbery Medal)** — the classic dystopia for 11–12-year-olds; raises profound questions about freedom, memory and choice in a clear, gripping style.

- ***Shakespeare Stories* by Leon Garfield** — beautifully written prose retellings that let children grasp the plot before the verse.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Reads whole, demanding books independently** — including a 19th-century text and a Shakespeare play — and can summarise, infer and discuss them. *See it:* reading journal and book talks; accurate plot and character summaries.
2. **Writes a multi-paragraph analytical essay** with a thesis, embedded quotations, analysis of language/structure/form and context. *See it:* the *Christmas Carol* and *Midsummer Night's Dream* essays.
3. **Analyses poetry** (form, imagery, sound, voice) and compares two poems. *See it:* the Unit 2 comparison.
4. **Constructs an argument** with claim, evidence, reasoning, counter-claim and rebuttal, and a balanced discussion when the task demands it. *See it:* the *Giver* argument essay and Unit 5 balanced argument.
5. **Researches independently and responsibly** — focused questions, evaluated sources, own-words notes, a bibliography. *See it:* the Unit 5 research report.
6. **Writes compelling narrative and memoir** with deliberate structure, "show, don't tell", purposeful endings and controlled voice. *See it:* the short story, the dystopian opening and the graduation memoir.
7. **Speaks with purpose and confidence** — persuasive speech, formal debate, discussion and performance. *See it:* the Unit 4 speech, the Shakespeare performance, the graduation speech.
8. **Uses grammar, punctuation and spelling accurately and for effect** — sentence variety, dialogue punctuation, cohesion, formal register, commonly confused words, Greek and Latin roots. *See it:* edited final drafts with few surface errors.
9. **Evaluates texts critically** — identifies bias, rhetoric, fallacies and misleading claims. *See it:* the speech analysis and the source evaluations.

## Teacher guidance

- **Read the whole books.** Extracts don't build stamina or love of reading. Read aloud generously (especially Dickens and Shakespeare), use audiobooks for support, and give children their own copies to annotate where possible.
- **Sensitive content.** *The Giver* (the truth about "release", a war memory), *A Long Walk to Water* (war and loss) and *Brown Girl Dreaming* (segregation) deal with serious themes. Prepare children beforehand, inform parents, read the hardest passages together, and leave time to talk. The treatment is age-appropriate and these books are widely taught at ages 11–12, but know your children.
- **Common misconceptions:** retelling instead of analysing (use What–How–Why and "zoom in on a word"); treating a quotation as self-explanatory; confusing theme (a full-sentence idea) with topic (one word); thinking a balanced argument has no conclusion; thinking a persuasive text should ignore the other side; confusing dramatic and situational irony; assuming "The Road Not Taken" simply praises the unusual path; believing Shakespeare's language is a different language rather than earlier English.
- **Grammar in service of writing.** Teach each grammar point in the lesson where the writing needs it (sentence variety in narrative, embedding quotations in analysis, modal verbs in persuasion, cohesion in reports, formal style in essays) and look for it in the next piece of writing, not in isolated exercises.
- **Differentiation.** Support means more scaffolding for the same rich text (audiobooks, glossaries, paraphrase alongside the original, sentence frames) — never an easier book in place of the class text. Stretch means deeper analysis (alternative interpretations, context, comparison, evaluation), not more of the same.
- **International and inclusive.** The texts span the USA, Britain, India, France, Sudan/South Sudan, Japan and the Caribbean. Invite children's home languages into poetry (Unit 2) and memoir; accept both British and American spelling conventions if used consistently.
- **Vertical alignment.** Year 5 secured the full KS2 grammar toolkit, précis and "point–evidence–explain" responses; Year 6 moves to extended analytical writing, argument with rebuttal and research with citation. Secondary English (UK Year 8 / US Grade 7 / MYP 2) assumes a child can write a thesis-driven essay, read heritage texts and a Shakespeare play with support, and speak formally — all mastery checks this year.
- **Handwriting and typing.** Handwriting should be fluent, joined and legible for timed writing; typing (Computing link) is used for publishing and research reports.
