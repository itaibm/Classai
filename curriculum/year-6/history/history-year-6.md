> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Built to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Aligned to the **UK National Curriculum
> Key Stage 3 History** (concepts of chronology, cause and consequence, change and continuity,
> significance, sources and interpretations; world history beyond Britain), **US C3 Framework
> (grades 6–8, D2.His)**, **IB MYP Year 1 Individuals & Societies** (criteria A–D) and
> **Cambridge Lower Secondary Global Perspectives** skills.

## Year overview

Year 6 History is a **1,400-year journey through a connected world**, from the House of Wisdom in
Baghdad to the fall of the Berlin Wall. Earlier years studied single civilisations and periods in
depth; this year asks children to **connect** them — to see how trade, ideas, empires, revolutions
and rights movements linked peoples on every inhabited continent — and to think like historians. The
spine of every unit is the question **"How do we know?"**: every lesson works with real sources
(images, objects, maps, documents, testimony) and every unit meets an **interpretation** that
historians argue about.

**The historian's toolkit (taught in Unit 1 and used all year):** chronology and periods (BCE/CE,
centuries, decades); sources and **provenance** (who made it, when, why — and how that affects its
usefulness); **interpretations** (why historians disagree); **causation** (long- and short-term
causes, consequences); **change and continuity**; and **significance** (criteria for deciding what
matters, and to whom).

**What we assume from Years 3–5:** a chronological framework from the ancient world to 1950;
Year 3's ancient world, including the overland Silk Road (retrieved in Lesson 7, which turns to the
Indian Ocean sea routes instead);
Year 4's depth studies of the Vikings, Abbasid Baghdad, Benin and the Maya (retrieved here, not
re-taught); from Year 5, the Ottoman Empire, the Renaissance and the printing revolution, the
transatlantic slave trade and resistance taught honestly, the history of flight, the Second World War
from a home-front perspective (including refugees and the Kindertransport), votes for women, a local
history enquiry, and source work on reliability, usefulness and "whose voice do we hear?". **What Year 6 adds:** global connections, multiple interpretations, formal source
evaluation and an extended enquiry answer. **What secondary relies on:** a secure mental timeline
of world history, source evaluation using provenance, and extended writing that reaches a supported
judgement.

**Sequence.** Unit 1 builds the toolkit. Units 2–5 move chronologically: a connected medieval world
(600–1450), encounters and empires (1400–1700), revolutions (1750–1900) and the twentieth-century
world. Unit 6 is an independent **oral-history enquiry** presented at the graduation exhibition.
One lesson a week, 40–45 minutes.

## Unit 1 — Thinking like a historian

**Essential question:** How do historians find out about the past — and why don't they always agree?

**Key vocabulary:** chronology, BCE, CE, century, decade, era, primary source, secondary source, provenance, reliability, usefulness, bias, interpretation, significance, evidence, inference

### Lesson 1 — The big timeline
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can place major eras and civilisations from every continent on a timeline and use BCE/CE and centuries correctly." (UK KS3 History — chronology; C3 D2.His.1.6-8)
- **Hook:** "The Great Pyramid of Giza was already about 2,500 years old when Julius Caesar was born. Cleopatra lived closer in time to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid. True?" (Yes.)
- **Key activity:** Build a long washing-line timeline (3000 BCE to today) with event cards from every continent (Indus Valley cities, the Maya, the Tang dynasty, the Kingdom of Aksum, Great Zimbabwe, Inca Empire, the printing press, the Industrial Revolution); one-minute retrieval of Year 5's century conversion (1450 is in the 15th century); the new step is calculating long intervals across BCE/CE and comparing them, using a number line with no year 0.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which is the longer gap: from the building of the Great Pyramid of Giza (c. 2560 BCE) to Cleopatra's death (30 BCE), or from Cleopatra's death to the Moon landing (1969 CE)? Show your working." → Pyramid to Cleopatra: about 2,560 − 30 = 2,530 years; Cleopatra to the Moon landing: 30 + 1,969 − 1 = 1,998 years (no year 0); so the first gap is longer — Cleopatra lived closer in time to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid.
- **Differentiation:** support: a timeline with century markers already labelled / stretch: explain why there is no year 0 and calculate how many years passed from 500 BCE to 500 CE (1,000 years)
- **Materials:** string or washing line, pegs, illustrated event cards from many continents, world map
- **Joy:** "Human timeline" — race to peg events in order

### Lesson 2 — Sources and provenance
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use provenance (who, when, why) to judge how useful a source is for a specific question." (UK KS3 History — sources; C3 D2.His.9.6-8, D2.His.10.6-8)
- **Hook:** "A king's official portrait and a secret letter from his servant describe the same king. Which is more useful?"
- **Key activity:** Teach the NOP questions (Nature, Origin, Purpose); evaluate a set of sources about one event (e.g. a school sports day: an official report, a photograph, a diary entry, a newspaper headline); then apply to a historical pair (a painting of Napoleon crossing the Alps by Jacques-Louis David vs Paul Delaroche's version on a mule).
- **Check for understanding:** "Delaroche painted Napoleon on a mule in 1850, about fifty years after the crossing; David painted him on a rearing horse in 1801 for Napoleon himself. Use Nature, Origin and Purpose to explain why neither painting is simply 'reliable', and what else you would check." → David's was made at the time but to glorify Napoleon, so it exaggerates; Delaroche's looks more realistic (mules were used) but was painted decades later, not by an eyewitness, and with its own purpose (a less heroic view); both need checking against written accounts from people who were on the crossing.
- **Differentiation:** support: a NOP question card with sentence starters / stretch: explain the difference between "reliable" and "useful"
- **Materials:** source cards, images of the two Napoleon paintings, NOP question cards
- **Joy:** "Source detectives" — spot the propaganda

### Lesson 3 — Interpretations: why historians disagree
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why interpretations of the same person or event differ, using Christopher Columbus as a case." (UK KS3 History — interpretations; C3 D2.His.4.6-8, D2.His.5.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Some cities celebrate Columbus Day; some now celebrate Indigenous Peoples' Day instead. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Read three short interpretations of Columbus (a 19th-century textbook hero, a Taíno-perspective account of the effects of conquest, a modern balanced historian's view); identify what each emphasises and why (time written, author's viewpoint, evidence used).
- **Check for understanding:** "Give two reasons why interpretations of Columbus have changed over time." → Historians now include Indigenous perspectives and evidence of violence and disease, and society's values about conquest and human rights have changed.
- **Differentiation:** support: interpretations simplified with key sentences highlighted / stretch: write your own balanced interpretation, citing evidence
- **Materials:** three interpretation extracts, a map of Columbus's voyages, highlighters
- **Joy:** "Historians' courtroom" — each side presents its evidence

### Lesson 4 — Significance: who and what matters?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use criteria to judge the historical significance of an event or person." (UK KS3 History — significance; C3 D2.His.2.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Which was more significant: the invention of the printing press or the invention of the smartphone?"
- **Key activity:** Introduce significance criteria (the "5 Rs": Remarkable, Remembered, Resonant, Resulted in change, Revealing); apply to five inventions (paper in Han China, the printing press, the steam engine, vaccines, the internet); rank and justify; begin the year's "significance wall".
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might something be significant to one group of people but not to another?" → It may have changed one group's lives greatly (e.g. vaccines for families once hit by smallpox) while hardly affecting another; significance depends on criteria and perspective.
- **Differentiation:** support: a criteria checklist with examples / stretch: argue that something "unremarkable" at the time (e.g. a small invention) became highly significant
- **Materials:** invention cards, 5 Rs criteria poster, diamond-ranking templates
- **Joy:** "Diamond nine" ranking debate

**End-of-unit check:** Given two sources about the same event, the child explains which is more useful for a stated question using provenance, and uses the significance criteria to justify a ranking of three events on the timeline.

## Unit 2 — A connected world, 600–1450

**Essential question:** How connected was the medieval world — and who gained from those connections?

**Key vocabulary:** caliphate, Abbasid, House of Wisdom, translation, Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, monsoon, dhow, Swahili, caravan, dynasty, Song, movable type, compass, Mongol, khan, Pax Mongolica, empire, Mali, Mansa, Timbuktu, pilgrimage, Great Zimbabwe, trade network

### Lesson 5 — From Baghdad to Toledo: how knowledge crossed the medieval world
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how knowledge gathered in Abbasid Baghdad reached Europe through al-Andalus and Sicily, and judge who deserves credit for it." (UK KS3 History — a significant society; C3 D2.His.14.6-8)
- **Hook:** "The word 'algebra' comes from a book written in Baghdad around 820 CE. How did it end up in the maths lessons of European universities 400 years later?"
- **Key activity:** Five-minute retrieval of Year 4's Baghdad unit (the Round City, the House of Wisdom, al-Khwarizmi) — not re-taught. New: the routes by which that knowledge travelled west — Córdoba in al-Andalus (the philosopher Ibn Rushd, known in Europe as Averroes), the translators of 12th-century Toledo (such as Gerard of Cremona, who translated dozens of Arabic works into Latin) and the multilingual court of Norman Sicily (al-Idrisi's world map for King Roger II, 1154). Trace Hindu–Arabic numerals from India to Baghdad to Europe (Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci*, 1202; Maths link).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it misleading to say that Arabic scholars simply 'preserved' Greek knowledge for Europe?" → They also corrected, combined and added to it (algebra, medicine, optics, astronomy), and Europeans learned from their new work, not just from the old Greek texts.
- **Differentiation:** support: an illustrated route map with the three 'gateways' labelled / stretch: explain why Toledo and Sicily, where Muslims, Christians and Jews lived side by side, became translation centres
- **Materials:** map of the Mediterranean c. 1150, image of al-Idrisi's map, Hindu–Arabic vs Roman numeral cards, translation-chain cards
- **Joy:** "Translation relay": a sentence passes from Greek to Arabic to Latin to English through four 'translators' — what survives, what changes?

### Lesson 6 — Song China: an age of invention
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe inventions and innovations of Song China (960–1279) and explain their global consequences." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.1.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Paper money, the magnetic compass for navigation, printing with movable type, gunpowder weapons — all from China. Which changed the world most?"
- **Key activity:** Carousel of Song inventions with source images (e.g. a Song-era banknote image, a compass model); discuss consequences (compass → long sea voyages; printing → spread of knowledge); make a simple floating-needle compass.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain one consequence of the magnetic compass for world history." → It made navigation out of sight of land safer, enabling long sea voyages and trade (later including European voyages of exploration).
- **Differentiation:** support: invention cards with a consequence prompt / stretch: evaluate why Europe is often credited with inventions first made in China
- **Materials:** needle, magnet, cork, bowl of water, invention cards, images of Song artefacts
- **Joy:** Make a working floating compass

### Lesson 7 — The Indian Ocean world: monsoons, dhows and the Swahili coast
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the monsoon winds made the Indian Ocean a great trading network linking East Africa, Arabia, India and China, and use archaeological evidence to show its reach." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.14.6-8; Geography link)
- **Hook:** A bag of "finds": a blue-and-white porcelain shard, a cowrie shell, a copper coin and a glass bead. "All of these were found in Africa. How did a Chinese bowl end up in the ruins of Great Zimbabwe?"
- **Key activity:** Two-minute retrieval of Year 3's overland Silk Road (Zhang Qian, goods passed stage to stage, ideas and diseases travelling too) — not repeated. New: the sea routes. The **monsoon** winds blow from the north-east for part of the year and from the south-west for the rest, so a ship's captain sailed one way, traded, and waited for the wind to turn; **dhows** with lateen sails; the Swahili city-states (Kilwa Kisiwani, Mombasa, Zanzibar), whose Swahili language and culture blended African and Arabic influences; Ibn Battuta's visit to Kilwa in 1331, which he described as one of the most beautiful cities he had seen; gold from Great Zimbabwe (Lesson 10) passing through Kilwa. Evidence carousel: Chinese porcelain found at Kilwa and Great Zimbabwe; Kilwa copper coins found as far away as northern Australia; a giraffe from East Africa that reached the Chinese emperor's court in 1414 (preview of Zheng He, Lesson 12). Pupils map the monsoon routes with seasonal wind arrows.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did merchants sailing between East Africa and India have to plan their voyages around the seasons, and what does Chinese porcelain found at Great Zimbabwe tell a historian?" → the monsoon winds blow in one direction for part of the year and the opposite way for the rest, so ships could only sail each way in the right season and had to wait for the wind to change before returning; the porcelain shows that Great Zimbabwe was connected, through the Swahili ports, to trade that reached as far as China — it was part of a wide trading world, not isolated
- **Differentiation:** support: a map with the routes and wind arrows partly drawn, and captioned finds cards / stretch: compare the Indian Ocean and overland Silk Road networks — which carried more goods, and why might sea trade have been cheaper?
- **Materials:** world map with the Indian Ocean centred, wind-arrow cards for both monsoon seasons, photographs of finds (porcelain, coins, beads), images of a dhow, an extract from Ibn Battuta's description of Kilwa
- **Joy:** "Catch the monsoon" — a sailing game in which ships can only move when the wind card for their direction comes up

### Lesson 8 — The Mongols: destroyers or connectors?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare two interpretations of the Mongol Empire and reach a supported judgement." (UK KS3 History — interpretations; C3 D2.His.4.6-8)
- **Hook:** "The largest contiguous land empire in history was built by people on horseback in just a few decades."
- **Key activity:** Two-minute retrieval of Year 4's Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 (the end of the Golden Age) — which now becomes one side of a bigger argument. Map the Mongol Empire's expansion under Genghis Khan and his successors; read two interpretations (a chronicler describing the destruction of cities; a modern historian stressing the Pax Mongolica, safe trade and the relay postal system); weigh evidence for each; link to Marco Polo's travels.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might a 13th-century Persian chronicler and a 21st-century historian see the Mongols differently?" → The chronicler lived through (or near) the destruction; the modern historian can see long-term effects such as increased trade and exchange.
- **Differentiation:** support: interpretation cards with key phrases highlighted / stretch: a written judgement "How far were the Mongols connectors rather than destroyers?"
- **Materials:** maps of Mongol expansion, interpretation extracts, a "judgement line" (from destroyers to connectors)
- **Joy:** "Judgement line" — stand where your evidence puts you

### Lesson 9 — Mali and Mansa Musa
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use the Catalan Atlas and other sources to explain the wealth and significance of the Mali Empire." (UK KS3 History — a non-European society; C3 D2.His.10.6-8)
- **Hook:** The Catalan Atlas (1375) shows an African king holding a gold nugget: "Who is he, and why did a European mapmaker put him there?"
- **Key activity:** Analyse the Catalan Atlas image of Mansa Musa; learn about his pilgrimage to Mecca (1324–1325) — accounts say he gave away so much gold in Cairo that its value fell there; Timbuktu's Sankore mosque and manuscripts; trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does the Catalan Atlas tell us about how Europeans saw Mali, and what can't it tell us?" → Europeans knew Mali as extraordinarily rich in gold; it cannot tell us about ordinary life in Mali or how Malians saw themselves.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided questions on the image / stretch: explain why Arabic sources such as Ibn Battuta (who visited Mali in 1352–1353) are important for historians
- **Materials:** Catalan Atlas image, map of trans-Saharan trade routes, images of Timbuktu's manuscripts and mosque
- **Joy:** "Gold and salt" trade game — why would anyone swap gold for salt?

### Lesson 10 — Great Zimbabwe: whose history?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the history of Great Zimbabwe was distorted by colonial interpretations and how archaeologists established the truth." (UK KS3 History — interpretations; C3 D2.His.6.6-8)
- **Hook:** "For decades, colonial authorities claimed that Africans could not have built these great stone walls. They were wrong. How do we know?"
- **Key activity:** Examine images of the Great Enclosure (built without mortar, roughly 11th–15th centuries); read how colonial writers attributed it to outsiders and how archaeologists (e.g. Gertrude Caton-Thompson in 1929) showed it was built by ancestors of the Shona; discuss why the myth served colonial rule; Zimbabwe took its name from the site at independence in 1980.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did some colonial writers claim Great Zimbabwe was built by outsiders?" → To justify colonial rule by denying that Africans had built great civilisations — the claim served a political purpose, not the evidence.
- **Differentiation:** support: a timeline of interpretations with images / stretch: explain what kinds of archaeological evidence (objects, dating, building methods) settled the question
- **Materials:** images of Great Zimbabwe, a map of its Indian Ocean trade links (Chinese porcelain and Persian glass were found there), interpretation cards
- **Joy:** Build a dry-stone wall with sugar cubes or wooden blocks (no glue!)

### Lesson 11 — Unit 2 enquiry: how connected was the medieval world?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write an extended answer that reaches a judgement using evidence from several societies." (UK KS3 History — extended writing; C3 D2.His.16.6-8)
- **Hook:** "One Chinese bowl found in Zimbabwe tells a story of connection across thousands of kilometres."
- **Key activity:** Plan and write a structured answer ("The medieval world was highly connected. How far do you agree?") with an introduction, two or three evidence paragraphs, a counter-point and a conclusion with a judgement.
- **Check for understanding:** "What makes a historical judgement 'supported'?" → It is backed by specific evidence (examples, dates, sources) and explains why that evidence matters more than the counter-evidence.
- **Differentiation:** support: a paragraph frame and an evidence bank / stretch: include an interpretation and evaluate it
- **Materials:** evidence cards from the unit, writing frames, maps
- **Joy:** "Connection map" wall — thread string between the unit's places

**End-of-unit check:** An extended answer on medieval connections with at least three societies as evidence and a supported judgement, plus one source-evaluation question on the Catalan Atlas.

## Unit 3 — Encounters and empires, 1400–1700

**Essential question:** What happened when peoples from different continents met — and whose stories get told?

**Key vocabulary:** voyage, treasure fleet, Mexica (Aztec), Tenochtitlan, conquistador, codex, conquest, Columbian Exchange, epidemic, Mughal, tolerance, empire, colonisation, perspective

### Lesson 12 — Zheng He's treasure fleets
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe Zheng He's voyages (1405–1433) and explain why Ming China stopped them." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.14.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Decades before Columbus sailed with three small ships, a Chinese admiral led fleets of more than 200 ships as far as East Africa."
- **Key activity:** Map the seven voyages (Southeast Asia, India, Arabia, East Africa); compare the size of a treasure ship with Columbus's *Santa María* (scale drawing on the playground — noting that historians debate the exact size of the largest ships); discuss reasons the voyages stopped (cost, politics, priorities on the northern border).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might world history have been different if Ming China had continued its voyages?" → China might have built overseas trade networks or colonies, changing which powers dominated the oceans.
- **Differentiation:** support: a voyage map with dates / stretch: compare Zheng He's aims (prestige, tribute, trade) with Columbus's aims
- **Materials:** world map, voyage cards, chalk and tape measure for scale drawing
- **Joy:** Chalk the ships to scale on the playground

### Lesson 13 — Tenochtitlan: a city on a lake
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe Tenochtitlan and Mexica society using archaeological and written sources." (UK KS3 History — a non-European society; C3 D2.His.1.6-8)
- **Hook:** "When Spanish soldiers first saw Tenochtitlan in 1519, one wrote that it was like an enchanted vision from a story. What did they see?"
- **Key activity:** Examine a reconstruction of Tenochtitlan (founded around 1325 on an island in Lake Texcoco, with causeways, canals and chinampas — floating gardens); extracts from Bernal Díaz del Castillo's account; the Templo Mayor excavations in modern Mexico City.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it significant that a Spanish soldier described Tenochtitlan as grander than any city he had seen?" → It is evidence, from an enemy who had no reason to flatter it, that the Mexica had built an extraordinary city.
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled reconstruction image / stretch: explain what chinampas show about Mexica engineering and food supply
- **Materials:** reconstruction images, Díaz extracts, a map of the Valley of Mexico, photographs of the Templo Mayor
- **Joy:** Model a chinampa garden in a tray with soil and cress

### Lesson 14 — The conquest of Mexico: whose voices?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare Spanish and Indigenous sources about the conquest (1519–1521) and explain why the Spanish were able to win." (UK KS3 History — interpretations; C3 D2.His.6.6-8, D2.His.14.6-8)
- **Hook:** "How did a few hundred Spanish soldiers defeat a great empire? The usual answer — 'guns and horses' — leaves out the most important reasons."
- **Key activity:** Compare an extract from Bernal Díaz with Nahua accounts recorded in the Florentine Codex (compiled under Bernardino de Sahagún with Indigenous scholars); identify causes of the fall (thousands of Indigenous allies such as the Tlaxcalans, a smallpox epidemic, weapons, Malintzin as interpreter); rank causes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the smallpox epidemic a crucial but often forgotten cause of the fall of Tenochtitlan?" → It killed a huge proportion of the city's population during the siege, weakening its defence far more than the small number of Spanish soldiers could.
- **Differentiation:** support: cause cards with short explanations / stretch: evaluate the Florentine Codex as a source (written decades later, under Spanish supervision, but from Indigenous testimony)
- **Materials:** source extracts, images from the Florentine Codex, cause-ranking cards
- **Joy:** "Cause pyramid" building and defending

### Lesson 15 — The Columbian Exchange
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the Columbian Exchange and evaluate its consequences for different peoples." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.14.6-8; Geography link)
- **Hook:** "Before 1492, there were no tomatoes in Italy, no chillies in India, no potatoes in Ireland and no horses in the Americas."
- **Key activity:** Sort foods, animals, people and diseases by where they came from; draw arrows on a world map; discuss winners and losers (population growth from new crops in Asia and Europe; catastrophic epidemics in the Americas; the transatlantic slave trade, building on Year 5).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the Columbian Exchange described as both a blessing and a catastrophe?" → New crops fed growing populations worldwide, but diseases killed millions of Indigenous Americans and the exchange drove the enslavement of millions of Africans.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture sorting cards / stretch: explain why the potato may have helped European populations grow
- **Materials:** food and animal picture cards, a world map, real foods (tomato, potato, chilli, cocoa, maize)
- **Joy:** A "Columbian Exchange" snack tasting (check allergies)

### Lesson 16 — The Mughal Empire: Akbar and the Taj Mahal
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the Mughal emperor Akbar (reigned 1556–1605) ruled a diverse empire and evaluate the significance of Mughal culture." (UK KS3 History — a non-European society; C3 D2.His.2.6-8)
- **Hook:** "An emperor who could not read built one of the greatest libraries of his time and invited scholars of many faiths to debate in his court."
- **Key activity:** Examine Akbar's policies (abolishing the tax on non-Muslims, debates in the Ibadat Khana between followers of different religions); Mughal miniature paintings as sources; the Taj Mahal (built by Shah Jahan, 1630s–1650s) as a symbol.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might Akbar's religious tolerance have made his empire stronger?" → Most of his subjects were Hindu; treating them fairly won loyalty and brought talented people of all faiths into government.
- **Differentiation:** support: a guided reading of a miniature painting / stretch: compare Akbar's tolerance with religious conflict in Europe in the same century
- **Materials:** images of Mughal miniatures, the Taj Mahal and Fatehpur Sikri, a map of the Mughal Empire
- **Joy:** Create a miniature painting in the Mughal style (Art link)

### Lesson 17 — Unit 3 enquiry: encounters — exchange or exploitation?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use evidence from the unit to answer 'Were encounters between 1400 and 1700 more about exchange or exploitation?'" (UK KS3 History — extended writing; C3 D2.His.16.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Zheng He, Cortés, Akbar — three encounters, three outcomes."
- **Key activity:** Compare the three case studies on a judgement grid; write a structured answer with a judgement; peer review.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might your answer differ depending on whose perspective you take?" → The same encounter was an opportunity for some (Spanish crown, traders) and a catastrophe for others (the Mexica), so the answer depends on perspective.
- **Differentiation:** support: a writing frame and evidence bank / stretch: include historians' interpretations
- **Materials:** judgement grids, evidence cards, writing frames
- **Joy:** "Historians' panel" — each child argues from one case study

**End-of-unit check:** An extended answer weighing exchange vs exploitation with evidence from at least two case studies, and a comparison of a Spanish and an Indigenous source on the conquest.

## Unit 4 — The age of revolutions, 1750–1900

**Essential question:** How did revolutions in ideas, industry and politics change the lives of ordinary people?

**Key vocabulary:** revolution, Enlightenment, rights, liberty, equality, industrialisation, factory, steam engine, urbanisation, child labour, reform, independence, abolition, emancipation, modernisation, Meiji

### Lesson 18 — New ideas: the Enlightenment and rights
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain Enlightenment ideas about rights and reason and how they inspired revolutions." (UK KS3 History — ideas, political power; C3 D2.His.1.6-8)
- **Hook:** "'All men are created equal' — written in 1776 by a man who enslaved more than 600 people during his lifetime. How do we make sense of that?"
- **Key activity:** Introduce Enlightenment ideas (reason, natural rights, government by consent); read the famous line of the US Declaration of Independence (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789); discuss who was left out (enslaved people, women, Indigenous peoples) and how people used the same words to claim their rights later.
- **Check for understanding:** "How could the words of the Declaration of Independence be used by people it left out?" → They could argue that if all people are equal, they too deserve rights — as abolitionists and women's rights campaigners did.
- **Differentiation:** support: key phrases paraphrased / stretch: compare with Olympe de Gouges's *Declaration of the Rights of Woman* (1791)
- **Materials:** extracts of the two declarations, a timeline of revolutions
- **Joy:** "Class declaration of rights" — draft and vote

### Lesson 19 — The Industrial Revolution: what changed?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the causes and consequences of the Industrial Revolution, starting in Britain and spreading worldwide." (UK KS3 History — industrialisation; C3 D2.His.14.6-8)
- **Hook:** "For almost all of human history, most people farmed. Within about 150 years, millions lived in cities and worked in factories. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Causes card sort (coal and iron, steam power, cotton, capital from trade and empire, new machines, population growth); before-and-after images of Manchester; spread to Belgium, the USA, Germany and Japan; consequences for work, cities and the environment.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain one link between the Industrial Revolution and empire." → Raw cotton was grown by enslaved people in the Americas or bought from colonies, and factories sold finished cloth around the world — industry and empire fed each other.
- **Differentiation:** support: cause cards with pictures / stretch: rank causes and justify the most important
- **Materials:** cause cards, images of industrial cities, a map of industrialisation spreading
- **Joy:** "Factory line" simulation making paper products — how fast does division of labour work?

### Lesson 20 — Children in the Industrial Revolution
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use testimony from 19th-century inquiries to explain children's working lives and evaluate the sources." (UK KS3 History — sources; C3 D2.His.10.6-8)
- **Hook:** "At your age, you might have worked 12 hours a day in a mill."
- **Key activity:** Read extracts of children's testimony to the 1832 Sadler Committee and the 1842 Mines Commission (with illustrations of children in mines); evaluate provenance (Sadler wanted reform — did that shape the questions?); learn about reform laws (the 1833 Factory Act); compare with child labour today and the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might the Sadler Committee's evidence be both very useful and possibly one-sided?" → It records children's own words about real conditions, but the committee was set up by a reformer who may have chosen witnesses and questions to prove his case.
- **Differentiation:** support: shortened testimony with a glossary / stretch: explain how the evidence was used to change laws
- **Materials:** testimony extracts, illustrations from the 1842 report, UNCRC summary
- **Joy:** "Voices from the mill" — perform a testimony as a reader's theatre

### Lesson 21 — Revolutions of rights: America, France and Haiti
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare the causes and outcomes of the American (1775–1783), French (1789) and Haitian (1791–1804) revolutions." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.14.6-8, D2.His.15.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Three revolutions in 30 years. Only one ended slavery. Which one?"
- **Key activity:** Groups study one revolution each (causes, key people, outcome, who gained rights); teach the others; complete a comparison grid.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why was the Haitian Revolution the most radical of the three?" → Enslaved people freed themselves, ended slavery and founded an independent nation (1804) led by formerly enslaved people — it applied "liberty and equality" to everyone.
- **Differentiation:** support: a fact sheet per revolution / stretch: explain how the French Revolution's ideas helped spark the Haitian Revolution
- **Materials:** revolution fact sheets, comparison grids, a map of the Atlantic world
- **Joy:** "Jigsaw" expert teams teach each other

### Lesson 22 — Toussaint Louverture: why is Haiti's story less well known?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate the significance of the Haitian Revolution and explain why it has been under-represented in some histories." (UK KS3 History — significance and interpretations; C3 D2.His.2.6-8)
- **Hook:** "The first nation founded after a successful revolt of enslaved people. Why do so many people never learn about it?"
- **Key activity:** Toussaint Louverture's life and leadership (portraits, his letters); apply the significance criteria from Lesson 4; discuss the debt France forced Haiti to pay from 1825 for recognition; reasons for silence in European and American histories.
- **Check for understanding:** "Use two significance criteria to argue that the Haitian Revolution matters in world history." → e.g. Resulted in change (it ended slavery in a colony and inspired others) and Revealing (it shows the limits of the "rights" claimed in Europe and the US).
- **Differentiation:** support: a significance frame / stretch: explain how the 1825 indemnity affected Haiti's development for over a century
- **Materials:** portraits and letter extracts, significance criteria card
- **Joy:** Add Haiti to the class "significance wall"

### Lesson 23 — Abolition: the struggle against slavery
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how enslaved people, formerly enslaved writers and campaigners fought to end slavery, building on Year 5." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.3.6-8)
- **Hook:** "An autobiography that became a bestseller in 1789 helped change British law. Who wrote it?" (Olaudah Equiano)
- **Key activity:** Sources from Olaudah Equiano's *Interesting Narrative* (1789) and Frederick Douglass's *Narrative* (1845); the Wedgwood medallion; resistance by enslaved people; key abolition dates (British Empire 1833–1834; USA 1865; Brazil 1888); rank the factors that ended slavery.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it wrong to say that slavery was ended only by white politicians?" → Enslaved people resisted and rebelled, formerly enslaved writers like Equiano and Douglass persuaded the public, and campaigns were built by many people of all backgrounds.
- **Differentiation:** support: short source extracts with a glossary / stretch: evaluate which factor was most important and why
- **Materials:** source extracts, image of the Wedgwood medallion, factor cards
- **Joy:** Design a campaign badge for a cause you care about today

### Lesson 24 — Meiji Japan: modernising a nation, and Unit 4 enquiry
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Japan modernised rapidly after 1868 and write a judgement on 'Which revolution changed ordinary lives the most?'" (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.16.6-8)
- **Hook:** "In 1853, American warships arrived in Tokyo Bay. Within 50 years, Japan had railways, factories and a modern navy."
- **Key activity:** Short study of the Meiji Restoration (1868): railways (the first line opened in 1872), schools for all children, industrialisation, adopting and adapting foreign ideas; then plan and write a short judgement comparing the unit's revolutions.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did Japan's leaders choose to copy from Western countries, and what did they keep?" → They adopted industry, railways, schooling and military technology while keeping the emperor and many Japanese traditions.
- **Differentiation:** support: a writing frame for the judgement / stretch: compare Meiji Japan's industrialisation with Britain's
- **Materials:** images of Meiji Japan (woodblock prints of railways), comparison grids, writing frames
- **Joy:** Woodblock-print style image of a train (Art link)

**End-of-unit check:** A written judgement on which revolution changed ordinary lives most, supported by evidence from at least two revolutions, plus a source evaluation of a piece of child-labour testimony.

## Unit 5 — The twentieth-century world

**Essential question:** How did the twentieth century's wars and freedom movements shape the world we live in today?

**Key vocabulary:** world war, alliance, trench, empire, Holocaust, genocide, United Nations, human rights, decolonisation, independence, partition, non-violence, civil rights, segregation, apartheid, Cold War, superpower, space race, Berlin Wall

### Lesson 25 — The First World War: a world war
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why the First World War (1914–1918) was a world war, including the contributions of soldiers and workers from Asia, Africa and elsewhere." (UK KS3 History — challenges for Britain, Europe and the wider world; C3 D2.His.14.6-8)
- **Hook:** "About 1.3 million soldiers from India served in the First World War. Why were they fighting in a war that began in Europe?"
- **Key activity:** Causes (alliances, empires, the assassination in Sarajevo in 1914); a world map of participants and theatres (Europe, Middle East, Africa, Pacific); soldiers and labourers from India, Africa, China, the Caribbean, Australia and New Zealand; letters home as sources; remembrance around the world.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did a war that began in Europe involve people on every inhabited continent?" → European powers ruled large empires and drew soldiers, workers and resources from their colonies; fighting spread to colonies and to other regions.
- **Differentiation:** support: a map with labels and fact cards / stretch: explain long-term and short-term causes separately
- **Materials:** world map of 1914, letter extracts, photographs of soldiers from many countries
- **Joy:** Write a letter home from a soldier or worker from one country (historical empathy)

### Lesson 26 — The Second World War and the Holocaust
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can outline the global course of the Second World War (1939–1945) and explain what the Holocaust was, through the testimony of Anne Frank." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.1.6-8)
- **Hook:** "A 13-year-old girl received a diary for her birthday in 1942. It became one of the most-read books in the world."
- **Key activity:** A global overview timeline (Europe, the Pacific, North Africa; the war ending in 1945 after the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki); the Holocaust: the murder of about six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, alongside Roma, disabled people and others; read carefully chosen entries from *The Diary of a Young Girl*; discuss why we remember.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is Anne Frank's diary such an important source?" → It gives the voice of one real young person, showing the human cost of Nazi persecution in a way statistics cannot; it is personal testimony written at the time.
- **Differentiation:** support: a teacher-led reading with discussion prompts / stretch: research an act of rescue (e.g. Miep Gies, who helped hide the Frank family, or Chiune Sugihara, who issued visas to Jewish refugees in Lithuania)
- **Materials:** *The Diary of a Young Girl* (Anne Frank) selected entries (teacher-chosen; use the Anne Frank House's education materials), a world timeline of WWII, photographs of the Secret Annex. **Parent note:** inform families before this lesson; builds on Year 5's Kindertransport lesson; no graphic images.
- **Joy:** (Sensitive lesson) — end with "upstanders": children create a tribute card to a rescuer, focusing on courage and humanity

### Lesson 27 — The United Nations and human rights
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why the United Nations (1945) and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) were created and how they are used today." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.Civ.10.6-8)
- **Hook:** "After two world wars, representatives of many nations wrote down rights that belong to every human being. What would you put on the list?"
- **Key activity:** Children list rights they think everyone should have; compare with the UDHR's 30 articles (simplified version); learn about Eleanor Roosevelt's role chairing the drafting committee and contributors from many countries (e.g. Hansa Mehta of India, who helped change "all men" to "all human beings"); link to the UNCRC (Life Skills).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did Hansa Mehta want Article 1 to say 'All human beings' rather than 'All men'?" → To make clear that rights belong to women as well as men — the wording affects who is included.
- **Differentiation:** support: a simplified UDHR / stretch: evaluate one article that is still not achieved everywhere and why
- **Materials:** simplified UDHR, UN flag and map of member states
- **Joy:** "Human rights charter" for the class

### Lesson 28 — Independence for India and Pakistan (1947)
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how India won independence, including Gandhi's methods of non-violent resistance, and the human cost of Partition." (UK KS3 History — the end of empire; C3 D2.His.14.6-8)
- **Hook:** "In 1930, Gandhi walked about 385 km to the sea to make salt. Why would walking to the sea challenge an empire?"
- **Key activity:** The Salt March as a case study in non-violent protest (breaking the British salt tax); other leaders (Nehru, Jinnah, Sarojini Naidu); independence on 15 August 1947 and Partition (millions displaced, great violence — told sensitively); link to the civil rights movement later.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why was the Salt March such an effective protest?" → Salt was something everyone needed, so the tax affected everyone; peaceful law-breaking exposed the injustice and won support worldwide.
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture-story sequence of the Salt March / stretch: explain why independence and Partition were both celebrated and mourned
- **Materials:** images and newsreel stills of the Salt March, a map of Partition, Gandhi quotation cards
- **Joy:** Plan a peaceful protest for a school issue (with rules for respectful campaigning)

### Lesson 29 — African independence: Ghana and beyond
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Ghana became one of the first sub-Saharan African countries to win independence from colonial rule (1957) and describe the wave of independence that followed." (UK KS3 History — the end of empire; C3 D2.His.14.6-8)
- **Hook:** "At midnight on 6 March 1957, Kwame Nkrumah told a huge crowd in Accra that Ghana was free 'for ever'."
- **Key activity:** Read an extract of Nkrumah's independence speech; map African independence dates (1960 — the "Year of Africa", when 17 African nations became independent); discuss the challenges new nations faced (borders drawn by colonial powers, economies built for export).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did many African countries face difficulties after independence?" → Colonial powers had drawn borders that ignored peoples and languages and had built economies around exporting raw materials, not developing local industry or education.
- **Differentiation:** support: a coloured map activity / stretch: compare Ghana's route to independence with India's
- **Materials:** a map of Africa with independence dates, Nkrumah speech extract, flag cards
- **Joy:** Colour the "independence map" decade by decade

### Lesson 30 — The US civil rights movement
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the methods and achievements of the US civil rights movement using sources, and connect it to Year 6 English texts." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.3.6-8)
- **Hook:** "In 1960, six-year-old Ruby Bridges walked into an all-white school in New Orleans, escorted by federal marshals."
- **Key activity:** Sources: photographs (Ruby Bridges, the Montgomery bus boycott, the 1963 March on Washington); Rosa Parks (1955) and the boycott's organisation; the Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965); link to *Brown Girl Dreaming* and "I Have a Dream" from English.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why was the Montgomery bus boycott successful?" → It was organised, non-violent and sustained for over a year; the bus company lost money and the case reached the Supreme Court, which ruled bus segregation unconstitutional.
- **Differentiation:** support: a photograph analysis frame / stretch: evaluate the role of ordinary people vs famous leaders
- **Materials:** photographs, *Ruby Bridges: Through My Eyes* (Ruby Bridges), timeline cards
- **Joy:** "Photograph detectives" — what can one image tell us?

### Lesson 31 — Ending apartheid: Nelson Mandela and South Africa
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what apartheid was and how it was ended through resistance inside South Africa and pressure from outside." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.14.6-8)
- **Hook:** "In 1964, Nelson Mandela was sentenced to life imprisonment. In 1994, he became South Africa's president. How?"
- **Key activity:** Apartheid laws (pass laws, separate areas and schools); Sharpeville (1960) and the Soweto uprising (1976, led by students); international sanctions and boycotts (including sport); Mandela's release (1990) and the first democratic election (1994); the Truth and Reconciliation Commission; link to Mandela's speech in English Unit 4.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the Soweto uprising important for children to know about?" → Students not much older than them led a protest against being taught in Afrikaans, showing young people's power to change history — and many were killed, which increased world pressure.
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture timeline / stretch: evaluate internal resistance vs international pressure as causes of apartheid's end
- **Materials:** timeline cards, photographs, extracts from *Long Walk to Freedom* (abridged children's edition)
- **Joy:** "Ballot box" re-enactment of the 1994 election queues

### Lesson 32 — The Cold War and the space race
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what the Cold War was and how rivalry between the USA and the USSR drove the space race, ending with the fall of the Berlin Wall." (UK KS3 History; C3 D2.His.14.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Two superpowers never fought each other directly — but raced each other to space. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Map the two blocs; the Berlin Wall (1961–1989) through photographs; space race milestones (Sputnik 1957, Yuri Gagarin 1961, Valentina Tereshkova 1963, Apollo 11 in 1969); the fall of the Wall on 9 November 1989 and the end of the USSR (1991).
- **Check for understanding:** "How was the space race part of the Cold War?" → Each superpower wanted to prove its system and technology were best; rockets also showed military power, so space achievements were a way to compete without direct war.
- **Differentiation:** support: a milestone card sort / stretch: evaluate whether the space race brought benefits to the whole world
- **Materials:** Cold War map, Berlin Wall photographs, space-race timeline cards
- **Joy:** "Newsroom 1989": write the headline for the night the Wall fell

### Lesson 33 — Unit 5 enquiry: the most significant change of the twentieth century
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can argue which change of the twentieth century was most significant, using criteria and evidence." (UK KS3 History — significance and extended writing; C3 D2.His.16.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Human rights, independence movements, the end of apartheid, space travel — which matters most for your life today?"
- **Key activity:** Apply the 5 Rs significance criteria to four developments; debate; write a structured argument with a judgement.
- **Check for understanding:** "What makes your judgement convincing?" → Criteria are applied consistently, with specific evidence, and the counter-argument is answered.
- **Differentiation:** support: a writing frame and evidence bank / stretch: argue that significance depends on perspective, with examples
- **Materials:** significance criteria cards, evidence bank, writing frames
- **Joy:** "Significance showdown" debate

**End-of-unit check:** A significance argument about a twentieth-century development with criteria and evidence, and a source-analysis question on a photograph or testimony from the unit.

## Unit 6 — My history enquiry: an oral history

**Essential question:** How has the world changed within living memory — and how can I record history myself?

**Key vocabulary:** oral history, interview, testimony, memory, reliability, corroboration, change, continuity, archive

### Lesson 34 — Planning and conducting an oral-history interview
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan and conduct an interview with an older person about how the world has changed in their lifetime." (UK KS3 History — local and personal history; C3 D2.His.10.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Your grandparents are living sources. What do they remember that no textbook records?"
- **Key activity:** Choose a theme (school, technology, a major event they remember, migration, childhood); write open questions; practise interviewing with a partner; conduct the interview at home (recorded with permission); note how memory can be reliable or unreliable and how to corroborate details (photos, documents, other people).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is an oral history both valuable and something to check against other sources?" → It records personal experience no one else has, but memory can fade or change over time, so details should be corroborated.
- **Differentiation:** support: a question bank to choose from / stretch: interview two people about the same event and compare
- **Materials:** interview planning sheets, recording device, consent form
- **Joy:** Hearing family stories for the first time

### Lesson 35 — Presenting my oral history at the graduation exhibition
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can turn my interview into a short history presentation that places personal memories in the wider history I have learned." (UK KS3 History; IB MYP Criterion C)
- **Hook:** "Your family's story is part of world history."
- **Key activity:** Select key quotations; connect them to the year's history (e.g. independence, migration, technology, the end of the Cold War); create a display (timeline, quotations, photographs with permission); present at the graduation exhibition (links to the cross-subject capstone).
- **Check for understanding:** "How does your interview connect to the wider history you studied this year?" → A specific link between the personal testimony and a historical development.
- **Differentiation:** support: a display template / stretch: a short audio documentary with narration
- **Materials:** display boards, printed photographs (with permission), audio editing app (optional)
- **Joy:** Family members visiting the exhibition to hear their own stories told

**End-of-unit check:** The oral-history presentation, assessed on quality of questions, use of testimony as evidence, awareness of reliability and connection to wider history.

- ***A Little History of the World* by E. H. Gombrich** — a warm, story-like tour of world history written for young readers; ideal background reading all year.
- ***The Silk Roads: A New History of the World — Illustrated Edition* by Peter Frankopan (illustrated by Neil Packer)** — an award-winning historian retells world history from the east, perfect for Unit 2.

- ***Ruby Bridges: Through My Eyes* by Ruby Bridges** — her own account of desegregating a school, with photographs; perfect for Lesson 30.

- ***I Am Malala* (Young Readers Edition) by Malala Yousafzai with Patricia McCormick** — history in the making: education and human rights (links with English).
- ***Horrible Histories* series by Terry Deary** — funny, well-researched books that draw reluctant readers into history.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Chronology:** places major events from every continent on a timeline and uses BCE/CE and centuries correctly. *See it:* the Unit 1 timeline task.
2. **Source evaluation:** explains how provenance affects a source's usefulness for a stated question. *See it:* the Catalan Atlas and Sadler testimony evaluations.
3. **Interpretations:** explains why historians disagree (evidence, perspective, time of writing) and evaluates an interpretation. *See it:* the Columbus, Mongol and Great Zimbabwe lessons.
4. **Causation:** distinguishes long- and short-term causes and ranks them with reasons. *See it:* the conquest of Mexico cause pyramid.
5. **Significance:** uses explicit criteria to judge significance and recognises that it depends on perspective. *See it:* the Unit 5 enquiry.
6. **Knowledge of a connected world:** can explain at least one society from each of Asia, Africa, the Americas and Europe and how they were connected. *See it:* the Unit 2 and 3 enquiries.
7. **Extended writing:** writes a structured answer that reaches a supported judgement. *See it:* the unit enquiries.
8. **Historical enquiry:** designs and carries out an oral-history enquiry and connects personal testimony to wider history. *See it:* the Unit 6 presentation.

## Teacher guidance

- **Sensitive history.** Conquest, slavery, the Holocaust, Partition and apartheid involve great suffering. Teach them truthfully but age-appropriately: centre human dignity, agency and resistance, not graphic detail; prepare children beforehand; give time to talk; inform parents of the Holocaust lesson. Never role-play perpetrators or victims of atrocities. The Holocaust is introduced through Anne Frank and rescuers here; in-depth study belongs in secondary school (UK Year 9).
- **Common misconceptions:** history is "what happened" rather than an evidence-based interpretation; primary sources are always reliable and secondary sources aren't; people in the past were less intelligent; progress is always steady and upward; Africa and the Americas "had no history" before Europeans (challenged directly in Units 2–3); change happens because of single great individuals (always include ordinary people's agency).
- **Local adaptation.** Every unit works in any country; add a lesson connection to your own country's history (e.g. how your country was affected by the Silk Roads, empires, the world wars or independence movements) in the flexible week.
- **Differentiation.** Support means shorter sources with glossaries, sentence frames and picture timelines; stretch means evaluating interpretations, comparing across societies and writing judgements that weigh counter-evidence.
- **Vertical alignment.** Earlier years built chronology and single-civilisation depth studies (Year 4: the Vikings, Baghdad, Benin, the Maya); Year 5 covered 1450–1950 — the Ottomans, the Renaissance and printing, the transatlantic slave trade and resistance, flight, the Second World War home front and votes for women — with reliability, usefulness and "whose voice?". Year 6 retrieves these briefly rather than re-teaching them. Year 6 adds global connections, formal provenance, interpretations and extended judgements, preparing for secondary history (UK Year 8 / US Grade 7 / MYP 2), which assumes children can evaluate sources and write a supported argument.
- **Cross-curricular links:** English (speeches of King and Mandela; *Brown Girl Dreaming*; *A Christmas Carol* and the Industrial Revolution; *A Long Walk to Water* and South Sudan), Geography (trade, empire and development), Maths (timelines and intervals, Hindu–Arabic numerals), Art (Mughal miniatures, woodblock prints), Life Skills (human rights, the UNCRC).
