# Year 6 Music (ages 11–12) — Scope & Sequence

> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Built to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Aligned to the **UK National Curriculum
> Key Stage 3 Music** (Year 7) and the **UK Model Music Curriculum (Year 7)**, the **US National Core
> Arts Standards (Music, Grade 6: Creating, Performing, Responding, Connecting)**, **IB MYP Year 1
> Arts (Music)** and **ABRSM/Trinity Grade 1 theory** as a notation benchmark.

## Year overview

In the graduation year children become **independent musicians**: they read standard notation for
rhythm and pitch, play melody *and* chords on a keyboard (with ukulele as an alternative), understand
how harmony and structure work, improvise over the blues, listen analytically to music from around the
world and across film and concert traditions, and write and record a song of their own. The year ends
with a **graduation concert**, the music strand of the graduation capstone.

**The instrument pathway.** Earlier years moved from untuned percussion to recorder and ukulele and
then to keyboard basics. In Year 6 **keyboard is the main instrument** (a 61-key keyboard or a free
piano app is enough), with **ukulele** as a parallel route for chords; every lesson includes singing
or playing.

**What we assume from Years 4 and 5:** singing in parts (rounds, partner songs, harmony in thirds, a
descant, three-part singing); ukulele in C and G with fingerpicking, TAB and a reggae strum; note values,
dotted notes, ties, syncopation and time signatures including 5/4 (Holst's "Mars", Year 4) and 6/8;
treble-stave reading; keyboard basics (five-finger melody, C, F and G triads, hands together); world
traditions — samba, djembe, gamelan, raga and tala (Year 4), taiko, kora, siku hocket, Cuban son and
maqam (Year 5); opera, Peking opera and musical theatre, and setting words to music (Year 5).
**What Year 6 adds:** irregular metres (7/8), triplets and swing; the bass clef and grand staff; major
scales with fingering, major and minor triads, primary chords and common progressions; the 12-bar
blues and improvisation; deeper study of raga and tala, West African cross-rhythm, gamelan
interlocking and Afro-Brazilian samba-reggae, and fusion; orchestral timbre, programme and film music
with leitmotif; songwriting with music technology. **What secondary relies on:**
notation literacy, keyboard and chord fluency, structural listening and confidence in composing with
technology.

**Listening log.** Every lesson begins with 5 minutes of focused listening to a named piece, recorded
in a listening journal (title, composer/performer, place and date, instruments, one musical feature,
personal response). One lesson a week, 40–45 minutes.

## Unit 1 — Rhythm, metre and notation

**Essential question:** How is rhythm organised and written down so that anyone, anywhere, can play it?

**Key vocabulary:** pulse, metre, simple time, compound time, irregular metre, 5/4, 7/8, grouping, triplet, swing, straight, ostinato, phase, stave, treble clef, bass clef, grand staff, ledger line

### Lesson 1 — Irregular metre: 5/4 and 7/8
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can feel, count and perform music in 5/4 and 7/8, explaining how the beats are grouped." (UK KS3 Music — staff notation; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.6)
- **Hook:** Listening: Dave Brubeck Quartet's "Take Five" (by Paul Desmond, 1959) and a Macedonian *lesnoto* dance in 7/8 — "Can you dance to a bar with an odd number of beats?"
- **Key activity:** Five-minute retrieval of note values and simple/compound time (Years 4–5 rhythm flashcards). New: irregular metres are counted in uneven groups — 5/4 as 3+2 (as in "Take Five"), 7/8 as 3+2+2 (*lesnoto*) or 2+2+3; clap the groupings with accents, step them, and read and perform rhythms in each.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does 7/8 feel 'limping' compared with 6/8, even though they have almost the same number of quavers?" → 6/8 divides evenly into two groups of three; 7/8 has uneven groups (e.g. 3+2+2), so one group is longer and the beat feels lopsided.
- **Differentiation:** support: count aloud with the accented numbers written (1-2-3-1-2-1-2) / stretch: write a 4-bar rhythm in 7/8 with a different grouping and perform it
- **Materials:** rhythm flashcards, whiteboards with staves, drums or body percussion, recordings of "Take Five" and a *lesnoto* dance
- **Joy:** "Odd-time dance" — step the 7/8 groups in a circle

### Lesson 2 — Triplets and swing
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read and perform triplets and play the same rhythm 'straight' and 'swung', explaining the difference." (UK KS3 Music; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.6)
- **Hook:** Listening: Duke Ellington's "It Don't Mean a Thing (If It Ain't Got That Swing)" (1932) after Scott Joplin's "The Entertainer" (Year 5): "Both are syncopated — so why does one *swing*?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of Year 5 syncopation; triplets (three notes in the time of two, written with a '3'); clap 'straight' quavers, then swing them (long–short, like the first and last notes of a triplet); play a simple riff both ways; prepares the blues (Unit 3).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between straight and swung quavers?" → Straight quavers are equal; swung quavers are uneven — the first of each pair is longer (roughly the first two-thirds of a triplet) and the second shorter.
- **Differentiation:** support: speak "tri-pl-et" and "swing-a" word rhythms before clapping / stretch: notate a swung riff using triplets and perform it against a straight beat
- **Materials:** rhythm cards, percussion, keyboards, recordings of Ellington and Joplin
- **Joy:** "Swing switch" — the class flips from straight to swing on the teacher's signal

### Lesson 3 — Minimalism: Steve Reich's Clapping Music
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a phasing rhythm and explain how minimalist music builds from a simple repeated pattern." (UK KS3 Music — listening; NCAS MU:Re7.2.6)
- **Hook:** Listening: Steve Reich's *Clapping Music* (1972) — "Two people, two hands each, one pattern. How can this be a whole piece?"
- **Key activity:** Learn the 12-quaver pattern; one group keeps it steady while the other shifts one quaver at a time (phasing); discuss minimalism (repetition, gradual change).
- **Check for understanding:** "What changes and what stays the same in *Clapping Music*?" → The pattern stays the same; the second part shifts by one quaver at a time, so the combination changes until the parts line up again.
- **Differentiation:** support: perform only the steady part / stretch: perform the shifting part and write the first three shifts in notation
- **Materials:** recording and score of *Clapping Music*
- **Joy:** Surviving all 12 shifts together

### Lesson 4 — The bass clef and the grand staff
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can name notes in the bass clef and find them on the keyboard, and read a simple left-hand part on the grand staff." (UK KS3 Music — staff notation; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.6)
- **Hook:** "Your left hand has been playing without its own clef. Meet the bass clef — the same five lines, completely different notes."
- **Key activity:** Retrieve treble-stave notes from Year 5 with a 60-second sight-reading of "Sakura Sakura" (Japan). New: bass-clef lines G B D F A ("Good Boys Deserve Fudge Always") and spaces A C E G ("All Cows Eat Grass"); the grand staff, with middle C on a ledger line between the two staves; read and play a simple left-hand bass line (e.g. the Pachelbel ground bass for Lesson 9) from notation.
- **Check for understanding:** "The bottom line of the stave is E in the treble clef but G in the bass clef. Why does the same line mean different notes?" → The clef tells you which notes the lines stand for; the bass clef fixes the F below middle C on its fourth line, so every line and space has a different name from the treble clef.
- **Differentiation:** support: letter names written under the first line of bass notes and a grand-staff keyboard chart / stretch: sight-read a two-hand melody-and-bass exercise
- **Materials:** keyboards or glockenspiels, grand-staff flashcards, notation for "Sakura Sakura" and a simple bass line
- **Joy:** "Clef swap" — the same pattern of dots read in treble then bass sounds like a different tune

### Lesson 5 — Unit 1 performance and check
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a short rhythmic ensemble piece from notation and compose a 4-bar rhythm in a given time signature." (UK KS3 Music; NCAS MU:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Your rhythm, on the stave, played by your friends."
- **Key activity:** Compose a 4-bar rhythm (including a triplet or a syncopation) in a chosen metre; swap with a partner to perform; build into a class rhythmic ensemble with ostinati; add a short bass-clef line on keyboards.
- **Check for understanding:** Listening to the performance: "Did your partner play your rhythm the way you imagined? If not, what in the notation caused the difference?" → The child identifies a notation detail (e.g. a missing dot or unclear beaming) and corrects it.
- **Differentiation:** support: 3/4 or 6/8 with a note-value bank / stretch: 5/4 or 7/8 with a triplet or swung figure
- **Materials:** manuscript paper or notation app, percussion
- **Joy:** Class rhythm orchestra

**End-of-unit check:** Perform a 4-bar rhythm in an irregular metre from notation accurately; perform a riff straight and swung; name ten notes across the treble and bass staves; write a 4-bar rhythm with correct bar totals; describe how *Clapping Music* uses phasing.

## Unit 2 — Keyboard and harmony

**Essential question:** How do chords work, and how can I use them to accompany a melody?

**Key vocabulary:** keyboard, octave, tone, semitone, sharp, flat, major scale, fingering, triad, root, third, fifth, chord, primary chords (I, IV, V), chord progression, ground bass, broken chord, accompaniment

### Lesson 6 — Scales and keys: the major-scale pattern
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play a one-octave C major scale with thumb-under fingering and use the tone–semitone pattern to build another major scale." (UK KS3 Music — keyboard; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.6)
- **Hook:** "In Year 5 your hand stayed in five-finger position. How do pianists play eight notes with five fingers?"
- **Key activity:** Two-minute retrieval of keyboard geography and the five-finger position from Year 5. New: tones and semitones (the major scale pattern T-T-S-T-T-T-S); right-hand C major scale with fingering 1-2-3-1-2-3-4-5 (thumb under after E); left hand one octave; build G major and find why it needs F sharp.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where are the semitones in the C major scale, and how can you see them on the keyboard?" → Between E–F and B–C, where there is no black key between the white keys.
- **Differentiation:** support: coloured stickers on keys and a five-finger position first / stretch: build a G major scale (needs F sharp) using the tone–semitone pattern
- **Materials:** keyboards (or piano apps), headphones, key stickers, scale fingering chart
- **Joy:** Play the scale in contrary motion — hands moving apart — and hear the mirror

### Lesson 7 — Building triads
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can build major and minor triads on any white note and hear the difference." (UK KS3 Music — harmony; NCAS MU:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** Play C major then C minor: "One note changed. Why does it sound so different?"
- **Key activity:** Build triads by "play one, miss one" (C–E–G); find all white-note triads; identify which are major and which minor by ear and by counting semitones (major third = 4 semitones; minor third = 3).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between C major and C minor on the keyboard?" → The middle note (the third) is lowered by a semitone: E becomes E flat.
- **Differentiation:** support: triad shapes marked with stickers / stretch: build triads with black notes (e.g. D major needs F sharp)
- **Materials:** keyboards, triad worksheets
- **Joy:** "Happy or sad?" chord-guessing game

### Lesson 8 — Primary chords I, IV, V
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play the primary chords (I, IV, V) in C major and use them to accompany a simple melody." (UK KS3 Music — harmony; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.6)
- **Hook:** "Three chords and the truth": hundreds of folk and pop songs use only three chords.
- **Key activity:** Play C, F and G chords (left hand) in simple positions; accompany "Twinkle Twinkle"-level melodies and then a folk song (e.g. "Kumbaya" or "Oh When the Saints"), changing chord where the melody note fits.
- **Check for understanding:** "How do you decide which chord fits under a melody note?" → Choose the chord that contains that note (or most of the bar's strong notes).
- **Differentiation:** support: ukulele route (C, F, G7 chord shapes) or single root notes in the left hand / stretch: use inversions for smooth movement between chords
- **Materials:** keyboards or ukuleles, chord charts, song sheets
- **Joy:** Singing while accompanying yourself

### Lesson 9 — The ground bass: Pachelbel's Canon
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a repeating bass line (ground bass) and explain how a canon and a chord progression work together." (UK KS3 Music — listening; NCAS MU:Re7.2.6)
- **Hook:** Listening: Johann Pachelbel's Canon in D (c. 1700) — "The bass plays the same eight notes 28 times. Why doesn't it get boring?"
- **Key activity:** Learn the ground bass (D–A–B–F♯–G–D–G–A) on keyboard or tuned percussion; the upper parts enter one after another (canon); discuss how many modern songs use a similar progression.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is a ground bass?" → A repeated bass line over which other parts change and develop.
- **Differentiation:** support: play only the first four notes of the ground bass / stretch: play the bass with the left hand and the chords with the right
- **Materials:** keyboards, glockenspiels, recording of Pachelbel's Canon, bass-line score
- **Joy:** Class performance of the Canon in layers

### Lesson 10 — The "four-chord" progression in popular music
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play the I–V–vi–IV progression and recognise it in popular songs from different countries." (UK KS3 Music — popular music; NCAS MU:Re7.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Can you name ten songs that use exactly the same four chords?"
- **Key activity:** Play C–G–Am–F; listen to several songs (from different decades and countries) using it; sing a song over it; discuss why this progression is so popular (strong movement, emotional minor chord).
- **Check for understanding:** "Which chord in C–G–Am–F is minor, and what does it add?" → A minor (vi); it adds a touch of emotion or sadness to the bright major chords.
- **Differentiation:** support: ukulele or root notes only / stretch: play in a broken-chord (arpeggio) pattern and transpose to G major (G–D–Em–C)
- **Materials:** keyboards, ukuleles, chord charts, selected songs
- **Joy:** "Four-chord medley" sing-along

### Lesson 11 — Unit 2 performance: melody and accompaniment
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a melody with a chord accompaniment (hands together or in a pair) with accurate timing." (UK KS3 Music — performance; NCAS MU:Pr6.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Two hands, one piece of music."
- **Key activity:** Rehearse a short arrangement (e.g. "Ode to Joy" with I–V chords, or a folk song with I–IV–V); perform to the class; peer feedback on timing and balance.
- **Check for understanding:** Listening back: "Where did the chord change, and did it arrive on time?" → The child identifies the chord changes and evaluates the timing.
- **Differentiation:** support: perform as a pair (one melody, one chords) / stretch: hands together with a broken-chord accompaniment
- **Materials:** keyboards, arrangements, recording device
- **Joy:** Recording your first two-handed performance

**End-of-unit check:** Play a C major scale with correct fingering; build major and minor triads; perform a melody with I–IV–V accompaniment; recognise a ground bass or the four-chord progression by ear.

## Unit 3 — The blues: structure and improvisation

**Essential question:** How did the blues become the root of so much modern music — and how can I improvise within it?

**Key vocabulary:** blues, 12-bar blues, chord progression, walking bass, swing, blues scale, blue note, improvisation, call and response, riff, lyrics (AAB), verse

### Lesson 12 — The roots of the blues
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the origins of the blues in African American communities in the southern United States and identify its main features." (UK KS3 Music — history and listening; NCAS MU:Cn11.0.6)
- **Hook:** Listening: B.B. King's "The Thrill Is Gone" (1969) and a field holler: "What connects them?"
- **Key activity:** History (roots in West African musical traditions, work songs and spirituals; the Mississippi Delta; the migration north to Chicago); listen for blue notes, call and response and AAB lyrics; link to History Unit 4 (slavery and abolition) and Unit 5 (civil rights).
- **Check for understanding:** "What does 'AAB' mean in blues lyrics?" → The first line is sung, repeated (perhaps with a small change), then answered by a different, rhyming line.
- **Differentiation:** support: a listening grid with pictures / stretch: trace the blues' influence on rock and roll and soul
- **Materials:** recordings (field holler, Bessie Smith, B.B. King), map of the USA, listening grids
- **Joy:** Writing a class AAB verse about a school-life problem

### Lesson 13 — The 12-bar blues chord pattern
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play the 12-bar blues chord pattern (I–I–I–I / IV–IV–I–I / V–IV–I–I) in C." (UK KS3 Music — harmony; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.6)
- **Hook:** "One chord pattern — twelve bars — thousands of songs."
- **Key activity:** Play C, F and G in the 12-bar pattern; count bars aloud; add a swing rhythm; the class plays chords as a band while the teacher sings.
- **Check for understanding:** "In which bar does chord V first appear in the 12-bar blues?" → Bar 9.
- **Differentiation:** support: single root notes or ukulele / stretch: add a walking bass line or a boogie-woogie left-hand pattern
- **Materials:** keyboards, ukuleles, 12-bar chart, backing track
- **Joy:** "House band" jam

### Lesson 14 — The blues scale and improvisation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can improvise a short solo using the C blues scale over a 12-bar backing." (UK KS3 Music — improvising; NCAS MU:Cr1.1.6)
- **Hook:** "There are no wrong notes in the blues scale — only brave ones."
- **Key activity:** Learn the C blues scale (C–E♭–F–F♯–G–B♭–C); question-and-answer phrases with the teacher; improvise 2-bar then 4-bar phrases over a backing; use rests and repetition.
- **Check for understanding:** "What makes an improvised phrase sound musical rather than random?" → Repetition of a motif, rests for "breathing", a clear rhythm and ending on a strong note like C.
- **Differentiation:** support: use only three notes (C, E♭, G) / stretch: improvise a full 12-bar chorus with a planned shape
- **Materials:** keyboards, xylophones with blues-scale bars, backing track
- **Joy:** "Trading fours" — solo swaps around the class

### Lesson 15 — Writing a blues song
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write and perform a 12-bar blues verse with AAB lyrics, a riff and chords." (UK KS3 Music — composing; NCAS MU:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** "The 'I lost my homework' blues."
- **Key activity:** In groups: write AAB lyrics, choose a riff, play chords, perform with an improvised solo section.
- **Check for understanding:** Listening to the song: "Where is the AAB pattern in your lyrics, and how do the chords support it?" → Lines match the three 4-bar phrases (I, IV–I, V–IV–I).
- **Differentiation:** support: a lyric frame and a given riff / stretch: add a bridge or a stop-time break
- **Materials:** keyboards, ukuleles, percussion, lyric sheets
- **Joy:** The "blues club" performance with dimmed lights

### Lesson 16 — Unit 3 performance and appraisal
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform my blues song and appraise others using musical vocabulary." (UK KS3 Music; NCAS MU:Re9.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Critics' table."
- **Key activity:** Performances; peer appraisal (structure, rhythm, improvisation, ensemble); listening journal comparison of a blues and a rock and roll track.
- **Check for understanding:** In appraisal: "Name one musical feature in a partner's song that made it sound like the blues." → e.g. 12-bar structure, blue notes, swing rhythm or AAB lyrics.
- **Differentiation:** support: an appraisal frame / stretch: a written comparison of two performances
- **Materials:** recording device, appraisal sheets
- **Joy:** Class blues album recording

**End-of-unit check:** Perform the 12-bar blues chords in time, improvise a phrase with the blues scale, explain the blues' origins and identify its features in an unfamiliar recording.

## Unit 4 — Music of the world

**Essential question:** How do musical traditions around the world organise rhythm, melody and ensemble differently — and what can we learn from each?

**Key vocabulary:** raga, tala, drone, sitar, tabla, improvisation, polyrhythm, cross-rhythm, timeline (bell pattern), djembe, dundun, master drummer, gamelan, colotomic, balungan, kotekan, samba, samba-reggae, surdo, bloco, fusion

### Lesson 17 — Indian classical music: raga and drone
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what a raga and a drone are and improvise a melody over a drone using the notes of a raga." (UK KS3 Music — world music; NCAS MU:Re7.2.6)
- **Hook:** Listening: Ravi Shankar playing sitar (e.g. a recorded *alap*): "No chords, no bars — so what holds it together?"
- **Key activity:** A raga as a set of notes with rules and a mood (e.g. Raga Yaman in the evening); the tanpura drone; sing sargam (Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni Sa); improvise slowly over a drone.
- **Check for understanding:** "How is a raga different from a Western major scale?" → A raga has rules for how notes move, characteristic phrases, a mood and sometimes a time of day — not just a set of notes.
- **Differentiation:** support: improvise on three notes of the raga / stretch: shape a short *alap*-style improvisation that rises gradually
- **Materials:** keyboards or tuned percussion, a tanpura drone app or recording, recordings of Ravi Shankar
- **Joy:** Meditative improvisation over the drone

### Lesson 18 — Indian classical music: tala
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can clap *teentaal* (16 beats) and explain how a tala cycle works." (UK KS3 Music — world music; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.6)
- **Hook:** Listening: Zakir Hussain on tabla — "How can the fingers move that fast?"
- **Key activity:** Clap and wave *teentaal* (4+4+4+4, with *sam* on beat 1 and *khali* — the "empty" wave — on beat 9); learn simple tabla syllables (*bols*: "dha dhin dhin dha"); perform over a melody.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is *sam*, and why do musicians aim to land on it together?" → The first beat of the cycle; landing together shows the cycle is complete and creates excitement.
- **Differentiation:** support: clap only the beat-1 accents / stretch: recite a rhythmic *tihai* (a phrase repeated three times ending on *sam*)
- **Materials:** tabla recordings, tala chart, hand drums
- **Joy:** Landing on *sam* together

### Lesson 19 — West African drumming: polyrhythm
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform one part in a West African polyrhythmic drumming piece, keeping my part against others." (UK KS3 Music — world music; NCAS MU:Pr4.3.6)
- **Hook:** "In a West African drum ensemble, no single drum plays 'the rhythm' — the music is in how the parts fit."
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4's djembe strokes (bass, tone, slap). New: cross-rhythm — clap 3 against 2 (one hand three, the other two in the same time span); the timeline bell pattern (a 12-pulse bell rhythm of the kind used in Ewe music from Ghana) as the reference every drummer hears; then three interlocking parts (bell, djembe, dundun) of a traditional rhythm (e.g. *Kuku* from Guinea) with master-drummer calls.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is cross-rhythm, and why does the bell pattern help you keep your part?" → Two conflicting groupings of the beat played together (e.g. 3 against 2); the bell pattern is a fixed timeline that every player measures their part against, so they don't drift.
- **Differentiation:** support: the bell part (steadiest) / stretch: the master-drummer role with calls
- **Materials:** djembes or hand drums, cowbell or agogo, recordings of West African ensembles
- **Joy:** The drum circle

### Lesson 20 — Indonesian gamelan: cyclic music
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a simple gamelan-style piece with layered parts and a cyclic structure marked by a gong." (UK KS3 Music — world music; NCAS MU:Re7.2.6)
- **Hook:** Listening: Balinese *gamelan gong kebyar* then Javanese gamelan — "Why do they sound so different?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4's Balinese gamelan listening. New: the colotomic structure (gongs of different sizes marking points in the cycle, with the largest gong ending it); the *balungan* (core melody) in a pentatonic scale; Balinese *kotekan*, where two players (*polos* and *sangsih*) split a fast figuration so that neither plays it alone (compare Year 5's siku hocket); perform on glockenspiels with pentatonic notes.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does the large gong do in gamelan music?" → It marks the end (and beginning) of each cycle, like a musical full stop.
- **Differentiation:** support: the slow core melody / stretch: the fast decorating (interlocking) part
- **Materials:** glockenspiels and metallophones set to a pentatonic scale, a gong or cymbal, recordings of Balinese and Javanese gamelan
- **Joy:** The "gong moment" at the end of each cycle

### Lesson 21 — Samba-reggae: Olodum and Afro-Brazilian identity
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play a part in a samba-reggae groove and explain how it differs from the Rio samba played in Year 4 and why it was created." (UK KS3 Music — world music; NCAS MU:Pr4.3.6)
- **Hook:** Listening and video: the drum group Olodum in Salvador, Bahia — "It uses samba drums, but it sounds slower and heavier. Why did musicians in Salvador invent a new style in the 1980s?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4's Rio samba batucada (surdo pulse, caixa, tamborim, whistle breaks). New: samba-reggae from Salvador — several surdo parts playing interlocking melodic patterns, a reggae-influenced feel, snare patterns; its roots in Afro-Brazilian *blocos* celebrating Black identity and African heritage; play a layered samba-reggae groove with a break.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give two musical differences between samba-reggae and Rio samba, and one reason samba-reggae matters to the people who created it." → e.g. slower tempo and interlocking surdo melodies with a reggae feel; it was created by Afro-Brazilian groups in Salvador to celebrate Black identity and African heritage.
- **Differentiation:** support: the lowest surdo part / stretch: an interlocking surdo part or leading a break
- **Materials:** samba instruments or substitutes (large drums, buckets, shakers, cowbells), a whistle, video of Olodum
- **Joy:** A samba-reggae parade around the playground

### Lesson 22 — Fusion: when traditions meet, and unit check
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how musicians combine traditions (fusion) and compare two musical traditions using musical vocabulary." (UK KS3 Music — listening and appraising; NCAS MU:Cn11.0.6)
- **Hook:** Listening: Paul Simon with Ladysmith Black Mambazo ("Homeless", from *Graceland*, 1986) and Anoushka Shankar's sitar with other genres — "What happens when musical worlds meet?"
- **Key activity:** Identify elements from each tradition in fusion pieces; discuss respect and collaboration in fusion; written comparison of two traditions from the unit (structure, rhythm, melody, instruments, social role).
- **Check for understanding:** "Compare how rhythm is organised in Indian tala and in samba-reggae." → Tala is a long cycle (e.g. 16 beats) with *sam*; samba-reggae is a short repeated groove with interlocking drum patterns and breaks.
- **Differentiation:** support: a comparison grid / stretch: a paragraph evaluating the ethics and success of a fusion project
- **Materials:** recordings, comparison grids, listening journals
- **Joy:** Create a class "fusion" piece combining a drone, a tala and a samba groove

**End-of-unit check:** Perform one part in a polyrhythmic or cyclic ensemble; describe raga, tala, cross-rhythm, the gamelan cycle and kotekan, and samba-reggae; compare two traditions in writing using musical vocabulary.

## Unit 5 — Music that tells stories: programme and film music

**Essential question:** How can music, without words, tell a story or create a character?

**Key vocabulary:** programme music, leitmotif, motif, orchestra, orchestration, dynamics, crescendo, tempo, accelerando, ostinato, dissonance, consonance, major/minor, timbre, underscore, diegetic, non-diegetic, storyboard, click track

### Lesson 23 — Programme music: In the Hall of the Mountain King
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Grieg uses tempo, dynamics and orchestration to tell a story." (UK KS3 Music — listening; NCAS MU:Re7.2.6)
- **Hook:** Listening: Edvard Grieg's "In the Hall of the Mountain King" from *Peer Gynt* (1875) — "What's happening in the story? How do you know?"
- **Key activity:** Listen and draw a "story graph" of tempo and dynamics; identify the repeated motif, crescendo and accelerando; learn and play the motif on keyboards.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does Grieg create excitement towards the end?" → The same motif repeats louder (crescendo), faster (accelerando) and with more instruments, building to a climax.
- **Differentiation:** support: a listening map with pictures / stretch: annotate the entry of each instrument group
- **Materials:** recording, keyboards, story-graph sheets
- **Joy:** Act out the chase to the music

### Lesson 24 — The orchestra and timbre: Britten's Young Person's Guide
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify orchestral instruments and families by timbre and explain how a composer uses them to vary one theme." (UK KS3 Music — listening; NCAS MU:Re7.2.6; Science link)
- **Hook:** Listening: Benjamin Britten's *The Young Person's Guide to the Orchestra* (1946) — "One tune by Henry Purcell, played by the whole orchestra, then taken apart instrument by instrument. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Hear Purcell's theme (from *Abdelazer*) played by the full orchestra and then by each family; follow the variations for individual instruments with picture cards; link timbre to how sound is made (vibrating strings, air columns, struck skins — science link); compose a 4-bar motif and choose the instrument whose timbre suits it. (Year 4 met Holst's "Mars" for its 5/4 metre.)
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does the same theme sound so different on the tuba and on the piccolo?" → Each instrument has its own timbre (tone colour) and range, from how it makes sound and its size — so the same notes take on a different character.
- **Differentiation:** support: identify the four families from picture cards while listening / stretch: identify individual instruments (e.g. oboe, horn, timpani) and explain Britten's order
- **Materials:** recording and video of *The Young Person's Guide to the Orchestra*, instrument cards
- **Joy:** "Instrument bingo" as the variations go by

### Lesson 25 — Leitmotif in film: John Williams and beyond
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what a leitmotif is and how film composers use it to represent characters and ideas." (UK KS3 Music — film music; NCAS MU:Re7.2.6)
- **Hook:** Two notes: E–F, E–F, getting faster. "What's coming?" (the shark in *Jaws*, 1975)
- **Key activity:** Leitmotifs in John Williams's *Star Wars* scores (the main theme, "The Imperial March") and a film score from another country (e.g. Joe Hisaishi's music for Studio Ghibli films); how motifs change when a character changes; play the *Jaws* motif and a "hero" motif.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the *Jaws* motif so effective with only two notes?" → A semitone rising and falling, low and getting faster and louder, creates dread; it tells us the shark is near before we see it.
- **Differentiation:** support: match motifs to characters from picture cards / stretch: explain how a leitmotif is varied (major to minor, slower, different instrument) to show change
- **Materials:** film-music clips, keyboards, motif cards
- **Joy:** "Name that film" quiz

### Lesson 26 — Composing a soundtrack to a silent film clip
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compose and perform an underscore for a short silent film clip, matching music to action and mood." (UK KS3 Music — composing; NCAS MU:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** A 60-second clip from a silent comedy (e.g. Charlie Chaplin): "It needs music. You're the composer."
- **Key activity:** Storyboard the clip with timings; choose motifs for characters; plan dynamics, tempo and timbre for each section; rehearse with keyboards and percussion or music software.
- **Check for understanding:** Watching with the music: "Where does your music change with the action, and how?" → A specific moment where tempo, dynamics or timbre change to match what happens on screen.
- **Differentiation:** support: a storyboard with suggested moods / stretch: include a leitmotif and vary it
- **Materials:** silent film clip (public-domain), storyboard sheets, keyboards, percussion, music software
- **Joy:** "Premiere" — watch the clip with your live soundtrack

### Lesson 27 — Unit 5 performance and appraisal
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform my soundtrack and appraise others' using the vocabulary of dynamics, tempo, timbre and motif." (UK KS3 Music; NCAS MU:Re9.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Film festival awards."
- **Key activity:** Final performances with the clip; peer appraisal; listening journal comparing the class's soundtrack with the original (if one exists).
- **Check for understanding:** In appraisal: "Which soundtrack best matched the mood, and what musical choice did it?" → A specific musical feature (e.g. a sudden silence before the fall).
- **Differentiation:** support: an appraisal frame / stretch: a written review of a film score of choice
- **Materials:** performances, appraisal sheets
- **Joy:** "Oscars" for best soundtrack

**End-of-unit check:** Identify instrument families and explain how a composer creates character with tempo, dynamics, timbre and motif; compose and perform a soundtrack that matches action.

## Unit 6 — Songwriting and music technology

**Essential question:** How are songs built, and how can technology help me write, arrange and record one?

**Key vocabulary:** verse, chorus, pre-chorus, bridge, hook, lyrics, rhyme scheme, melody, chord progression, arrangement, DAW (digital audio workstation), track, loop, tempo (BPM), quantise, mix, volume, panning

### Lesson 28 — How songs are built
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify song structures (verse, chorus, bridge) and the hook in songs from different countries." (UK KS3 Music — popular music; NCAS MU:Re7.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Why does a chorus get stuck in your head?"
- **Key activity:** Map the structure of three songs from different countries and decades (e.g. a Beatles song, a K-pop song, a Latin pop song with age-appropriate lyrics); identify the hook; notice repetition and contrast.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between a verse and a chorus?" → Verses usually have different lyrics each time and tell the story; the chorus repeats the same words and melody and contains the hook.
- **Differentiation:** support: a structure-mapping template with colours / stretch: explain the job of the bridge (contrast before the final chorus)
- **Materials:** selected songs (lyrics checked for age-appropriateness), structure templates
- **Joy:** "Hook hunter" competition

### Lesson 29 — Writing lyrics
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a verse and a chorus with a clear message, a rhyme scheme and a singable rhythm." (UK KS3 Music — composing; NCAS MU:Cr1.1.6; English link)
- **Hook:** "What do you want to say to the world as you leave primary school?"
- **Key activity:** Choose a theme (friendship, growing up, a cause — possibly the capstone issue); brainstorm images; write a chorus (the message) and a verse (the story); check syllable rhythm by speaking over a beat.
- **Check for understanding:** "Speak your chorus over the beat. Where do the stressed syllables fall?" → Stressed syllables land on strong beats; the child adjusts words that feel awkward.
- **Differentiation:** support: a lyric frame and rhyme bank / stretch: use imagery and a surprising final line (English Unit 2 link)
- **Materials:** lyric sheets, rhyme dictionaries, a drum loop
- **Joy:** Writing a chorus the whole class wants to sing

### Lesson 30 — Melody and chords
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can set my lyrics to a melody over a chord progression, using chord notes on strong beats." (UK KS3 Music — composing; NCAS MU:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Your chorus needs a tune people will remember."
- **Key activity:** Choose a progression (e.g. I–V–vi–IV); improvise melodies over it while singing lyrics; record ideas on a phone; refine the best, making the chorus melody higher or more rhythmic than the verse.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a melody sound 'right' when strong beats use notes from the chord?" → Those notes are consonant with the harmony; other notes can pass between them on weaker beats.
- **Differentiation:** support: a given melody shape to adapt / stretch: a different progression for the verse and chorus
- **Materials:** keyboards, ukuleles, recording devices
- **Joy:** The "voice memo" moment when a tune clicks

### Lesson 31 — Arranging and recording in a DAW
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can arrange and record my song in a digital audio workstation with drums, bass, chords and vocals on separate tracks." (UK KS3 Music — music technology; NCAS MU:Cr3.2.6; Computing link)
- **Hook:** "Every song you hear on the radio was built track by track like this."
- **Key activity:** Use a free DAW (e.g. BandLab or GarageBand): set tempo; add a drum loop; record or program chords and a bass line; record vocals; arrange sections (verse, chorus, bridge).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it useful to record each instrument on a separate track?" → Each part can be edited, balanced and fixed on its own without re-recording everything.
- **Differentiation:** support: a template project with drums and chords ready / stretch: add a countermelody and automation (volume changes)
- **Materials:** tablets or computers with a DAW, headphones, a microphone
- **Joy:** Hearing your song produced

### Lesson 32 — Mixing, sharing and feedback
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can mix my song (balance, panning), share it and respond to feedback." (UK KS3 Music — music technology; NCAS MU:Pr6.1.6)
- **Hook:** "The same tracks can sound amateur or professional — it's the mix."
- **Key activity:** Balance volume so the vocal is clear; pan instruments; listening party; peer feedback; one improvement.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did you change in the mix after feedback, and why?" → A specific change (e.g. lowering the drums so the lyrics are clear).
- **Differentiation:** support: a mixing checklist / stretch: add effects (reverb) and explain their purpose
- **Materials:** DAW, headphones, feedback sheets
- **Joy:** Class "album launch" listening party

**End-of-unit check:** A recorded original song with verse and chorus, chords, melody and at least three tracks, and a short explanation of structure and mixing choices.

## Unit 7 — Graduation concert

**Essential question:** How do musicians prepare and give a performance that moves an audience?

**Key vocabulary:** programme, rehearsal, ensemble, balance, cue, stage presence, performance anxiety, encore, evaluation

### Lesson 33 — Planning the programme and rehearsing
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can help plan a concert programme and rehearse my part with attention to accuracy, balance and expression." (UK KS3 Music — performance; NCAS MU:Pr4.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Your graduation concert: the best of the year."
- **Key activity:** Choose items (a keyboard piece, the blues song, the samba or drumming piece, class songs, individual songwriting); design the running order; rehearse in sections; learn stage etiquette.
- **Check for understanding:** "What will you listen for in rehearsal to make the ensemble balanced?" → Whether the melody or vocals can be heard over the accompaniment and percussion.
- **Differentiation:** support: ensemble parts with the steadiest roles / stretch: solo or leadership roles, including counting in the band
- **Materials:** instruments, running-order sheet, rehearsal space
- **Joy:** Designing the concert programme booklet (Art link)

### Lesson 34 — Dress rehearsal and managing nerves
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform my part in a full run-through and use strategies to manage performance nerves." (UK KS3 Music; NCAS MU:Pr5.1.6; Life Skills link)
- **Hook:** "Even famous performers get nervous. What do they do?"
- **Key activity:** Breathing and grounding techniques (Life Skills link); full dress rehearsal with transitions; notes and fixes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which strategy will you use if you feel nervous before your item?" → A named strategy (e.g. slow breathing, focusing on the first bar, a friendly face).
- **Differentiation:** support: a buddy for entrances / stretch: stage manager or compère roles
- **Materials:** full set-up, programme
- **Joy:** First full run-through

### Lesson 35 — The graduation concert and evaluation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform in a concert for an audience and evaluate my performance and my growth as a musician this year." (UK KS3 Music; NCAS MU:Pr6.1.6, MU:Re9.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Ladies and gentlemen…"
- **Key activity:** The concert (part of the graduation celebration); afterwards, watch the recording and complete a reflection (what went well, what I'd improve, what I'm proudest of this year).
- **Check for understanding:** "Name one musical skill you have now that you didn't have in September, and show or play it." → A demonstrated skill (e.g. reading and playing chords, improvising a blues phrase).
- **Differentiation:** support: a reflection frame / stretch: a written review of the concert as a music critic
- **Materials:** concert venue, recording device, reflection sheets
- **Joy:** The graduation concert

**End-of-unit check:** The concert performance (accuracy, ensemble, expression) and a reflection that uses musical vocabulary to evaluate the performance and the year's growth.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Reads notation:** performs rhythms in simple, compound and irregular metres (including triplets and swing); names notes on the treble and bass staves, including ledger-line middle C. *See it:* Unit 1 performance.
2. **Plays keyboard (or ukulele) with chords:** major scale, major/minor triads, I–IV–V and I–V–vi–IV progressions, melody with accompaniment. *See it:* the Unit 2 performance.
3. **Understands and uses structure:** 12-bar blues, verse/chorus/bridge, ground bass, tala cycles, gamelan cycles. *See it:* the blues song and the songwriting project.
4. **Improvises** a musical phrase over a blues backing or a drone. *See it:* Unit 3 and 4 improvisations.
5. **Listens analytically** across traditions, identifying instruments, texture, rhythm and structure, and compares them with vocabulary. *See it:* the listening journal and the Unit 4 comparison.
6. **Composes** a soundtrack and a song, and uses music technology to record and mix. *See it:* the film soundtrack and the recorded song.
7. **Performs** in an ensemble with accuracy, balance and confidence. *See it:* the graduation concert.

## Teacher guidance

- **Common misconceptions:** the time signature's top number is the "speed" (it's the number of beats per bar); faster music is always louder (tempo and dynamics are separate); minor always means sad (it depends on tempo, rhythm and context); world music is "simple" (tala, polyrhythm and gamelan are highly complex); improvising means playing randomly (it uses scales, motifs and structure).
- **Respect traditions.** Present each tradition through its own terms and musicians; explain the cultural and social role of the music (e.g. gamelan in ceremonies, samba schools as community organisations); avoid "exotic" framing.
- **Non-specialist teachers.** Use backing tracks, BBC Ten Pieces and Chrome Music Lab; the keyboard skills needed are simple and can be learned alongside the children.
- **Differentiation.** Support routes use ukulele or single root notes, fewer notes, steadier parts and picture-based listening maps; stretch routes use hands together, inversions, leadership roles, harder rhythms and written appraisal.
- **Hearing and sensory needs.** Provide ear defenders for drumming lessons; offer visual notation and tactile rhythm (tapping on shoulders) for children who need it.
- **Vertical alignment.** Year 4 taught rounds, first ukulele chords, note values and 5/4, and samba, djembe, gamelan and raga; Year 5 taught choral harmony, ukulele in two keys, 6/8 and syncopation, treble-stave reading, keyboard basics, taiko, kora, siku, son and maqam, and opera. Year 6 retrieves these and adds irregular metre and swing, the bass clef, scales and harmony, the blues, deeper world-music study, orchestral timbre, film music and music technology, preparing for secondary music (UK Year 8 / US Grade 7 / MYP 2), which expects keyboard and notation basics and confident composing.
- **Cross-curricular links:** Maths (note values as fractions, time signatures, ratios in rhythm), History (the blues, civil rights), Geography (music of the world), Science (sound and timbre), English (lyrics and poetry), Computing (DAWs), Art (Kandinsky and music), Life Skills (managing nerves, the graduation concert).
