# Year 6 Art & Design (ages 11–12) — Scope & Sequence

> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Built to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Aligned to the **UK National Curriculum
> Key Stage 3 Art and Design** (Year 7), the **US National Core Arts Standards (Visual Arts, Grade 6:
> Creating, Presenting, Responding, Connecting)**, **IB MYP Year 1 Arts and Design** (criteria A–D,
> including the design cycle) and **Cambridge Lower Secondary Art & Design Stage 7**.

## Year overview

Year 6 Art & Design asks children to work like **artists and designers**: to look hard, to master
techniques, to learn from great makers across the world, to develop ideas in a sketchbook, and to
make purposeful choices they can explain. Each unit builds one discipline — **drawing, perspective,
colour and painting, printmaking, sculpture, and graphic/product design** — through named artists and
designers from many cultures and centuries. The year ends with a **graduation exhibition**: each child
develops and curates a personal project, the visual-arts strand of the graduation capstone.

**What we assume from Years 4 and 5:** Year 4 — one-point perspective, relief printmaking with a
two-colour reduction print, clay (pinch, coil, relief), Hepworth's carved forms, El Anatsui, textiles
and Islamic geometric pattern. Year 5 — tonal drawing and atmospheric perspective, ink and brush,
illustration and visual storytelling, collage and photomontage (Matisse, *jianzhi*, Bearden, Höch),
portraits and identity (Rembrandt, Kahlo, Wiley), wire, plaster and clay figure sculpture, photography,
digital collage and pictograms. **What Year 6 adds:** sustained observational drawing and reductive
charcoal; linear perspective in two points and extreme viewpoints; colour theory applied in paint;
printmaking beyond relief (monoprint, collagraph, stencil and screen printing, linocut); site-specific
sculpture, slab building and assemblage; the design process (brief → research → ideas → prototype →
test → refine); curation and artist statements. **What secondary relies on:** a well-used sketchbook habit, secure drawing and colour
skills, confidence with several media, and the vocabulary to analyse and evaluate art.

**Assessment is by looking and talking**, never quizzes: each lesson's check is a question to ask
while looking at the child's work, and each unit ends with a group critique using the sentence
stems "I notice… I wonder… I think the artist chose… because…". The sketchbook is the record of
learning. One lesson a week, 40–45 minutes (longer making sessions can be doubled up where the
timetable allows).

## Unit 1 — Seeing and drawing: line, tone and texture

**Essential question:** How can drawing help us see the world more truly?

**Key vocabulary:** observation, contour, blind contour, line weight, tone, value, tonal scale, highlight, shadow, cast shadow, hatching, cross-hatching, stippling, texture, composition, viewfinder, sketchbook

### Lesson 1 — Looking hard: contour and blind contour drawing
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make a contour drawing that follows the edges of an object closely, looking more at the object than the paper." (UK KS3 Art — drawing; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** Pages from Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks (studies of hands, plants and machines): "He drew to *think*. What was he trying to understand?"
- **Key activity:** Warm-up blind contour drawings of your own hand (never look at the paper!); then slow contour drawings of a shoe, a plant or a kitchen utensil; vary line weight (heavier where edges are near or in shadow); set up the year's sketchbook with a title page.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the drawing together: "Where did you look most — at the object or the paper? Show me a place where your line really follows the edge." → The child points to a passage of careful, observed line and explains the choice of line weight.
- **Differentiation:** support: draw a simple object with strong edges (a mug, a leaf) and use a viewfinder / stretch: a complex object (a bicycle wheel, a crumpled paper bag) with varied line weight
- **Materials:** sketchbooks, drawing pencils (HB, 2B, 4B), fine liners, everyday objects, viewfinders cut from card, images of Leonardo's notebook pages
- **Joy:** Laughing at the wonderfully wobbly blind contour hands — then seeing how much they capture

### Lesson 2 — Reductive charcoal: drawing light out of dark
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make a reductive charcoal drawing, lifting out lights with an eraser to model form on a toned ground." (UK KS3 Art — drawing; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** A white cloth draped over a chair under a single lamp: "Instead of adding dark to white paper, what if we started dark and drew with light?"
- **Key activity:** Two-minute retrieval of Year 5's tonal scale and the parts of light on a form (highlight, core shadow, reflected light, cast shadow). New: cover the paper in a mid-tone of compressed or willow charcoal; block in the darks; 'draw' the lights with a putty rubber and an eraser edge; study Georges Seurat's conté crayon drawings, built entirely from tone with no outlines.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the drawing: "Where is the light coming from, and how did you use the eraser to show it?" → The child identifies the light source and points to lifted-out highlights on that side and the deepened cast shadow on the other.
- **Differentiation:** support: a single white object (an egg or mug) with a tone guide / stretch: draped fabric with folds, keeping at least five distinct tones
- **Materials:** willow and compressed charcoal, putty rubbers, erasers, cartridge or sugar paper, fixative (adult, well ventilated) or hairspray alternative, white objects and fabric, desk lamp, images of Seurat's drawings
- **Joy:** Watching a form appear out of darkness as you "rub in" the light

### Lesson 3 — Mark-making for texture: Dürer's Young Hare
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use hatching, cross-hatching and stippling to show texture and tone, learning from Albrecht Dürer." (UK KS3 Art — great artists; NCAS VA:Re7.2.6)
- **Hook:** Albrecht Dürer's *Young Hare* (1502): "You can almost feel its fur. How did he do it with a brush and watercolour?"
- **Key activity:** Discuss the painting's directional marks; a mark-making sampler (hatching, cross-hatching, stippling, scumbling) in pen; a close-up texture study of a real object (a feather, bark, fabric, fur, a pine cone) using marks that follow the form.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the study: "Which marks did you use for the roughest part, and why do they work?" → The child explains how the direction and density of marks create the texture.
- **Differentiation:** support: a textured object with a clear pattern (bark or a pine cone) / stretch: combine three techniques in one drawing and vary density to create a full tonal range
- **Materials:** fine liners (0.1–0.5), ink and dip pens (optional), textured objects, image of *Young Hare*
- **Joy:** "Texture detective" — partners guess the object from a close-up drawing

### Lesson 4 — Still life: composition and observation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compose and draw a still life with accurate proportions, overlapping objects and a full tonal range." (UK KS3 Art — drawing; NCAS VA:Cr1.2.6)
- **Hook:** Still lifes from different traditions: a Dutch Golden Age painting (e.g. by Clara Peeters), a Cézanne apple painting and a Korean *chaekgeori* (books and objects) screen: "Why do artists keep painting ordinary things?"
- **Key activity:** Arrange a still life with the children (three objects, varied height, overlap); use a viewfinder to choose a composition; sketch with sight-size measuring (pencil at arm's length); build tone from light to dark.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the drawing: "How did you check the proportions of the tallest object compared with the others?" → The child demonstrates pencil measuring and shows where they corrected a proportion.
- **Differentiation:** support: two objects and a light-to-dark tone guide / stretch: include a reflective or transparent object (glass, metal) and show reflections
- **Materials:** still-life objects (fruit, bottles, fabric, books), viewfinders, pencils, images of the three still lifes
- **Joy:** Setting up the class's own "museum-worthy" still life

### Lesson 5 — Drawing unit critique and extended drawing
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can complete an extended observational drawing and discuss my own and others' work using art vocabulary." (UK KS3 Art — evaluation; NCAS VA:Re9.1.6)
- **Hook:** "An exhibition of your best drawing — what would you choose, and why?"
- **Key activity:** 25-minute sustained drawing of a subject of choice (a corner of a room, a plant, shoes); a gallery walk with sticky-note comments; a group critique using "I notice… I wonder…".
- **Check for understanding:** In the critique: "Choose one strength in a partner's drawing and explain it using the word 'tone', 'line' or 'texture'." → A specific, vocabulary-rich comment linked to what is visible.
- **Differentiation:** support: a smaller format and a chosen subject / stretch: a larger format with a complex composition and a written reflection in the sketchbook
- **Materials:** cartridge paper, pencils, fine liners, sticky notes
- **Joy:** The first gallery walk of the year

**End-of-unit check:** A sustained observational drawing and a short critique conversation in which the child explains their use of line, tone and texture and identifies one thing to improve.

## Unit 2 — Space and perspective: drawing buildings

**Essential question:** How can a flat drawing create the illusion of deep space?

**Key vocabulary:** perspective, horizon line, eye level, vanishing point, orthogonal lines, one-point perspective, two-point perspective, three-point perspective, bird's-eye view, worm's-eye view, foreshortening, scale, overlap, atmospheric perspective, architecture, façade

### Lesson 6 — Discovering perspective
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how artists create depth (overlap, size, height on the page, detail, atmospheric perspective) and find the horizon line and vanishing point in a painting." (UK KS3 Art — history of art; NCAS VA:Re7.1.6)
- **Hook:** A medieval painting next to Raphael's *The School of Athens* (1509–1511): "What changed in how artists showed space?"
- **Key activity:** The story of Filippo Brunelleschi's mirror experiment in Florence (early 1400s); find the vanishing point in *The School of Athens* by extending lines with a ruler on a printout; compare with Chinese landscape painting, which creates depth through layers and height on the page rather than a single vanishing point.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the printout: "Where do all the lines meet, and what's there?" → At the vanishing point, near the central figures — the lines draw our eye to them.
- **Differentiation:** support: a printout with some lines already drawn / stretch: compare the two approaches to space (European linear perspective vs Chinese layered space) in the sketchbook
- **Materials:** printouts of *The School of Athens* and a Chinese landscape scroll (e.g. by Guo Xi), rulers, coloured pencils
- **Joy:** "Find the vanishing point" race on famous images

### Lesson 7 — Viewpoint and foreshortening: bird's-eye and worm's-eye views
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw objects and buildings from an extreme high or low viewpoint, using foreshortening and a third vanishing point." (UK KS3 Art — drawing; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** Photographs looking straight up at skyscrapers and straight down from a tower: "Why do the sides of the buildings lean together?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of Year 4's one-point perspective (one corridor sketch in three minutes). New: eye level and viewpoint — from far above or below, vertical lines also converge to a third vanishing point; foreshortening (a pointing hand or an outstretched foot looks shorter and larger); draw a hand pointing at the viewer, then a tower seen from below or a street from above.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the drawing: "Why do the vertical edges of your tower get closer together at the top?" → When we look up, the top of the tower is further from us, so its vertical edges converge towards a vanishing point high above.
- **Differentiation:** support: foreshortening only (a hand, a shoe) with a photo reference / stretch: a full three-point perspective building with windows correctly diminishing
- **Materials:** rulers, sharp HB pencils, erasers, A3 paper, photographs of extreme viewpoints, a camera for reference shots
- **Joy:** "Ant's view / eagle's view" — photograph the school from both, then draw one

### Lesson 8 — Two-point perspective: buildings and streets
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw a building or street corner in two-point perspective." (UK KS3 Art — drawing; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** Photos of a street corner in Tokyo, Marrakech and Buenos Aires: "Why do we see two sides of each building?"
- **Key activity:** Demonstrate two-point perspective (two vanishing points on the horizon, a leading vertical edge); draw a box, then a building with windows and a door; extend to a street corner.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the drawing: "Why does the top of the building slope down towards the vanishing points while the bottom slopes up?" → The top is above eye level and the bottom is below it; all horizontal edges head towards a vanishing point on the horizon.
- **Differentiation:** support: draw boxes floating above and below the horizon first / stretch: a street of several buildings with roofs, awnings and people to scale
- **Materials:** rulers, long paper, pencils, photographs of street corners
- **Joy:** Drawing a "city of the future" block

### Lesson 9 — Architects and bold forms: Gaudí and Zaha Hadid
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can analyse how two architects used form and space, and design and draw my own building inspired by them." (UK KS3 Art — architecture and design; NCAS VA:Re7.2.6)
- **Hook:** The Sagrada Família (Barcelona) and the Heydar Aliyev Center (Baku): "Where are the straight lines?"
- **Key activity:** Compare Antoni Gaudí's nature-inspired forms (still being built since 1882) with Zaha Hadid's flowing modern forms; sketch details; design a building (a library, a museum or a school) inspired by one, drawing it in perspective with annotations.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the design: "What did you borrow from your architect, and how did you change it to make it yours?" → A specific borrowed feature (organic curves, flowing lines) and a personal adaptation.
- **Differentiation:** support: design a building façade (front view) with annotations / stretch: a perspective drawing plus a plan view
- **Materials:** images of Gaudí's and Hadid's buildings, pencils, fine liners, coloured pencils
- **Joy:** Designing a building you'd love to go to school in

### Lesson 10 — Impossible spaces: M. C. Escher and unit critique
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Escher played with perspective to make impossible spaces, create an optical illusion drawing and take part in a critique." (UK KS3 Art — great artists; NCAS VA:Re9.1.6)
- **Hook:** Escher's *Relativity* (1953): "Which way is up?"
- **Key activity:** Discuss how Escher combines several vanishing points; draw a Penrose triangle or an impossible staircase; finish the perspective drawings; critique using the unit's vocabulary.
- **Check for understanding:** In the critique: "How does your drawing create depth? Name two techniques." → e.g. vanishing points and overlap, or size and atmospheric perspective.
- **Differentiation:** support: a guided Penrose triangle with dotted guide lines / stretch: design your own impossible room
- **Materials:** Escher images, rulers, isometric paper, pencils
- **Joy:** Fooling a family member with your impossible drawing

**End-of-unit check:** A two-point perspective drawing of a building or street and a critique conversation explaining the horizon line, vanishing points and one design choice inspired by an architect.

## Unit 3 — Colour and painting

**Essential question:** How do artists use colour to create light, space and feeling?

**Key vocabulary:** primary, secondary, tertiary, complementary, analogous, warm, cool, hue, tint, shade, tone, saturation, palette, colour temperature, wash, impasto, glaze, expressive colour, abstraction

### Lesson 11 — Colour theory in paint
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can mix a 12-part colour wheel, tints and shades, and explain complementary and analogous colours." (UK KS3 Art — colour; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** "With only three colours plus white, can you mix 50 different colours in 10 minutes?"
- **Key activity:** Mix a 12-part colour wheel from primaries (a warm and a cool version of each where available); tints (add white) and shades (add a little complementary or black); complementary pairs placed side by side "vibrate".
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the wheel: "Which colour makes orange look brightest when placed next to it, and why?" → Blue — its complementary colour (opposite on the wheel), which creates the strongest contrast.
- **Differentiation:** support: a printed wheel template with mixing guides / stretch: mix a subtle neutral grey from two complementaries and use it in a small study
- **Materials:** acrylic or poster paints (primaries + white), brushes, palettes, water pots, colour-wheel templates
- **Joy:** "Mix to match" — match paint swatches cut from magazines

### Lesson 12 — Expressive colour: Van Gogh
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can analyse how Van Gogh used complementary colour and directional brushstrokes, and paint a small study using those techniques." (UK KS3 Art — great artists; NCAS VA:Re7.2.6)
- **Hook:** *The Starry Night* (1889) and *Café Terrace at Night* (1888): "Can a night sky be this colourful?"
- **Key activity:** Look closely at brushwork (directional, thick strokes — impasto); complementary colours (blue and orange/yellow); paint a small night-scene or landscape using directional strokes and complementaries; read an extract from one of Van Gogh's letters to his brother Theo about colour.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the painting: "Show me where you used complementary colours and why." → The child points to a complementary pairing and explains how it creates vibrancy or contrast.
- **Differentiation:** support: a simplified composition with a drawn outline / stretch: mix and use thick impasto paint with a palette knife
- **Materials:** acrylic paints, stiff brushes, palette knives, card, images of Van Gogh's paintings
- **Joy:** Painting a swirling sky

### Lesson 13 — Colour and feeling: Kandinsky and abstraction
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can create an abstract painting that expresses a piece of music or a feeling through colour, line and shape." (UK KS3 Art; NCAS VA:Cr1.1.6; Music link)
- **Hook:** Wassily Kandinsky believed he could "hear" colours. Play a piece of music: "What colour is this?"
- **Key activity:** Look at Kandinsky's *Composition VII* (1913) and *Several Circles* (1926); listen to two contrasting pieces of music (e.g. from the Music unit); paint an abstract response to one; explain choices.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the painting: "Which colour and shape choices match the music, and why?" → The child links specific choices (e.g. sharp yellow triangles for loud brass) to features of the music.
- **Differentiation:** support: a limited palette and shape stencils / stretch: a diptych responding to two contrasting pieces
- **Materials:** paints, brushes, paper, recordings of two contrasting pieces, Kandinsky images
- **Joy:** Painting to music

### Lesson 14 — Colour, identity and place: Amrita Sher-Gil and Tarsila do Amaral
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how two modern painters used colour and simplified form to represent their countries and people, and apply this in my own painting." (UK KS3 Art — diverse artists; NCAS VA:Cn11.1.6)
- **Hook:** Amrita Sher-Gil (India, 1913–1941) and Tarsila do Amaral (Brazil, 1886–1973): "Both studied in Paris, then went home and invented a new way of painting their own countries."
- **Key activity:** Compare Sher-Gil's *Three Girls* (1935) and Tarsila do Amaral's *Abaporu* (1928): colour palette, simplified shapes, subjects; paint a place or people important to you using a chosen palette and simplified forms.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the painting: "How does your palette show something about your place?" → A reason connecting colours to the place (landscape, light, culture, feeling).
- **Differentiation:** support: a colour-palette card to choose from / stretch: a written comparison of the two painters in the sketchbook
- **Materials:** paints, brushes, card, images of the two paintings, photographs of the child's chosen place
- **Joy:** Painting a place that matters to you

### Lesson 15 — Close-up painting: Georgia O'Keeffe, and critique
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can paint a close-up study from observation using blending and colour gradation, and discuss my work in a critique." (UK KS3 Art; NCAS VA:Re9.1.6)
- **Hook:** Georgia O'Keeffe's enormous flower paintings: "Why paint a flower as big as a window?"
- **Key activity:** Use a viewfinder to crop a flower, leaf or shell dramatically; paint with smooth gradations (wet-into-wet or blended acrylic); unit critique of all colour work.
- **Check for understanding:** In the critique: "How did you create a smooth change of colour? Show where." → The child points to a gradation and explains the technique (blending, glazing, wet-into-wet).
- **Differentiation:** support: a simpler subject (a leaf) with a drawn outline / stretch: use glazing layers to build luminous colour
- **Materials:** paints (watercolour or acrylic), soft brushes, flowers or shells, viewfinders
- **Joy:** "Macro world" gallery of giant close-ups

**End-of-unit check:** A finished painting (from Lessons 12–15) and a critique conversation in which the child explains their colour choices using complementary, analogous, warm/cool and tint/shade.

## Unit 4 — Printmaking

**Essential question:** How does printmaking let artists repeat, layer and share images?

**Key vocabulary:** relief print, linocut, gouge, block, brayer, ink, registration, edition, artist's proof, stencil, screen print, squeegee, mesh, positive space, negative space, mirror image, monoprint, collagraph

### Lesson 16 — Prints with a purpose: Posada, Corita Kent and Warhol
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why artists have chosen printmaking to reach many people and compare three printmakers' purposes and methods." (UK KS3 Art — history of art; NCAS VA:Re7.1.6)
- **Hook:** José Guadalupe Posada's skeleton *calaveras* (Mexico, around 1900), Corita Kent's joyful screen-printed words (USA, 1960s) and Andy Warhol's screen-printed, repeated Marilyns and soup cans (1960s): "Why would an artist *want* many identical copies?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4's relief-printing basics (raised parts print, the image reverses) in two minutes; compare the three artists' methods (relief engraving, screen printing) and purposes (popular satire, messages of hope, commenting on mass production); sketch design ideas for the unit.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did Posada, Kent and Warhol all choose print rather than painting for these works?" → Print makes many copies cheaply to reach many people, and repetition itself can carry meaning (e.g. Warhol's comment on mass-produced goods).
- **Differentiation:** support: a comparison grid with images / stretch: explain how Warhol's repetition changes what we think about an everyday object
- **Materials:** images of Posada, Corita Kent and Warhol prints, sketchbooks
- **Joy:** Spotting printed images in the room — how many copies of each exist?

### Lesson 17 — Monoprints and collagraphs
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make monoprints and a collagraph plate and explain how texture transfers to a print." (UK KS3 Art — printmaking; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Can you print a leaf, a piece of lace and your fingerprint — all in one picture?"
- **Key activity:** Monoprint (ink a surface, draw on paper laid over it); build a collagraph plate from card, string, fabric and leaves glued to board; ink and print.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the prints: "Which material gave the most interesting texture, and why?" → A material with raised texture that held ink well (e.g. lace, string) — the child explains how raised surfaces print.
- **Differentiation:** support: a pre-cut card plate / stretch: plan a collagraph image with foreground and background textures
- **Materials:** water-based block ink, brayers, inking plates, card, string, lace, leaves, PVA glue, paper
- **Joy:** Peeling back the paper to reveal the print

### Lesson 18 — Stencils and screen printing
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can cut a paper stencil and pull a screen print (or stencil print) with a clean, even layer of ink." (UK KS3 Art — printmaking; NCAS VA:Cr2.2.6)
- **Hook:** "Corita Kent printed bright words through silk. How can ink pass through some parts of a screen and not others?"
- **Key activity:** Design a bold two-shape image or a word (connect to Lesson 27 lettering); cut a paper stencil (remembering 'islands' such as the middle of an O need bridges); print through an embroidery-hoop screen or a stretched-mesh frame with a squeegee (or dab through the stencil with a sponge); pull several prints and compare.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the stencil and print: "Why did the centre of your letter O disappear — or how did you stop it?" → The centre is a separate 'island' with nothing holding it in the stencil, so it falls out; a bridge (a thin strip) keeps it attached.
- **Differentiation:** support: sponge-stencilling through a card stencil / stretch: two stencils printed in register for two colours
- **Materials:** paper for stencils, craft knives (adult) or safety scissors, embroidery hoops with net curtain or screen mesh, squeegees or stiff card, water-based screen or block ink, sponges, paper, images of Corita Kent's prints
- **Joy:** The first clean pull of the squeegee

### Lesson 19 — Linocut: cutting with gouges
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can cut a lino block safely with different gouges to create line, texture and solid areas, and print an edition." (UK KS3 Art — printmaking; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** Elizabeth Catlett and the People's Graphic Workshop (*Taller de Gráfica Popular*) in Mexico City: "Year 4 drew into soft foam. Lino lets you cut lines as fine as hair — or clear whole areas. How did these artists make faces so powerful in black and white?"
- **Key activity:** Safety demonstration (bench hook, cut away from the hand, warm the lino); test strip with V-gouge and U-gouge marks (lines, hatching, dots, clearing); transfer the design (mirror-reversed); cut; print an edition of three and number it (1/3, 2/3, 3/3). Picasso's multi-colour reduction linocuts are shown as the stretch model.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the test strip and block: "Which gouge did you use for the fine lines and which for clearing, and why does that matter in the print?" → The V-gouge cuts fine lines that print as thin white lines; the U-gouge clears wide areas that print as white shapes.
- **Differentiation:** support: soft-cut (safety) lino and a bold simple design / stretch: a two- or three-colour reduction linocut printed in register, like Picasso's
- **Materials:** soft-cut or traditional lino, V- and U-gouges, bench hooks, a warm radiator or hand-warmers for the lino, tracing paper, block ink, brayers, paper, images of Catlett's and TGP prints
- **Joy:** Peeling back the first lino print

### Lesson 20 — Print exhibition and critique
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can select and mount my best print and evaluate the edition in a critique." (UK KS3 Art — evaluation; NCAS VA:Pr5.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Why do printmakers throw away some of their prints?"
- **Key activity:** Compare prints in the edition (ink coverage, registration, cleanliness); select and mount the best; critique; a print swap with a partner.
- **Check for understanding:** In the critique: "Why did you choose this print as your best?" → A reason based on technical quality (even ink, good registration, clean edges) and effect.
- **Differentiation:** support: a quality checklist / stretch: write an artist statement for the print
- **Materials:** mounting card, glue, prints, checklists
- **Joy:** Print swap — keep a classmate's print

**End-of-unit check:** A signed linocut edition and one stencil or collagraph print, with a critique conversation explaining the processes, positive/negative space and one improvement.

## Unit 5 — Sculpture and 3D form

**Essential question:** How do sculptors turn materials into form — and what can objects tell us about the cultures that made them?

**Key vocabulary:** form, mass, volume, void, relief, in the round, maquette, armature, coil, slab, score and slip, lost-wax casting, bronze, assemblage, found object, installation

### Lesson 21 — Sculpture and place: Henry Moore and Isamu Noguchi
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how sculptors design for a particular site and make a maquette for a public sculpture in our school grounds." (UK KS3 Art — sculpture; NCAS VA:Re7.2.6)
- **Hook:** Henry Moore's large reclining figures set in open landscape and Isamu Noguchi's sculptural playgrounds and gardens (e.g. Moerenuma Park, Sapporo): "Is a playground a sculpture?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4's Hepworth and the idea of the void. New: site-specific sculpture — scale, materials that survive weather, how people move around and through it. Choose a real spot in the school grounds; photograph and measure it; make a scaled maquette in air-dry clay, card or wire; photograph the maquette in the real site to test it.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the maquette in its photographed site: "How did the place you chose change your design?" → A specific link (e.g. made it low and curved so younger children can sit on it; oriented the opening towards the sunrise).
- **Differentiation:** support: a card maquette from a template on a site photo / stretch: a scale drawing with measurements and a materials proposal for the full-size version
- **Materials:** air-dry clay, card, wire, tape measures, cameras or tablets, site photographs, images of Moore's and Noguchi's work
- **Joy:** "Photo trick" — place your maquette so it looks giant in the real site

### Lesson 22 — The Benin Bronzes: history, skill and return
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the Benin Bronzes were made (lost-wax casting), what they record about the Kingdom of Benin, and why their return is debated." (UK KS3 Art — art from other cultures; NCAS VA:Cn11.1.6; History link)
- **Hook:** A Benin plaque showing the Oba (king) with attendants: "These brass plaques are a history book — and many were taken from Benin City in 1897."
- **Key activity:** Learn the lost-wax technique (with a wax-carving demonstration); read the plaques as historical records; discuss the 1897 British expedition and the return of objects to Nigeria in recent years; design a relief plaque recording an event from the child's own life.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the plaque design: "How does your plaque show who is most important?" → e.g. by size, central position or detail — as in the Benin plaques (hierarchical scale).
- **Differentiation:** support: a template for a relief plaque / stretch: write a museum label about a Benin plaque, including where it is now and the debate about its return
- **Materials:** images of Benin plaques and heads, a lost-wax video, modelling wax or clay, plaque design sheets
- **Joy:** Carving your own "history plaque"

### Lesson 23 — Clay: slab and coil construction
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can construct a clay form with slabs or coils, joining pieces securely by scoring and slipping." (UK KS3 Art — sculpture; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** Ancient coil pots from many cultures (e.g. Jōmon pottery from Japan and pottery by Maria Martinez of San Ildefonso Pueblo): "People have been making pots this way for thousands of years."
- **Key activity:** Demonstrate score-and-slip; build a vessel or container with coils or slabs; add surface texture or a relief (impressed patterns).
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the joins: "Why must you score and slip before joining clay?" → Scoring roughens the surfaces and slip acts as glue so the pieces bond and won't crack apart as they dry.
- **Differentiation:** support: a pinch pot base with added coils / stretch: a lidded container or a vessel with a handle
- **Materials:** clay (air-dry or kiln clay), slip, modelling tools, rolling pins, guide sticks, boards
- **Joy:** The feel of clay — building something that could last thousands of years

### Lesson 24 — Assemblage: Louise Nevelson and the found object
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can create an assemblage from found objects, unified by colour and structure, and explain how material and arrangement carry meaning." (UK KS3 Art — contemporary art; NCAS VA:Cr1.2.6)
- **Hook:** Louise Nevelson's walls of wooden boxes filled with chair legs, banister rails and wooden offcuts — all painted one colour: "Why paint everything black? What happens to a chair leg when it's no longer a chair leg?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4's El Anatsui and Year 5's Hazoumè (material tells a story). New: assemblage as composition — pupils fill a shallow box with found objects (card tubes, lids, offcuts, broken toys), arrange for rhythm and balance, then unify with a single colour of paint; join individual boxes into a class 'wall'.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the box: "What does painting everything one colour do to the way we see your objects?" → It hides what the objects were, so we notice their shapes, shadows and rhythm instead — they become parts of a new whole.
- **Differentiation:** support: a smaller box with a limited set of objects / stretch: plan the whole-class wall's composition and colour as lead designer
- **Materials:** shallow boxes or card trays, cleaned found objects, PVA and glue guns (low-temperature, supervised), one colour of acrylic or poster paint per group
- **Joy:** Assembling the class "Nevelson wall" for the exhibition

### Lesson 25 — Sculpture critique and presentation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present my sculpture, explain my process and evaluate it from all sides." (UK KS3 Art — evaluation; NCAS VA:Pr6.1.6)
- **Hook:** "A sculpture has no front. Walk around it."
- **Key activity:** Photograph the sculpture from four angles; a walk-around critique; complete a sketchbook reflection.
- **Check for understanding:** In the critique: "Which view of your sculpture is strongest, and why?" → A view identified with reasons (form, balance, texture, void).
- **Differentiation:** support: reflection prompts / stretch: a short artist statement and a proposal for a large-scale version (where would it go?)
- **Materials:** sculptures, turntable or rotating board, camera or tablet
- **Joy:** A "sculpture park" display

**End-of-unit check:** A finished clay or assemblage sculpture, photographed from several views, and a critique conversation explaining the process (joining, construction) and how material and form create meaning.

## Unit 6 — Design for a purpose

**Essential question:** How do designers solve real problems for real people?

**Key vocabulary:** design brief, user, need, research, mood board, idea generation, prototype, test, iterate, evaluate, typography, serif, sans serif, logo, layout, hierarchy, grid, ergonomics, form follows function

### Lesson 26 — What makes good design? Dieter Rams, the Bauhaus and the Eameses
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate everyday designed objects using principles such as usefulness, simplicity and durability." (UK KS3 Design & Technology link; NCAS VA:Re8.1.6; IB MYP Design)
- **Hook:** A good and a bad design side by side (a door you push that looks like a pull; a well-designed water bottle): "Why did one make you feel clever and one make you feel silly?"
- **Key activity:** Introduce Dieter Rams's "ten principles of good design" (simplified), the Bauhaus idea "form follows function" and Charles and Ray Eames's chairs; evaluate five everyday objects from home.
- **Check for understanding:** Discussing an evaluated object: "What does 'form follows function' mean for this object?" → The object's shape should come from what it needs to do, e.g. a handle shaped to fit a hand.
- **Differentiation:** support: an evaluation grid with three criteria / stretch: redesign a badly designed object and annotate improvements
- **Materials:** everyday objects (bottles, scissors, remote controls, kettles), images of Bauhaus objects and Eames chairs, evaluation grids
- **Joy:** "Bad design museum" — find the silliest design at home

### Lesson 27 — Typography and lettering
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how typefaces create mood and design a word so its lettering expresses its meaning." (UK KS3 Art — graphic design; NCAS VA:Cr2.3.6)
- **Hook:** "Would you trust a hospital sign in a spooky Halloween font?"
- **Key activity:** Compare serif, sans serif, script and display typefaces; Arabic, Chinese and Devanagari calligraphy as design traditions; hand-letter an "expressive word" (e.g. "melt", "grow", "shatter").
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the lettering: "How does the design of your word show its meaning?" → Specific choices (drips for "melt", broken letters for "shatter") linked to meaning.
- **Differentiation:** support: a grid for letter shapes / stretch: design a full alphabet style or a monogram
- **Materials:** fine liners, markers, grid paper, examples of typefaces and calligraphy
- **Joy:** "Expressive word" wall

### Lesson 28 — Logo and poster design
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can design a logo and a poster with clear visual hierarchy for a real event (the graduation exhibition or capstone project)." (UK KS3 Art — graphic design; NCAS VA:Cr1.2.6)
- **Hook:** Famous logos reduced to simple shapes and posters by designers such as Paula Scher: "Why are the best logos so simple?"
- **Key activity:** Analyse visual hierarchy (what you see first, second, third) in posters; sketch logo thumbnails (at least eight); refine one; design a poster layout with a grid for the graduation exhibition.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the poster: "What does a viewer see first, second and third — and is that what you intended?" → The child explains the hierarchy (size, colour, position) and matches it to the most important information.
- **Differentiation:** support: a poster template with a grid / stretch: produce the poster digitally with free design software
- **Materials:** thumbnail sheets, markers, poster paper or design software, example posters
- **Joy:** Your poster used to advertise the real graduation exhibition

### Lesson 29 — Product design: brief, prototype, test
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can follow the design cycle to create a prototype that meets a user's need, then test and improve it." (IB MYP Design cycle; UK KS3 Design & Technology link; NCAS VA:Cr3.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Design something that makes a younger child's school day easier." (e.g. a pencil organiser, a book stand, a reusable snack wrap)
- **Key activity:** Interview a user (a younger sibling or pupil); write a brief with criteria; sketch ideas; build a cardboard prototype; test with the user; note improvements.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the prototype and notes: "What did testing tell you, and what will you change?" → A specific finding from the user and a planned change.
- **Differentiation:** support: a guided brief and a prototype kit / stretch: a second, improved iteration within the lesson
- **Materials:** cardboard, masking tape, scissors, glue guns (supervised), string, brief templates
- **Joy:** Seeing a younger child use your invention

### Lesson 30 — Design critique and portfolio
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present my design journey (brief → ideas → prototype → test → improvement) and evaluate it against the criteria." (IB MYP Design Criterion D; NCAS VA:Re9.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Designers pitch to clients. Pitch your design."
- **Key activity:** Create a one-page design portfolio sheet; 2-minute pitches; critique against the brief's criteria.
- **Check for understanding:** In the pitch: "Which criterion does your design meet best, and which least? What evidence do you have?" → An honest evaluation with evidence from testing.
- **Differentiation:** support: a portfolio template / stretch: include costings and material choices with reasons
- **Materials:** portfolio sheets, prototypes, photographs
- **Joy:** "Dragons' den" style pitch

**End-of-unit check:** A design portfolio sheet showing the full design cycle and a pitch conversation evaluating the product against its brief.

## Unit 7 — Graduation exhibition: personal project

**Essential question:** What do I want to say as an artist, and how will I show it to the world?

**Key vocabulary:** intention, theme, influence, development, experimentation, refinement, curation, artist statement, exhibition, installation, label, audience

### Lesson 31 — Choosing a theme and an influence
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose a personal theme, research an artist or designer who inspires me and write an intention." (UK KS3 Art; NCAS VA:Cr1.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Your graduation exhibition: one piece that says something about you and the world."
- **Key activity:** Brainstorm themes (identity, home, nature, the future, a cause I care about — possibly linked to the Life Skills capstone); choose an artist from any unit or research a new one; create a research page in the sketchbook; write an intention ("I want to… because…").
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the research page: "What exactly will you borrow from your artist, and what will be yours?" → A specific technique or idea to borrow and a personal element.
- **Differentiation:** support: choose from a shortlist of themes and artists / stretch: combine influences from two artists or cultures
- **Materials:** sketchbooks, art books, devices for research, printouts
- **Joy:** Freedom to choose

### Lesson 32 — Experimenting with media
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can test at least three media or techniques for my project and choose the best with reasons." (UK KS3 Art; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Artists experiment before they commit."
- **Key activity:** Small trials (e.g. the same idea in paint, print and collage); annotate what works; choose the medium.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the trials: "Why did you choose this medium over the others?" → A reason linked to the intention (e.g. print for repetition, paint for colour and mood).
- **Differentiation:** support: two trials with guidance / stretch: combine media in a mixed-media approach
- **Materials:** a wide range of materials from the year
- **Joy:** The "experiment wall"

### Lesson 33 — Making the final piece
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make my final piece with care, refining as I go." (UK KS3 Art; NCAS VA:Cr3.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Make it your best work yet."
- **Key activity:** Sustained making time; teacher conferences; peer "pit-stop" feedback halfway.
- **Check for understanding:** Looking at the work in progress: "What change did you make after the pit-stop feedback?" → A specific refinement.
- **Differentiation:** support: a staged plan with checkpoints / stretch: a larger scale or an extra element (e.g. a series)
- **Materials:** chosen media, workspace
- **Joy:** Deep, focused making time

### Lesson 34 — Artist statements and curating the exhibition
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write an artist statement and help curate an exhibition that tells a story." (UK KS3 Art; NCAS VA:Pr4.1.6, VA:Pr5.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Curators decide what visitors see first. How would you arrange the exhibition?"
- **Key activity:** Write an artist statement (title, medium, intention, influence, what I'd like viewers to notice); group work to plan the layout (themes, flow, lighting, labels); hang the work.
- **Check for understanding:** Reading the statement: "Does it tell a visitor what you intended and how you did it?" → A statement including intention, influence and technique.
- **Differentiation:** support: a statement template / stretch: act as lead curator and write the exhibition introduction
- **Materials:** label card, display boards, hanging materials, sketchbooks
- **Joy:** Turning the classroom into a gallery

### Lesson 35 — Graduation exhibition and final critique
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present my work to visitors, discuss it thoughtfully and reflect on my growth as an artist." (UK KS3 Art; NCAS VA:Re9.1.6)
- **Hook:** "Doors open!"
- **Key activity:** Exhibition opening for families (part of the graduation capstone); children act as guides for their own work; visitors leave comments; final sketchbook reflection comparing September's contour drawing with the final piece.
- **Check for understanding:** In conversation with a visitor: "What was the hardest part of making this, and how did you solve it?" → A clear account of a challenge and solution.
- **Differentiation:** support: prepared talking points / stretch: give a guided tour of the whole exhibition
- **Materials:** exhibition space, comment cards
- **Joy:** The graduation exhibition

**End-of-unit check:** The exhibited final piece with artist statement and sketchbook development, and a conversation with the teacher or a visitor explaining intention, influence, process and reflection.

## End-of-year mastery checks (assessed by looking and discussion)

1. **Draws from observation** with accurate proportions, a full tonal range and varied mark-making. *See it:* the Unit 1 sustained drawing.
2. **Uses perspective** (one- and two-point) to create convincing space. *See it:* the building drawing.
3. **Applies colour theory in paint** — mixes tints, shades and complementaries purposefully. *See it:* the Van Gogh or Sher-Gil/Amaral painting and its explanation.
4. **Makes prints by several methods** (collagraph, stencil or screen, linocut) with clear positive/negative space and clean inking. *See it:* the linocut edition and stencil print.
5. **Constructs in 3D** — joins clay securely or builds an assemblage, considering all viewpoints. *See it:* the sculpture and its photographs.
6. **Follows the design cycle** — brief, research, ideas, prototype, test, improve. *See it:* the design portfolio sheet.
7. **Analyses art from many cultures** using vocabulary and context, including questions about ownership and meaning (e.g. the Benin Bronzes). *See it:* critique conversations and museum labels.
8. **Develops and curates a personal project** with an intention, influences and an artist statement. *See it:* the graduation exhibition.

## Teacher guidance

- **Assessment is conversation.** Use the "look and talk" checks in every lesson; record comments in the sketchbook. Critique is kind, specific and helpful: "I notice… I wonder… One thing that could make it even stronger is…".
- **Common misconceptions:** "I can't draw" (drawing is a learnable skill of looking — show September vs June work); drawing symbols instead of what you see (use blind contour and upside-down drawing); black is the only way to make shadow (mix complementaries); perspective lines are random (all go to vanishing points on the horizon); printing does not reverse the image (it does); clay pieces just stick together (score and slip).
- **Cultural respect.** When studying art from other cultures, focus on the artists, their techniques and context; don't copy sacred or culturally restricted designs. Discuss questions of ownership and return (e.g. the Benin Bronzes) honestly and age-appropriately.
- **Safety.** Lino cutters (cut away from the body, use bench hooks), glue guns (low-temperature, supervised), carving tools, clay dust (wipe, don't sweep), and water-based inks only.
- **Differentiation.** Support means simpler subjects, templates, easier materials (foam instead of lino, clay instead of carving) and smaller formats — with the same artistic thinking. Stretch means more complex subjects, extra colours or layers, larger scale and written analysis.
- **Vertical alignment.** Year 4 taught one-point perspective, relief and reduction printing, Hepworth and El Anatsui; Year 5 taught tone and atmospheric perspective, illustration, collage, portraits, figure sculpture and pictograms. Year 6 deepens drawing, adds two- and three-point perspective, systematic colour, new print methods, site-specific sculpture and design, and ends with a curated exhibition, preparing for secondary art and design (UK Year 8 / US Grade 7 / MYP 2).
- **Cross-curricular links:** Maths (perspective and geometry, tessellation, scale), History (Benin, Mughal miniatures, Renaissance), Geography (architecture and place), Music (Kandinsky), Computing (digital design), Life Skills (the graduation capstone — posters and exhibition).
