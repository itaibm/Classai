# Year 6 Mathematics (ages 11–12) — Scope & Sequence

> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Built to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Aligned to **US CCSS Grade 6**
> (6.RP, 6.NS, 6.EE, 6.G, 6.SP), the **UK National Curriculum Key Stage 3** (Year 7 programme),
> **IB MYP Year 1** mathematics (criteria A–D) and the **Singapore Secondary 1** syllabus.
> This is the graduation year: the last maths a child does with us before secondary school.

## Year overview

Year 6 turns a confident primary arithmetician into a young mathematician who can **reason with
the general, not just the particular**. Three big shifts run through the year: from *numbers* to
*integers* (negative numbers become ordinary numbers you can multiply and divide); from *answers*
to *relationships* (ratio, rate and proportion describe how two quantities move together); and
from *calculating* to *generalising* (a letter stands for any number, so one expression or
equation can describe a whole family of situations).

**What we assume from Year 5 (ages 10–11, UK Y6 / US Grade 5):** place value to 10,000,000 and
three decimal places; long multiplication and long division by two-digit divisors; the four
operations with fractions (dividing a fraction by a whole number); fraction–decimal–percentage
equivalence and simple percentages of amounts; a first meeting with ratio (sharing, scaling),
formulae and one- and two-step equations; area of triangles and parallelograms; cuboid volume;
angle facts on a line, at a point and in a triangle; four-quadrant coordinates; pie charts and
the mean. Year 6 **revisits each of these briefly (retrieval), then goes deeper**, so a child who
arrived with gaps is not left behind.

**Sequence.** Term 1 secures the number system: powers, primes and estimation (Unit 1), the
integers (Unit 2), and the full fraction–decimal–percentage toolkit including **division of
fractions** (Unit 3), then launches algebra with **expressions** (Unit 4) and ends with a
retrieval-and-assessment unit (Unit 5). Term 2 uses that algebra to **solve linear equations and
inequalities** and to meet **graphs of relationships** (Unit 6); develops **ratio, rate and
proportion** as a single connected idea (Unit 7); and builds rigorous geometry with **angles,
parallel lines, bearings and ruler-and-compass constructions** (Unit 8), then consolidates (Unit 9). Term 3
applies number and algebra to **area, volume and surface area** (Unit 10), introduces
**probability** (Unit 11) and **statistics** as a full enquiry cycle (Unit 12), and closes with a
**graduation problem-solving unit** (Unit 13) in which children tackle rich, multi-strand problems
and a real-world maths project.

**Method.** Singapore / Maths-No-Problem mastery sequencing: every lesson moves concrete →
pictorial → abstract (algebra tiles, double-sided counters, bar models, balance scales, geoboards,
nets, dice), with "I do → we do → you do". Each unit ends with a mastery check; each term ends with
retrieval lessons; fluency (mental arithmetic, number facts, squares and primes) is kept warm with
a 5-minute daily starter. Problem solving and reasoning ("convince me", "always / sometimes /
never") are in every unit. Lessons are 35–45 minutes.

**What the next stage relies on.** Secondary maths (UK Year 8 / US Grade 7 / MYP 2) assumes a
child can operate fluently with negative numbers and fractions, form and solve linear equations,
use ratio and percentage multiplicatively, and handle area/volume formulae and averages. Every
one of those is a mastery check at the end of this year.

## Unit 1 — Place value, powers & primes

**Essential question:** How can the structure of our number system — powers of ten, primes and factors — make hard calculations easy?

**Key vocabulary:** power, index, square, cube, square root, cube root, prime, composite, prime factorisation, highest common factor (HCF), lowest common multiple (LCM), significant figure, decimal place, estimate, order of operations

### Lesson 1 — Place value from millions to thousandths
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read, write, compare and order any number from millions to thousandths and explain the value of each digit." (UK KS3 Number; CCSS 6.NS.3 foundation)
- **Hook:** Show the distance to the Moon (384,400 km) and the thickness of a human hair (about 0.07 mm): "One number system handles both — how?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieval of Year 5 place value: build 3,405.072 on a place-value chart with digit cards (I do); pairs build numbers read aloud and order five of them, including 0.5 vs 0.45 vs 0.405 (we do); independent ordering of mixed whole/decimal lists and "place the number on a blank number line" (you do).
- **Check for understanding:** "Mia says 0.45 is bigger than 0.5 because 45 is bigger than 5. Is she right? Explain." → No: 0.5 = 0.50, which is 50 hundredths; 0.45 is 45 hundredths, so 0.5 is bigger.
- **Differentiation:** support: place-value chart with columns labelled and base-10 blocks where the flat = 1 / stretch: order numbers written in different forms (3/8, 0.38, 38.5%, 0.3799) and justify each step
- **Materials:** place-value chart (millions → thousandths), digit cards 0–9, base-10 blocks, mini-whiteboards, blank number lines
- **Joy:** "Human number line": each child holds a mystery decimal card and the group silently orders itself across the room

### Lesson 2 — Multiplying and dividing by powers of ten
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply and divide whole numbers and decimals by 10, 100, 1000, 0.1 and 0.01 and explain why the digits move." (UK KS3 Number; CCSS 5.NBT.2 → 6.NS.3)
- **Hook:** "A grain of rice weighs about 0.02 g. How much do 1000 grains weigh? A million?"
- **Key activity:** Model on a sliding place-value chart (the digits move, the decimal point never does); discover that × 0.1 gives the same result as ÷ 10 by testing examples with a calculator, then explain using fractions (0.1 = 1/10); practise with metric conversions (m ↔ cm ↔ mm, kg ↔ g).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 4.5 × 0.1 smaller than 4.5?" → Because you are taking one-tenth of 4.5, which is 0.45; multiplying by a number less than 1 makes a positive number smaller.
- **Differentiation:** support: sliding digit strip on a printed chart; stick to × and ÷ by 10, 100, 1000 first / stretch: explain 0.36 ÷ 0.01 = 36 and generalise "dividing by a number less than 1 gives a bigger answer"
- **Materials:** sliding place-value chart (paper strip), calculators for pattern-spotting, metre rule, kitchen scale, bag of rice
- **Joy:** Estimate and then weigh 100 grains of rice to find the real mass of one grain

### Lesson 3 — Powers and index notation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write repeated multiplication using index notation and evaluate squares, cubes and powers of 10." (CCSS 6.EE.1; UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** The legend of the chessboard and the rice: 1 grain on the first square, doubling each square — how many on square 64?
- **Key activity:** Build squares with square tiles and cubes with multilink cubes (concrete); record 3 × 3 = 3², 2 × 2 × 2 = 2³ (abstract); explore powers of 2 up to 2¹⁰ and powers of 10 up to 10⁶; name base and index.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which is bigger, 2⁵ or 5²? Show why." → 2⁵ = 32 and 5² = 25, so 2⁵ is bigger; the index is not a multiplier (2⁵ is not 10).
- **Differentiation:** support: build each power with cubes before writing it; a squares chart 1–12 / stretch: compare 3⁴ and 4³ and investigate when aᵇ = bᵃ (2⁴ = 4² = 16)
- **Materials:** square tiles, multilink cubes, a chessboard, grains of rice, calculators
- **Joy:** Doubling the rice on the real chessboard until the pile is too big — then calculating 2⁶³ with a calculator for the last square

### Lesson 4 — Square roots and cube roots
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find square and cube roots of perfect squares and cubes and estimate the square root of any number between two integers." (UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "A square garden has an area of 49 m². How long is its fence on one side?"
- **Key activity:** Use square tiles to make squares of area 16, 25, 36 and find the side (the root); cube roots with multilink cubes (8, 27, 64); estimate √30 between 5 and 6 on a number line, then check with a calculator.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is √50 closer to 7 or to 8? Explain without a calculator." → Closer to 7, because 7² = 49 is only 1 away from 50, while 8² = 64 is 14 away.
- **Differentiation:** support: perfect squares to 100 with tiles and a square-number chart / stretch: estimate √200 to one decimal place by trial and improvement
- **Materials:** square tiles, multilink cubes, number lines, calculators
- **Joy:** "Root race": pairs race to estimate square roots on a number line, then reveal with the calculator for points

### Lesson 5 — Primes, factors and factor trees
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write any whole number up to 200 as a product of its prime factors using a factor tree." (UK KS3 Number; CCSS 6.NS.4 foundation)
- **Hook:** "Every whole number above 1 is built from primes, like molecules from atoms. What is 360 made of?"
- **Key activity:** Sieve of Eratosthenes on a 1–100 square to find primes; build factor trees for 36, 60, 360 using counters grouped into equal arrays; write answers in index form (360 = 2³ × 3² × 5); discover that every tree for the same number ends with the same primes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Aisha's factor tree for 60 starts 6 × 10; Ben's starts 4 × 15. Who will get the right answer?" → Both: they will both end with 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 = 2² × 3 × 5, because prime factorisation is unique.
- **Differentiation:** support: numbers below 50, with a list of primes to 50 to hand / stretch: explain why 1 is not prime and find the smallest number with exactly 10 factors (48)
- **Materials:** 1–100 squares, coloured pencils, counters, prime-number list
- **Joy:** "Prime detective" — the Sieve of Eratosthenes coloured by hand reveals the hidden pattern of primes

### Lesson 6 — Highest common factor and lowest common multiple
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find the HCF and LCM of two numbers using prime factors and a Venn diagram and choose which one a problem needs." (CCSS 6.NS.4; UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "Buses leave the station every 12 and every 18 minutes. Both leave at 9:00. When do they next leave together?"
- **Key activity:** Put the prime factors of 12 and 18 into a two-circle Venn diagram (hoops on the table with prime-number cards); the overlap multiplies to the HCF (6), everything in the diagram multiplies to the LCM (36); solve "tiles" (HCF) and "timetable" (LCM) problems and decide which is which.
- **Check for understanding:** "Two ribbons are 24 cm and 36 cm. You want to cut both into equal pieces as long as possible with nothing left over. HCF or LCM — and why?" → HCF (12 cm), because the piece length must divide both lengths exactly.
- **Differentiation:** support: list multiples and factors first, then use the Venn diagram / stretch: use 12 × 18 = HCF × LCM to explain why this works for any two numbers
- **Materials:** two hoops or drawn Venn circles, prime-number cards, ribbon and scissors, bus timetable card
- **Joy:** Solve a "mystery of the lighthouses" problem — two lights flashing every 8 and 12 seconds

### Lesson 7 — Rounding to decimal places and significant figures
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can round numbers to a given number of decimal places or significant figures and explain which is more sensible in context." (UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "The population of the world is about 8,000,000,000. Why does nobody say 8,045,311,447?"
- **Key activity:** Round on a number line first (where does 3.846 sit between 3.8 and 3.9?); rule for decimal places; significant figures introduced as "the digits that matter from the first non-zero digit" (0.004 72 to 1 s.f. = 0.005); choose sensible rounding for real data (heights, populations, prices).
- **Check for understanding:** "Round 0.0386 to 1 significant figure and to 1 decimal place. Why are the answers different?" → 0.04 (1 s.f.) and 0.0 (1 d.p.); decimal places count from the decimal point, significant figures from the first non-zero digit.
- **Differentiation:** support: zoomed number lines between tenths, rounding to 1 d.p. only / stretch: find the upper and lower bounds of a length given as 7.4 cm to 1 d.p. (7.35 ≤ x < 7.45)
- **Materials:** zoomable number lines, world population and city data cards, calculators
- **Joy:** "Headline writer": turn precise statistics into catchy but honest rounded news headlines

### Lesson 8 — Estimation and checking
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can estimate answers by rounding to 1 significant figure and use estimates to check calculations." (UK KS3 Number; CCSS 6.NS.3)
- **Hook:** A shop receipt with one deliberate error — can you find it in 30 seconds without adding everything?
- **Key activity:** Round each number to 1 s.f. and calculate mentally (e.g. 48.7 × 21.3 ≈ 50 × 20 = 1000); compare with calculator answers; spot unreasonable answers; decide if an estimate is an over- or underestimate.
- **Check for understanding:** "Estimate 612 ÷ 0.48. Is the exact answer bigger or smaller than your estimate? Why?" → 600 ÷ 0.5 = 1200; the exact answer (1275) is bigger, because the dividend was rounded down and the divisor rounded up.
- **Differentiation:** support: estimates with whole numbers only / stretch: estimate (3.9² × 21) ÷ 0.19 and justify over/under
- **Materials:** real shop receipts or printed ones, calculators, mini-whiteboards
- **Joy:** "Estimation supermarket sweep": fill an imaginary basket to as close to a budget as possible without a calculator

### Lesson 9 — Order of operations with powers and brackets
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate expressions with brackets, powers and all four operations in the correct order." (CCSS 6.EE.1, 6.EE.2c; UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "8 ÷ 2(2 + 2)" went viral online — why did people argue? (Write it clearly as 8 ÷ 2 × (2 + 2) to settle it: 16.)
- **Key activity:** Retrieve BIDMAS/PEMDAS; stress that × and ÷ are equal rank and done left to right (and likewise + and −); evaluate expressions with indices; "insert brackets to make it true" puzzles; check with a scientific calculator.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 2 × 3² equal to 36 or 18? Explain." → 18: the index applies only to the 3, so 3² = 9 first, then × 2. (2 × 3)² would be 36.
- **Differentiation:** support: step-by-step "one operation per line" layout with the order card / stretch: using the digits 1, 2, 3, 4 once each with any operations and brackets, make every target number from 1 to 20
- **Materials:** order-of-operations card, scientific calculators, puzzle cards
- **Joy:** The "Four fours" challenge — make as many numbers as possible from four 4s

### Lesson 10 — Mental and written calculation fluency with decimals
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add, subtract, multiply and divide multi-digit decimals fluently using efficient methods." (CCSS 6.NS.2, 6.NS.3)
- **Hook:** "A 2.5 kg bag of rice costs 4.35. What does 1 kg cost?"
- **Key activity:** Model 2.4 × 1.3 as an area on a 10 × 10 grid (pictorial), connect to the written column method; divide decimals by making the divisor a whole number (4.35 ÷ 2.5 = 43.5 ÷ 25) using equivalent division; mixed practice with estimation check.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 43.5 ÷ 25 the same as 4.35 ÷ 2.5?" → Both numbers were multiplied by 10, which keeps the quotient the same (like equivalent fractions: 4.35/2.5 = 43.5/25).
- **Differentiation:** support: decimal × whole number and ÷ whole number first, with place-value counters / stretch: 0.084 ÷ 0.012 and a two-step decimal word problem
- **Materials:** 10 × 10 grid paper, place-value counters (1, 0.1, 0.01), price labels from real shopping
- **Joy:** "Best-buy challenge" using real product labels from home

### Lesson 11 — Problem solving with number: Unit 1 reasoning
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose and combine powers, factors and estimation to solve multi-step problems and explain my reasoning." (UK KS3 Number; IB MYP Criterion A)
- **Hook:** "A square tile pattern uses 144 tiles. How many different rectangles could the same tiles make?"
- **Key activity:** Rich problems: rectangles from 144 tiles (factor pairs), the locker problem (why do only square-numbered lockers stay open?), prime factor codes; work in pairs, then present one solution using "I noticed… so I tried… which shows…".
- **Check for understanding:** "In the locker problem, why do square numbers have an odd number of factors?" → Factors come in pairs, except the square root, which pairs with itself, so the count is odd.
- **Differentiation:** support: 20 lockers modelled with cups turned up/down; factor pairs listed systematically / stretch: generalise the locker problem to n lockers and prove it
- **Materials:** 144 square tiles or squared paper, 20 paper cups, problem cards
- **Joy:** Act out the locker problem with 20 cups as "lockers"

### Lesson 12 — Unit 1 mastery check and fluency
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show mastery of powers, roots, primes, rounding and order of operations, and identify what I need to practise." (UK KS3 Number; CCSS 6.EE.1, 6.NS.4)
- **Hook:** "Beat your own score": a 3-minute fluency sprint on squares, cubes and primes, compared with the first-week baseline.
- **Key activity:** Low-stakes mastery task (below), then self-marking with a model answer; each child writes one "I can" and one "next step" and does targeted practice from a choice board (roots, HCF/LCM, rounding).
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain why 91 is not prime, even though it looks like it might be." → 91 = 7 × 13, so it has factors other than 1 and itself.
- **Differentiation:** support: choice board tasks with worked examples beside them / stretch: "Use prime factorisation to explain why 72 = 2³ × 3² has (3 + 1)(2 + 1) = 12 factors, then find the smallest number with exactly 12 factors" (60 = 2² × 3 × 5)
- **Materials:** mastery sheet, fluency sprint cards, choice-board cards, squared paper
- **Joy:** Personal-best graph started on the classroom wall (or learner's notebook)

**End-of-unit check:** (1) Write 540 as a product of prime factors in index form (2² × 3³ × 5). (2) Find the HCF and LCM of 18 and 30 (6; 90) and say which one answers "when do two events next coincide?" (3) Evaluate 5 + 2 × 3² (23). (4) Round 0.07052 to 2 s.f. (0.071). (5) Estimate 39.6 × 0.48 and say if the estimate is too big or too small (40 × 0.5 = 20, too big: both numbers were rounded up; exact 19.008).

## Unit 2 — Negative numbers (the integers)

**Essential question:** What does it mean for a number to be less than zero, and how can operations with negative numbers make sense?

**Key vocabulary:** integer, positive, negative, opposite, absolute value, zero pair, number line, sea level, credit, debit, product, quotient, ascending, descending

### Lesson 13 — Negative numbers in the real world
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use positive and negative numbers to describe real quantities with opposite directions, and explain what zero means in each context." (CCSS 6.NS.5)
- **Hook:** The Dead Sea shore is about 430 m below sea level; Mount Everest is 8,849 m above. How far apart are they vertically?
- **Key activity:** Sort context cards (temperatures in Oslo and Singapore, floors in a building with basements, bank credits and debts, elevations of the Dead Sea and Mariana Trench) onto a vertical number line; name what zero means in each; write each as a signed number.
- **Check for understanding:** "In the lift, floor −2 is two floors below ground. Is it higher or lower than floor −5? Explain using the number line." → Higher: −2 is closer to 0 and above −5 on a vertical number line.
- **Differentiation:** support: a large vertical thermometer number line from −20 to 20 / stretch: create contexts where zero is not a natural zero (e.g. sea level, a bank balance) and explain why
- **Materials:** large vertical number line or a real thermometer, context cards with world data, atlas
- **Joy:** "Around the world weather": plot today's temperatures in five cities, from Yakutsk to Dubai

### Lesson 14 — Ordering integers and absolute value
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare and order integers and explain absolute value as distance from zero." (CCSS 6.NS.7a–d)
- **Hook:** "Which is bigger: −10 or −3? Which debt would you rather have?"
- **Key activity:** Place integers on a number line; write inequalities (−7 < −2); introduce |−5| = 5 as distance from zero; distinguish "less than" from "smaller absolute value" with debts ("a debt of more than 30" means balance < −30).
- **Check for understanding:** "True or false: −8 > −3 because 8 > 3. Explain." → False: −8 is further left on the number line, so −8 < −3; the bigger the absolute value, the smaller the negative number.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters on a floor number line, ordering only between −10 and 10 / stretch: order −2.5, −2¼, −2.05, −(−2) and explain
- **Materials:** floor number line (tape), integer cards, mini-whiteboards
- **Joy:** "Integer war" card game — red cards negative, black positive; highest integer wins the round

### Lesson 15 — Adding integers with zero pairs
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add positive and negative integers using counters and a number line and explain the result." (CCSS 7.NS.1 foundation; UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "You owe 5 and then someone gives you 3. What is your balance?"
- **Key activity:** Two-colour counters: yellow = +1, red = −1; a yellow and a red make a zero pair. Model −5 + 3 (three zero pairs vanish → −2), then show the same on a number line (start at −5, move 3 right). Move to calculations without counters.
- **Check for understanding:** "Use a zero-pair picture to explain why −4 + 4 = 0." → Four red and four yellow counters make four zero pairs, so nothing is left.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters only for all questions, numbers within ±10 / stretch: find three different pairs of integers with a sum of −3, including one pair using two negatives
- **Materials:** two-colour (double-sided) counters, number lines
- **Joy:** "Hot-air balloon": sandbags (−) and balloons (+) change the height of a paper balloon on a number line

### Lesson 16 — Subtracting integers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can subtract integers, including subtracting a negative, and explain why subtracting a negative increases the result." (UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "Taking away a debt makes you richer. Why?"
- **Key activity:** With counters: 3 − (−2) needs two reds to remove, so add two zero pairs, then take away two reds → 5 yellows. Record pattern: 3 − 2 = 1, 3 − 1 = 2, 3 − 0 = 3, 3 − (−1) = 4… Conclude subtracting a negative is the same as adding its opposite.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain, using counters or a pattern, why 1 − (−4) = 5." → Adding four zero pairs lets you remove four reds, leaving 1 + 4 = 5 yellows; or the pattern rises by 1 each time the number subtracted falls by 1.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters for every question; start with positive − positive crossing zero / stretch: temperature change problems ("from −12 °C to 7 °C is a rise of?") and −3 − (−8) − 4
- **Materials:** double-sided counters, pattern sheets, number lines
- **Joy:** The "debt collector" game: take cards from your opponent — negative cards taken from your hand raise your score

### Lesson 17 — Adding and subtracting integers: fluency and contexts
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add and subtract integers fluently and solve problems about temperature, elevation and money." (CCSS 6.NS.5, 7.NS.1; UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** A submarine at −250 m rises 80 m, then dives 135 m. Where is it now?
- **Key activity:** Fluency practice with mixed calculations; context problems (world temperatures, bank statements, elevation of cities such as La Paz and Baku, which is below sea level); a magic square with negative numbers.
- **Check for understanding:** "The temperature in Ulaanbaatar is −18 °C at night and 7 °C by day. Kai says the difference is 11 degrees. What mistake did he make?" → He calculated 18 − 7; the difference is 7 − (−18) = 25 degrees.
- **Differentiation:** support: number lines available; problems with one step / stretch: complete a 3 × 3 magic square with integers from −4 to 4 (magic sum 0)
- **Materials:** number lines, bank statement cards, world-elevation cards, magic square templates
- **Joy:** Solve a negative-number magic square and invent one for a partner

### Lesson 18 — Multiplying integers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply integers and explain the sign rules using patterns and repeated groups." (CCSS 7.NS.2 foundation; UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "Why is a negative times a negative a positive? Mathematicians argued about this for centuries."
- **Key activity:** 3 × (−2) as three groups of −2 with counters (−6); (−2) × 3 by commutativity; extend a pattern table: −3 × 2 = −6, −3 × 1 = −3, −3 × 0 = 0, −3 × −1 = 3, −3 × −2 = 6 (rises by 3 each time). Summarise sign rules.
- **Check for understanding:** "Continue the pattern: −4 × 2 = −8, −4 × 1 = −4, −4 × 0 = 0, −4 × (−1) = ?, −4 × (−2) = ? Explain the pattern." → 4 and 8: each answer increases by 4 as the second number decreases by 1.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters for positive × negative; a completed pattern table to extend / stretch: work out (−1)¹⁰⁰ and (−1)¹⁰¹ and explain; is (−3)² the same as −3²?
- **Materials:** double-sided counters, pattern table sheets, a multiplication grid from −6 to 6 to complete
- **Joy:** Complete the "four-quadrant multiplication grid" and colour positives and negatives to see the chessboard pattern

### Lesson 19 — Dividing integers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide integers using the inverse of multiplication and apply sign rules." (UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "If −12 ÷ 3 = −4, what must −12 ÷ (−3) be?"
- **Key activity:** Use fact families: 3 × (−4) = −12, so −12 ÷ 3 = −4 and −12 ÷ (−4) = 3; build rules; mixed practice including averages of temperatures (mean of −3, 5, −8, 2 = −1).
- **Check for understanding:** "Find the mean of the temperatures −6, −2, 4 and −4. Explain each step." → Sum = −8; −8 ÷ 4 = −2.
- **Differentiation:** support: write the multiplication fact family first for every division / stretch: find integers a and b with a × b = −24 and a + b = 5 (8 and −3)
- **Materials:** fact-family triangles, weekly winter temperature data from a real city
- **Joy:** "Integer target": dice with + and − faces; reach a target using all four operations

### Lesson 20 — Order of operations with negative numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate expressions with integers, brackets and powers in the correct order." (CCSS 6.EE.2c; UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "Calculator battle: type −3² into two different calculators — why can they disagree?"
- **Key activity:** Distinguish (−3)² = 9 from −3² = −9 (the index applies only to the 3); evaluate multi-step integer expressions; spot-the-error worked examples.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which is correct: −2 × (−3) + 4 = 10 or = −2? Explain." → 10: (−2) × (−3) = 6 first, then 6 + 4 = 10.
- **Differentiation:** support: one operation per line, sign rules card / stretch: build an expression using −1, −2, −3 and 4 that equals exactly −24
- **Materials:** sign-rule cards, scientific calculators, spot-the-error cards
- **Joy:** Be the teacher: mark a "student's" work and write kind, accurate feedback

### Lesson 21 — Coordinates in four quadrants and distances
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plot points in four quadrants, reflect them across axes, and find horizontal and vertical distances between points." (CCSS 6.NS.6b–c, 6.NS.8)
- **Hook:** A treasure map on a grid where the treasure is at (−3, −4) — who can find it first?
- **Key activity:** Retrieve four-quadrant plotting; reflect a point in the x-axis and y-axis and describe the sign change; find distances between points with the same x- or y-coordinate using absolute value (from (−2, 3) to (5, 3) is 7); draw a polygon from vertices and find side lengths.

## Unit 3 — Fractions, decimals & percentages

**Essential question:** How can one amount be written as a fraction, a decimal and a percentage — and how do we choose the most useful form to calculate with?

**Key vocabulary:** numerator, denominator, equivalent, mixed number, improper fraction, reciprocal, quotient, multiplier, percentage increase, percentage decrease, original amount, recurring decimal

### Lesson 25 — Fraction–decimal–percentage equivalence (retrieval)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can convert between fractions, decimals and percentages, including fractions like 3/8 and 1/3, and order mixed forms." (UK KS3 Number; CCSS 6.RP.3c foundation)
- **Hook:** "Which is the better score: 17/20, 0.83 or 84%?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieval on a hundred square and fraction wall (1/4 = 25 squares = 0.25 = 25%); convert eighths by division (3 ÷ 8 = 0.375); meet recurring decimals (1/3 = 0.333…); order mixed forms on a 0–1 number line.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain why 3/8 is 37.5% and not 38%." → 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 exactly, which is 37.5 hundredths; 38% would be 0.38.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction wall and hundred square for tenths, fifths, quarters and halves / stretch: convert 5/12 and 2/7 to decimals and describe the repeating pattern
- **Materials:** fraction wall, hundred squares, fraction strips, calculators
- **Joy:** "FDP dominoes" — match equivalent forms to complete a loop

### Lesson 26 — Adding and subtracting fractions and mixed numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add and subtract fractions and mixed numbers with different denominators, using the lowest common multiple." (UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "A recipe needs 2⅓ cups of flour and you add 1¾ cups more. Will a 4-cup bowl overflow?"
- **Key activity:** Model with fraction strips, then use the LCM (Unit 1) as the common denominator; compare "add whole numbers then fractions" with "convert to improper fractions"; practise renaming when subtracting (3⅕ − 1¾).
- **Check for understanding:** "Leo wrote 2/3 + 1/4 = 3/7. Use a diagram to show why this cannot be right." → 2/3 alone is more than 3/7 (about 0.67 vs 0.43), so adding more can't give 3/7; the correct answer is 8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction strips for denominators up to 12 / stretch: find two unit fractions with different denominators whose sum is 5/6 (1/2 + 1/3)
- **Materials:** fraction strips (paper or plastic), measuring cups, recipe cards
- **Joy:** Measure 2⅓ + 1¾ cups of rice for real and see whether the bowl overflows

### Lesson 27 — Multiplying fractions and mixed numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply fractions and mixed numbers and explain why the product of two proper fractions is smaller than both." (CCSS 5.NF.4 → 6.NS.1; UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "Half of a half of a pizza — how much is that?"
- **Key activity:** Paper folding and area model: fold a sheet into thirds one way and quarters the other to show 2/3 × 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2; simplify before multiplying (cross-cancelling); convert mixed numbers to improper fractions (1½ × 2⅔ = 4).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 2/3 × 3/4 smaller than 3/4?" → You are taking two-thirds of 3/4 — a part of it — so the answer must be less than 3/4.
- **Differentiation:** support: area model on squared paper for every question / stretch: find the area of a rectangle measuring 2¾ m by 3⅓ m (9 1/6 m²) and check by estimation
- **Materials:** A4 paper for folding, squared paper, coloured pencils, fraction strips
- **Joy:** Folding paper models to "see" a fraction of a fraction

### Lesson 28 — Dividing a whole number by a fraction
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide a whole number by a fraction by asking 'how many groups fit?' and explain why the answer can be larger than the number I started with." (CCSS 6.NS.1)
- **Hook:** "You have 3 metres of ribbon. Each bow needs 3/4 m. How many bows can you make?"
- **Key activity:** Cut real ribbon (3 m into 3/4 m pieces → 4 bows); model 3 ÷ 1/4 with fraction strips (12 quarters in 3 wholes); bar model for 2 ÷ 2/5 = 5; record the pattern and notice 3 ÷ 1/4 = 3 × 4.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain, with a drawing, why 4 ÷ 1/2 = 8 and not 2." → There are 2 halves in each whole, so 4 wholes contain 8 halves; 4 × 1/2 would be 2.
- **Differentiation:** support: unit fractions only (÷ 1/2, 1/3, 1/4) with fraction strips / stretch: 5 ÷ 2/3 — what does the remainder 1/3 of a group mean? (7½ groups)
- **Materials:** ribbon, metre stick, scissors, fraction strips, bar-model templates
- **Joy:** Actually making the ribbon bows and checking the calculation

### Lesson 29 — Dividing a fraction by a fraction
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide fractions by fractions using common denominators and the reciprocal method, and explain why 'multiply by the reciprocal' works." (CCSS 6.NS.1)
- **Hook:** "How many 1/6-cup scoops are in 2/3 of a cup of sugar?"
- **Key activity:** Model with measuring scoops and fraction strips: 2/3 = 4/6, so four 1/6 scoops fit; method 1: make common denominators then divide numerators (3/4 ÷ 1/8 = 6/8 ÷ 1/8 = 6); method 2: multiply by the reciprocal; show both give the same answer and why (dividing by 1/8 means "how many eighths", which is × 8).
- **Check for understanding:** "Write a story problem for 3/4 ÷ 1/2 and solve it with a diagram." → e.g. "How many half-cup servings in 3/4 cup?" → 1½ servings (one full half-cup and half of another).
- **Differentiation:** support: common-denominator method only, with strips / stretch: explain why 2/3 ÷ 4/5 is less than 1 without calculating (2/3 is less than 4/5, so fewer than one group fits)
- **Materials:** measuring cups and spoons (including 1/2, 1/3, 1/4 cup), sugar or rice, fraction strips
- **Joy:** "Scoop it": test predictions with real measuring scoops

### Lesson 30 — Dividing mixed numbers and fraction word problems
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve word problems involving division of fractions and mixed numbers and interpret the answer in context." (CCSS 6.NS.1)
- **Hook:** "A path 7½ m long is to be tiled with tiles 5/8 m long. How many tiles?"
- **Key activity:** Bar models to decide the operation; convert mixed numbers to improper fractions; interpret remainders (round up for tiles, down for full servings); sort problems into "×" and "÷".
- **Check for understanding:** "A 2¼ kg bag of rice is split into 3/8 kg portions. Is the answer 6 portions or 27/32 kg? Explain." → 6 portions: we are asking how many 3/8 fit in 2¼ (= 18/8), and 18 ÷ 3 = 6.
- **Differentiation:** support: problems with whole number ÷ unit fraction and drawn bar models / stretch: write a problem where the answer 1 1/3 makes sense, and one where it must be rounded
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, measuring tape, problem cards
- **Joy:** "Question writer": swap self-written fraction problems with a partner

### Lesson 31 — Fractions of amounts and fractional change
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find a fraction of any amount and increase or decrease an amount by a fraction." (UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "A jacket is reduced by 2/5. It used to cost 85. Is the new price above or below 50?"
- **Key activity:** Bar model: split 85 into 5 equal parts (17), take 2 parts away (51 remains); calculate 3/5 of 85 directly and compare; increase by a fraction (add 1/4 → multiply by 5/4).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'decrease by 2/5' the same as 'find 3/5'?" → If 2 of 5 equal parts are removed, 3 of the 5 parts remain.
- **Differentiation:** support: amounts divisible by the denominator with drawn bar models / stretch: a price increases by 1/4 and then decreases by 1/4 — is it back to the start? (No: 100 → 125 → 93.75)
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, store catalogues or flyers, calculators

### Lesson 32 — Percentages of amounts using multipliers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find any percentage of an amount mentally, with a bar model, and using a decimal multiplier." (CCSS 6.RP.3c; UK KS3 Number)
- **Hook:** "A restaurant adds a 12.5% service charge to a 64 bill. How much is that?"
- **Key activity:** Build from 10% and 1% on a percentage bar (mental strategies); connect to multipliers (12.5% = 0.125, so 0.125 × 64 = 8); choose the most efficient method for each question.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain two different ways to find 35% of 80." → 10% = 8, so 30% = 24, 5% = 4, total 28; or 0.35 × 80 = 28.

### Lesson 33 — Percentage increase and decrease
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can increase and decrease amounts by a percentage using a single multiplier." (UK KS3 Number; CCSS 7.RP.3 foundation)
- **Hook:** "A game costs 40 and goes up 20%, then goes down 20% in a sale. Is it back to 40?"

- **Joy:** "The shop's trick": investigate whether "up 50% then down 50%" is a fair deal

### Lesson 34 — One quantity as a percentage of another
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can express one quantity as a fraction and a percentage of another, including test scores and survey results." (CCSS 6.RP.3c; UK KS3 Number)

- **Joy:** Convert your own quiz results across the unit into a percentage "progress line"

### Lesson 35 — Finding the whole from a part (reverse percentages with bar models)

- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find the whole when I know a percentage of it, using a bar model." (CCSS 6.RP.3c)

## Unit 4 — Algebraic expressions

**Essential question:** How can a letter stand for any number, and why does that let us describe patterns and rules for every case at once?

**Key vocabulary:** variable, term, coefficient, constant, expression, like terms, simplify, substitute, expand, factorise, formula, equivalent, sequence, nth term, term-to-term rule

### Lesson 41 — From words to expressions: brackets and order
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write expressions from word descriptions, including ones that need brackets, and explain how the wording decides the order of operations." (CCSS 6.EE.2a–b; UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** "Think of a number, double it, add 6, halve it, take away the number you first thought of… you got 3! How did I know?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieval (Year 5 wrote n + 5, 2n and 3n + 1 with cups and counters): conventions (2n not n2, no × sign). New step: phrases where the order matters — "3 times the sum of n and 4" → 3(n + 4) but "4 more than 3 times n" → 3n + 4; "the difference between n and 7, halved" → (n − 7)/2; "double n, then square" → (2n)² but "square n, then double" → 2n². Translate both ways and test each pair with a value.
- **Check for understanding:** "Write 'multiply n by 3, then subtract the result from 20' and 'subtract 20 from 3 times n'. Are they the same? Test with n = 10." → 20 − 3n and 3n − 20; with n = 10 they give −10 and 10, so they are different expressions.
- **Differentiation:** support: cups-and-counters model for every expression, one operation at a time / stretch: explain the "think of a number" trick with algebra (n → 2n → 2n + 6 → n + 3 → 3), then design a trick using brackets that always ends at 5
- **Materials:** paper cups, counters, expression cards, mini-whiteboards
- **Joy:** Perform the "think of a number" mind-reading trick, then reveal how it works

### Lesson 42 — Terms, coefficients and collecting like terms
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify terms, coefficients and constants and simplify expressions by collecting like terms." (CCSS 6.EE.2b, 6.EE.3; UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** "3 apples + 2 bananas + 4 apples — how would you say that more simply?"
- **Key activity:** Algebra tiles (long rectangles = x, small squares = 1) to model 3x + 2 + 4x − 1; group like tiles; record 7x + 1; include negative tiles (red side); explain why 3x + 2 cannot be simplified to 5x.
- **Check for understanding:** "Raj simplified 4a + 3b + 2a to 9ab. What would you tell him?" → a and b are different variables, so they are unlike terms and can't be combined; 4a + 2a = 6a, so the answer is 6a + 3b.
- **Differentiation:** support: tiles for every expression; only positive terms at first / stretch: simplify 5x² + 3x − 2x² − 7x + 4 and explain why x² and x are unlike
- **Materials:** algebra tiles (or card cut-outs), expression sorting cards
- **Joy:** "Algebra pyramids": each brick is the sum of the two below it

### Lesson 43 — Substitution
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate expressions and formulae by substituting positive and negative values." (CCSS 6.EE.2c; UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** "The formula to convert °C to °F is F = 1.8C + 32. Is 30 °C a hot day in Fahrenheit?"
- **Key activity:** Substitute into expressions (3n + 4 when n = 5 and when n = −2); remember order of operations (2x² when x = 3 is 18, not 36); use real formulae (temperature, taxi fares, P = 2l + 2w).
- **Check for understanding:** "If a = −3, what is a²? Is it 9 or −9? Why?" → 9: a² = (−3) × (−3) = 9.
- **Differentiation:** support: replace the letter with a bracket first, e.g. 3( ) + 4 / stretch: find values of x for which 2x + 5 > x² (e.g. x = 0, 1, 2, 3)
- **Materials:** formula cards, calculators, a thermometer that shows °C and °F
- **Joy:** "Formula machine": feed numbers into a paper machine and predict the output

### Lesson 44 — Writing formulae from tables, including decreasing and negative values
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a formula from a table or a real situation — including quantities that decrease and become negative — and use it to answer questions." (CCSS 6.EE.2, 6.EE.6, 6.EE.9; UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** "A candle 24 cm tall burns down 1.5 cm every hour. When will it be only 6 cm tall?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieval (Year 5 wrote C = 8t + 5 from contexts): tables of values → formula as 'starting value + change × steps'. New step: decreasing situations (h = 24 − 1.5t for the candle; a mountain temperature T = 5 − 3h falling 3 °C per hour and going below zero) and solving questions with the formula; distinguish an expression (3n + 1) from a formula (a rule linking variables with an equals sign).
- **Check for understanding:** "Write a formula for the candle's height h after t hours and find when it is 6 cm tall. Explain your steps." → h = 24 − 1.5t; 24 − 1.5t = 6, so 1.5t = 18 and t = 12 hours.
- **Differentiation:** support: complete a table of values first, then spot the starting value and the change per step / stretch: the temperature starts at 5 °C and falls 3 °C per hour — write a formula for T, find T after 4 hours (−7 °C) and when it reaches −16 °C (after 7 hours)
- **Materials:** a candle (adult-supervised demonstration) or candle pictures, table templates, formula cards, thermometer
- **Joy:** "Candle clock": mark a real candle every 30 minutes and test the class formula

### Lesson 45 — Expanding single brackets
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can expand a single bracket using the distributive law and show it with an area model." (CCSS 6.EE.3; UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** "Work out 7 × 23 in your head. Did you do 7 × 20 + 7 × 3?"
- **Key activity:** Area model for 7 × 23 = 7 × 20 + 7 × 3; then 3(x + 4) with algebra tiles = 3x + 12; include negatives (−2(x − 5) = −2x + 10) and variables outside the bracket (a(a + 3) = a² + 3a).
- **Check for understanding:** "Zara says 4(x + 3) = 4x + 3. Use an area model to show her mistake." → The 4 multiplies both terms: the rectangle has parts 4 × x and 4 × 3, so 4x + 12.
- **Differentiation:** support: tiles and grid method for every question / stretch: expand and simplify 3(2x − 1) − 2(x − 4) (4x + 5)
- **Materials:** algebra tiles, grid templates, squared paper
- **Joy:** "Mental maths superpower": use the distributive law to multiply big numbers in your head

### Lesson 46 — Factorising expressions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can factorise expressions by taking out the highest common factor." (CCSS 6.EE.3, 6.NS.4; UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** "Expanding is building the rectangle. Factorising is finding its sides. Can you find them for 12x + 18?"
- **Key activity:** Arrange algebra tiles for 6x + 9 into a rectangle (3 by 2x + 3); link to HCF (Unit 1); factorise fully (12x + 18 = 6(2x + 3), not 2(6x + 9)); check by expanding.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 2(6x + 9) fully factorised? Explain." → No: 6x + 9 still has a common factor of 3, so fully factorised it is 6(2x + 3).
- **Differentiation:** support: use tiles to make rectangles and find the sides / stretch: factorise 8a² + 12a (4a(2a + 3))
- **Materials:** algebra tiles, HCF prompt card
- **Joy:** "Reverse it": partners expand, then challenge each other to factorise back

### Lesson 47 — Equivalent expressions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can decide whether two expressions are equivalent by simplifying and by substituting." (CCSS 6.EE.4)
- **Hook:** "Are 2(x + 3) and 2x + 3 the same? One test value might fool you. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Sort expression cards into equivalent groups; test with several values (substituting x = 0 alone can mislead); prove using algebraic rules; write three equivalent forms of a perimeter.
- **Check for understanding:** "4x + 2 and 2(2x + 1) — equivalent? Prove it two ways." → Yes: expanding 2(2x + 1) gives 4x + 2; and substituting x = 3 gives 14 both times.
- **Differentiation:** support: substitution only with a table of values / stretch: find the value where 3x + 1 and x + 7 are equal (x = 3) and explain why that doesn't make them equivalent

### Lesson 48 — Index notation in algebra
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write and simplify products using index notation, e.g. a × a × a = a³ and 3a × 2a = 6a²." (CCSS 6.EE.1; UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** "Is x + x the same as x × x? Test with x = 2, then x = 3."
- **Key activity:** Contrast x + x = 2x with x × x = x² using tiles (two long tiles side by side vs a square tile); simplify products like 2a × 5b = 10ab and 3y × y = 3y²; substitute to check.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are 2x and x² equal when x = 2 but not when x = 3?" → 2 × 2 = 4 and 2² = 4, but 2 × 3 = 6 while 3² = 9 — they are different expressions that happen to agree at x = 2 (and x = 0).

### Lesson 49 — Sequences: term-to-term rules
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can generate sequences from a term-to-term rule and describe linear and non-linear sequences." (UK KS3 Algebra; CCSS 5.OA.3 → 6.EE)
- **Hook:** "1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8… This sequence is hiding in sunflowers and pine cones. What comes next?"
- **Key activity:** Generate sequences from rules; classify as arithmetic (constant difference, including negative differences), geometric (doubling) or other (square numbers, Fibonacci); look for Fibonacci numbers in a real pine cone or sunflower image.

### Lesson 50 — The nth term of a linear sequence
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find and use the nth term of a linear sequence and decide whether a number is in the sequence." (UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** "Is 1000 in the sequence 5, 8, 11, 14…? You have 60 seconds and no listing allowed."
- **Key activity:** Build the pattern with multilink cubes; connect the constant difference to the multiplier (difference 3 → 3n); compare 3n (3, 6, 9…) with the sequence to find the adjustment (+2) → 3n + 2; test membership by solving 3n + 2 = 1000 (n = 332.67…, not a whole number, so no).

### Lesson 51 — Visual patterns and generalising

- **Joy:** "Algebra architect": design a room plan with algebraic dimensions

### Lesson 53 — Algebraic reasoning: always, sometimes, never
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use algebra to prove simple general statements, such as the sum of three consecutive integers is a multiple of 3." (UK KS3 Algebra; IB MYP Criterion C)

## Unit 6 — Linear equations, inequalities & graphs

**Essential question:** How can we find an unknown number by keeping an equation balanced, and how does a graph show a relationship between two quantities?

**Key vocabulary:** equation, solve, solution, inverse operation, balance, unknown, inequality, less than, greater than, at most, at least, independent variable, dependent variable, table of values, linear

### Lesson 61 — One- and two-step equations (retrieval): negative, decimal and fractional solutions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve one- and two-step equations with the balance model, including those whose solutions are negative, decimal or fractional, and check by substituting." (CCSS 6.EE.5, 6.EE.7; UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** A real pan balance with a paper bag of unknown mass on one side and 250 g of weights on the other: "What's in the bag?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieval (Year 5 solved x + 7 = 15 and 3x + 4 = 19 with cups and counters): pan balance and algebra tiles on an equation mat — do the same to both sides. New step: solutions that are not positive whole numbers — x + 9 = 3 (x = −6), 4x = 10 (x = 2.5), 2x + 9 = 3 (x = −3), 3x − 1 = 6 (x = 7/3) — always checked by substituting.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must you do the same thing to both sides, and how can you be sure x = −3 solves 2x + 9 = 3?" → The two sides must stay equal (balanced); substituting gives 2 × (−3) + 9 = −6 + 9 = 3, which matches the right-hand side.
- **Differentiation:** support: physical balance and counters, positive whole-number solutions first / stretch: 0.4x = 6 (x = 15) and 5 − 2x = 11 (x = −3)
- **Materials:** pan balance, 10 g/50 g masses or counters, paper cups, algebra tiles, equation mats
- **Joy:** "What's in the bag?" — solve with the balance, then open the bag to check

### Lesson 62 — Equations with fractions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve equations in which the unknown is divided, such as x/4 + 3 = 7 and (2x + 1)/3 = 5, by undoing the operations in reverse order." (UK KS3 Algebra; CCSS 7.EE.4a foundation)
- **Hook:** "I think of a number, divide it by 4 and add 3. I get 7. What was my number?"
- **Key activity:** Function machines run backwards and bar models (a bar split into 4 equal parts, each x/4), then the balance method: x/4 + 3 = 7 → x/4 = 4 → x = 16; (2x + 1)/3 = 5 → 2x + 1 = 15 → x = 7; (x − 2)/5 = −1 → x = −3; ¾x − 2 = 7 → ¾x = 9 → x = 12.
- **Check for understanding:** "In (2x + 1)/3 = 5, why do we multiply both sides by 3 before subtracting 1?" → The whole of 2x + 1 was divided by 3, so dividing was the last step; undo it first to get 2x + 1 = 15, then subtract 1 and divide by 2 (x = 7).
- **Differentiation:** support: one-step equations such as x/5 = 4 with bar models / stretch: (x + 4)/3 = (x − 2)/2 — multiply both sides by 6 (2x + 8 = 3x − 6, so x = 14) and check
- **Materials:** function-machine cards, bar-model templates, algebra tiles, equation mats
- **Joy:** "Number-thinking duel": set each other "I think of a number, divide it by…" puzzles

### Lesson 63 — Equations with brackets
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve equations with brackets by expanding first or dividing first, and choose the more efficient way." (UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** "3(x + 2) = 21 — two methods, same answer. Which is faster?"
- **Key activity:** Solve by expanding (3x + 6 = 21) and by dividing both sides by 3 first (x + 2 = 7); compare efficiency; include negatives and fractions.
- **Check for understanding:** "For 5(x − 1) = 4, which method is easier and why?" → Expanding (5x − 5 = 4, x = 9/5) avoids dividing 4 by 5 early; both methods give x = 1.8.
- **Differentiation:** support: tiles to model 3(x + 2) as three groups / stretch: 2(3x − 1) + 4 = 3(x + 5) (x = 13/3)
- **Materials:** algebra tiles, equation mats, method-comparison sheet
- **Joy:** "Method race": one partner expands, one divides first — who finishes first?

### Lesson 64 — Unknowns on both sides
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve equations with the unknown on both sides by removing the smaller unknown term first." (UK KS3 Algebra)
- **Hook:** "Two phone plans: 20 + 3m and 5 + 6m, where m is gigabytes used. When do they cost the same?"
- **Key activity:** Balance model with cups on both sides: 3x + 4 = x + 10 → remove x from both sides → 2x + 4 = 10 → x = 3; interpret the hook (20 + 3m = 5 + 6m → m = 5 GB).
- **Check for understanding:** "In 5x + 2 = 2x + 14, what should you subtract from both sides first, and why?" → 2x, so the unknown remains on one side only with a positive coefficient: 3x + 2 = 14, x = 4.

## Unit 7 — Ratio, rate & proportion

**Essential question:** How can we compare quantities multiplicatively, and how do ratios, rates and percentages all describe the same idea?

**Key vocabulary:** ratio, part, whole, simplest form, equivalent ratio, unit ratio, rate, unit rate, best buy, direct proportion, inverse proportion, scale factor, scale drawing, map scale, speed, constant of proportionality

### Lesson 77 — Ratio and three-part ratios (retrieval)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use ratio notation for two or three quantities, write each part as a fraction of the whole, and explain how a ratio differs from a fraction." (CCSS 6.RP.1; UK KS3 Ratio)
- **Hook:** "The flag of a country is exactly 2 : 3. What does that mean for a flag 60 cm high?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieval (Year 5 introduced ratio and its link to fractions): two-colour multilink trains (3 red : 2 blue; red is 3/5 of the whole, not 3/2). New step: three-part ratios with three colours (2 : 3 : 5 → 2/10, 3/10 and 5/10 of the whole) in recipes, concrete mixes and flag stripes; write a ratio from a fraction (⅖ of a class are boys → boys : girls = 2 : 3).
- **Check for understanding:** "In a class the ratio of boys to girls is 2 : 3. What fraction are girls? Explain why it isn't 3/2 or 2/3." → 3/5: there are 5 equal parts in total and 3 of them are girls.
- **Differentiation:** support: cube trains for every ratio / stretch: a fruit mix is apples : pears : plums = 2 : 3 : 5 and a quarter of the plums are removed — what is the new ratio in whole numbers? (2 : 3 : 3.75 = 8 : 12 : 15)

- **Joy:** "Flag ratio investigation": measure real flag proportions (e.g. Switzerland is square 1 : 1; Nepal is not a rectangle)

### Lesson 78 — Equivalent ratios and simplest form
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can simplify ratios, including those with different units and decimals, and find equivalent ratios." (CCSS 6.RP.3a; UK KS3 Ratio)
- **Hook:** "One recipe uses 200 g of flour to 150 g of butter. Another uses 4 cups to 3 cups. Are they the same ratio?"
- **Key activity:** Simplify using HCF (200 : 150 = 4 : 3); convert units first (50 cm : 2 m = 50 : 200 = 1 : 4); remove decimals (1.5 : 2 = 3 : 4); build ratio tables (double number lines).
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 20 minutes : 1 hour the same as 20 : 1? Explain." → No: convert to the same units first — 20 min : 60 min = 1 : 3.
- **Differentiation:** support: cubes and a ratio table with doubling / stretch: simplify 2/3 : 5/6 (4 : 5)
- **Materials:** multilink cubes, ratio tables, measuring tape, recipe cards
- **Joy:** "Paint mixing": mix two paint colours in 1 : 2 and 2 : 4 to see they look the same

### Lesson 79 — Ratio tables and double number lines
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use ratio tables and double number lines to find missing values in equivalent ratios." (CCSS 6.RP.3a)
- **Hook:** "A recipe for 4 people needs 300 g of rice. How much for 10 people?"
- **Key activity:** Double number line for the recipe (4 ↔ 300, 2 ↔ 150, 10 ↔ 750); ratio tables with × and ÷ and addition of columns; plot the pairs on a graph and notice a straight line through the origin.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do the points from a ratio table lie on a straight line through (0, 0)?" → Each pair has the same multiplicative relationship (y = 75x here), and 0 people need 0 g.
- **Differentiation:** support: ratio table with steps partly filled / stretch: 7 people need 525 g — how much for 12? (900 g, via unit rate)
- **Materials:** double-number-line templates, graph paper, kitchen scales, rice
- **Joy:** Scale a real family recipe and cook it (link to Life Skills)

### Lesson 80 — Ratio problems: total, one part or difference known (retrieval)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve ratio problems whether I know the total, one part or the difference, choosing the bar model that fits." (UK KS3 Ratio; CCSS 6.RP.3)
- **Hook:** "Two sisters earn 60 by washing cars. One worked 2 hours, the other 3. How should they share it fairly?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieval (Year 5 taught each type in a separate lesson): bar models for the three types side by side — total known (60 in 2 : 3 → 12 per part → 24 and 36), one part known (cement : sand : gravel = 1 : 2 : 4 with 18 kg of sand → 9 kg per part → 36 kg of gravel), difference known (red : blue = 3 : 5 with 12 more blue → 6 per part → 48 beads). Pupils sort a mixed card set by type before solving.
- **Check for understanding:** "Red and blue beads are in the ratio 3 : 5. There are 12 more blue than red. How many beads altogether?" → The difference is 2 parts = 12, so 1 part = 6, and the total is 8 parts = 48.
- **Differentiation:** support: two-part ratios with bar models already drawn / stretch: share 1,000 in the ratio ½ : ⅓ (600 and 400)
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, counters, play money, problem-type sort cards
- **Joy:** Fair-share role play with play money

### Lesson 81 — Ratio problems where the ratio changes
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve problems where quantities are added or removed so that the ratio changes, using 'before and after' bar models or an equation." (UK KS3 Ratio; Singapore Secondary 1)
- **Hook:** "Amir and Bea have stickers in the ratio 3 : 4. Amir is given 10 more and now they have the same number. How many did each have at first?"
- **Key activity:** Bar models drawn before and after: 3 bars and 4 bars; adding 10 to Amir makes the bars equal, so 1 bar = 10 → 30 and 40. Then 'one quantity unchanged' problems: boys : girls = 5 : 3; 6 girls join and the ratio becomes 5 : 4 — the boys' 5 parts don't change, the girls grow from 3 parts to 4, so 1 part = 6 → 30 boys and 18 girls at first. Compare with the equation 3k + 6 = 4k.
- **Check for understanding:** "Red : blue counters = 2 : 5. After 9 red counters are added the ratio is 1 : 1. How many blue counters are there? Explain with a bar model." → Blue (5 parts) didn't change; red grew from 2 parts to 5 parts, so 3 parts = 9 and 1 part = 3; there are 15 blue counters.
- **Differentiation:** support: two-part ratios with the 'after' bar model drawn / stretch: both quantities change — A : B = 3 : 2; 5 is taken from each and the ratio becomes 2 : 1 (A = 15, B = 10)
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, two-colour counters, Cuisenaire rods
- **Joy:** "Before and after" puzzle cards — pairs write one for another pair to solve

### Lesson 82 — Unit rates and best buys
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find unit rates — price per item, metres per second, price per 100 ml — including rates that need unit conversions, and use them to compare value." (CCSS 6.RP.2, 6.RP.3b)
- **Hook:** "A cheetah covers 120 m in 5 s. A car does 90 km per hour. Who is faster?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieval (Year 5 compared pack prices by unit price): unit rate = "per 1" (120 m in 5 s = 24 m/s). New step: rates that need conversions (90 km/h = 90,000 m ÷ 3,600 s = 25 m/s) and best buys with awkward sizes (500 ml for 1.20 vs 1.5 L for 3.30), discussing when the "best buy" isn't best (waste, storage).
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain two different ways to compare 500 ml for 1.20 with 1.5 L for 3.30." → Price per 100 ml (0.24 vs 0.22) or scale the small bottle up to 1.5 L (3.60 vs 3.30); the big bottle is better value.
- **Differentiation:** support: whole-number unit rates with a ratio table / stretch: convert 25 m/s into km/h explaining each step (90 km/h), then a three-way comparison including a "buy 2 get 1 free" offer
- **Materials:** stopwatch, trundle wheel or tape measure, product labels and packaging, calculators
- **Joy:** Measure your own running speed over 20 m and compare with animals

### Lesson 83 — Inverse proportion
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise inverse proportion (when one quantity doubles, the other halves) and solve problems using the constant product." (UK KS3 Ratio; Singapore Secondary 1)
- **Hook:** "4 painters take 6 days to paint a house. How long would 8 painters take? And 3 painters?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: 24 cubes arranged as 4 rows of 6, 8 rows of 3 and 3 rows of 8 — the total (the job: 24 painter-days) never changes. Pictorial: a table where x × y is constant, beside a direct-proportion table where y ÷ x is constant. Abstract: 8 painters take 24 ÷ 8 = 3 days, 3 painters take 8 days (assuming everyone works at the same rate); a fixed 60 km journey takes 2 h at 30 km/h and 1.5 h at 40 km/h.
- **Check for understanding:** "Are 'the number of people sharing a 60-dollar prize' and 'the amount each gets' in direct or inverse proportion? Explain with numbers." → Inverse: 2 people get 30 each, 4 get 15, 6 get 10 — doubling the people halves each share, and people × share is always 60.
- **Differentiation:** support: build each arrangement with cubes and fill in the table / stretch: 3 identical pipes fill a tank in 40 minutes — how long do 5 pipes take? (24 minutes) Explain why adding pipes can never make the time zero.
- **Materials:** multilink cubes, table templates, problem cards, stopwatches
- **Joy:** "Many hands": time 1, 2 and 4 pupils sorting a box of counters and test whether it really is inverse proportion

### Lesson 84 — Direct proportion
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise when two quantities are in direct proportion and use the constant of proportionality." (CCSS 6.RP.3 → 7.RP.2; UK KS3 Ratio)
- **Hook:** "Is your age in proportion to your height? Is the cost of apples in proportion to their mass?"
- **Key activity:** Test tables: if y ÷ x is constant (and 0 ↔ 0), it's direct proportion; graph → straight line through origin; examples and non-examples (taxi fares with a fixed fee are not proportional).
- **Check for understanding:** "A taxi charges 3 plus 2 per km. Is cost in direct proportion to distance? Explain." → No: 0 km still costs 3, so the graph doesn't pass through the origin and cost ÷ distance is not constant.
- **Differentiation:** support: check the ratio table column by column / stretch: write the equation y = kx for a proportional table and interpret k in context
- **Materials:** graph paper, data tables, kitchen scales and apples or rice
- **Joy:** "Proportional or not?" card sort with real-world claims

### Lesson 85 — Scaling with fractional and decimal scale factors

- **Hook:** A recipe for shakshuka (or a family dish) serves 4. You're cooking for 6.

### Lesson 86 — Scale drawings

### Lesson 87 — Map scales: ratio, 'cm to km' and working backwards

## Unit 8 — Angles, parallel lines & constructions

**Essential question:** How can we use known angle facts and precise constructions to prove and create exact shapes?

**Key vocabulary:** acute, obtuse, reflex, protractor, vertex, vertically opposite, parallel, transversal, alternate, corresponding, co-interior, interior angle, exterior angle, polygon, regular, three-figure bearing, back bearing, construct, perpendicular bisector, angle bisector, compass, locus

### Lesson 95 — Measuring and drawing angles accurately
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can measure and draw angles, including reflex angles, to the nearest degree." (UK KS3 Geometry)
- **Hook:** "A skateboarder does a '540'. How many full turns is that?"
- **Key activity:** Estimate first (acute/obtuse/reflex); measure with a 180° protractor, choosing the right scale; draw reflex angles as 360° minus the smaller angle; label angles using three letters (∠ABC).
- **Check for understanding:** "How would you draw a 290° angle with a 180° protractor?" → Draw 360° − 290° = 70° and mark the reflex angle on the other side.
- **Differentiation:** support: 360° angle measurers and estimation first / stretch: draw a triangle given two angles and a side (ASA) accurately
- **Materials:** protractors (180° and 360°), rulers, sharp pencils
- **Joy:** "Angle estimation darts": estimate, measure, score by closeness

### Lesson 96 — Angle facts retrieval: lines, points, triangles and quadrilaterals
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find missing angles using angles on a line, around a point, vertically opposite angles, and the angle sums of triangles and quadrilaterals — giving a geometric reason for every step." (CCSS 7.G.5 foundation; UK KS3 Geometry)
- **Hook:** "Tear the corners off a paper triangle and a paper quadrilateral. What do they always make — and why are vertically opposite angles always equal?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieval (Year 5 proved these facts and used them with algebra): straws and split pins, tear-and-arrange corners; multi-step chains written with reasons ("angles in a triangle add to 180°"; "vertically opposite angles are equal"); isosceles triangles with two possible answers.
- **Check for understanding:** "An isosceles triangle has one angle of 40°. What could the other two be? Is there more than one answer?" → Yes, two: 40° and 100° (if 40° is a base angle) or 70° and 70° (if 40° is the apex angle).
- **Differentiation:** support: straws, paper shapes and a protractor to confirm, one-step problems / stretch: vertically opposite angles are 3x + 10 and 5x − 30 — find x (20), then use the angle in a triangle problem
- **Materials:** straws, split pins, paper triangles and quadrilaterals, scissors, protractors, angle puzzle cards
- **Joy:** "Angle chains": follow a chain of reasons to find a hidden angle

### Lesson 97 — Three-figure bearings
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can measure and draw three-figure bearings clockwise from north and use them with a scale drawing to describe a journey." (UK KS3 Geometry and Measures; Geography link)
- **Hook:** "A ship's radio says 'heading 135'. Which way is that — and why do sailors and pilots always use three digits?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: a floor compass rose — pupils face north and turn clockwise through 090°, 180° and 270°. Pictorial: draw a north line at the starting point, measure clockwise with a 360° protractor, and always write three figures (045°, not 45°). Abstract: bearings between places on a scale map (1 cm : 1 km); the back bearing differs by 180° (070° and 250°).
- **Check for understanding:** "The bearing of B from A is 070°. Jo says the bearing of A from B is also 070°. Explain why she is wrong and find it." → Going back you face the opposite way, so add 180°: 250° — measured clockwise from a north line drawn at B.
- **Differentiation:** support: bearings that are multiples of 45° on a compass rose with the eight points labelled / stretch: a boat sails 5 km on a bearing of 060° then 4 km on 150° — make a scale drawing and measure the distance and bearing back to the start (about 6.4 km on about 279°)
- **Materials:** floor compass rose, 360° protractors, rulers, scale maps, a magnetic compass
- **Joy:** "Orienteering": follow a bearing-and-distance course around the playground to a hidden treasure (PE and Geography link)

### Lesson 98 — Parallel lines: corresponding and alternate angles
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify corresponding, alternate and co-interior angles on parallel lines and use them to find missing angles." (UK KS3 Geometry; CCSS 8.G.5 foundation)
- **Hook:** "Look at a zebra crossing or railway tracks crossed by a road: which angles are equal?"
- **Key activity:** Draw two parallel lines (using both edges of a ruler) and a transversal; measure all eight angles; discover corresponding (F-shape) are equal, alternate (Z-shape) are equal, co-interior (C-shape) add to 180°; use tracing paper to slide and rotate angles to confirm.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are alternate angles equal? Use corresponding and vertically opposite angles." → An angle's corresponding angle is vertically opposite its alternate angle, so all three are equal.
- **Differentiation:** support: coloured highlighting of F, Z and C shapes; tracing paper / stretch: prove the angle sum of a triangle using a line parallel to one side
- **Materials:** rulers, protractors, tracing paper, coloured pencils
- **Joy:** "Angle hunt" photos: find parallel lines and transversals around the house or street

### Lesson 99 — Angle reasoning with parallel lines
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve multi-step angle problems and write a chain of reasons." (UK KS3 Geometry; IB MYP Criterion C)
- **Hook:** "Prove it" — a diagram with one given angle; find all the others with reasons.
- **Key activity:** Model writing reasons ("angle ABC = 65° because alternate angles are equal"); pairs solve multi-step problems; peer review reasoning for completeness.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'because it looks the same' not a valid reason?" → Diagrams may not be drawn to scale; a reason must be a geometric fact that is always true.
- **Differentiation:** support: reason bank card / stretch: problems where algebra is needed (co-interior angles 2x + 10 and 3x − 30 → x = 40)
- **Materials:** angle problem cards, reason bank card
- **Joy:** "Proof relay": each teammate adds one reasoned step

### Lesson 100 — Interior and exterior angles of polygons
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate interior and exterior angle sums of polygons and the angles of regular polygons." (UK KS3 Geometry)
- **Hook:** "Why do bees build hexagons and why can't they use regular pentagons?"

### Lesson 117 — Area of rectangles and triangles (retrieval and why the formula works)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find the area of right-angled and non-right-angled triangles and explain why the area is half of base × perpendicular height." (CCSS 6.G.1; UK KS3 Geometry)
- **Hook:** "Two triangles with the same base and height but very different shapes — can they have the same area?"

### Lesson 118 — Area of parallelograms and trapezia

### Lesson 120 — Circles: parts and circumference

### Lesson 121 — Area of a circle

## Unit 11 — Probability

**Essential question:** How can we measure how likely something is, and when should we trust theory versus experiment?

**Key vocabulary:** probability, outcome, event, equally likely, fair, biased, impossible, certain, theoretical probability, experimental probability, relative frequency, trial, sample space, mutually exclusive

### Lesson 135 — The language and scale of probability
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can place events on a probability scale from 0 to 1 and justify their position." (UK KS3 Probability; CCSS 7.SP.5 foundation)
- **Hook:** "Will it snow in Singapore tomorrow? Will the sun rise? Will a coin land heads?"
- **Key activity:** Place event cards on a 0–1 washing line (impossible, unlikely, even chance, likely, certain); write probabilities as fractions, decimals and percentages; discuss events whose likelihood depends on where you live.
- **Check for understanding:** "Can a probability be 1.2 or −0.3? Explain." → No: probabilities run from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain); nothing is more than certain or less than impossible.
- **Differentiation:** support: word labels on the line and a fraction strip beside it / stretch: explain why "50-50" doesn't mean "equally likely" for every event with two outcomes (e.g. passing a test)
- **Materials:** washing line and pegs, event cards, fraction strips
- **Joy:** "Probability washing line" debate

### Lesson 136 — Theoretical probability and equally likely outcomes
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate the probability of an event when all outcomes are equally likely." (UK KS3 Probability; CCSS 7.SP.7a foundation)
- **Hook:** "A bag has 3 red, 5 blue and 2 green counters. Which colour are you most likely to pick?"
- **Key activity:** P(event) = number of favourable outcomes / total outcomes; spinners, dice, counters in bags; check that probabilities of all outcomes add to 1.
- **Check for understanding:** "P(red) = 3/10. What is P(not red)? Explain why." → 7/10: an event either happens or doesn't, so the two probabilities add to 1.
- **Differentiation:** support: physical bags of counters to count from / stretch: "How many green counters must be added so P(green) = 1/2?" (6)
- **Materials:** cloth bags, coloured counters, dice, spinners
- **Joy:** "Design a bag" so that a friend's target probability is true

### Lesson 137 — Sample spaces for two events
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can list all outcomes of two events in a sample space diagram and use it to find probabilities." (UK KS3 Probability; CCSS 7.SP.8 foundation)
- **Hook:** "Roll two dice and add. Which total is most likely? Place your bets!"
- **Key activity:** Predict; roll two dice 50 times and tally totals; draw the 6 × 6 sample space; P(total 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 is most likely; compare with class results.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a total of 7 more likely than a total of 2?" → Six combinations make 7 ((1,6), (2,5), …, (6,1)) but only one makes 2 ((1,1)).
- **Differentiation:** support: a partly filled sample space grid / stretch: find P(product is even) for two dice (27/36 = 3/4)
- **Materials:** pairs of dice (two colours), sample space grids, tally sheets
- **Joy:** "Horse race" dice game with horses numbered 2–12

### Lesson 138 — Experimental probability and relative frequency
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can estimate probability from experiments using relative frequency and explain why more trials give a better estimate." (UK KS3 Probability; CCSS 7.SP.6 foundation)
- **Hook:** "Drop a drawing pin 50 times. Does it land point up or point down more often? There's no way to calculate it — only to test."
- **Key activity:** Run trials (drawing pins or bottle caps); record relative frequency after 10, 20, 50, 100 trials; graph it and watch it settle; pool class data.
- **Check for understanding:** "After 10 flips of a fair coin you got 7 heads. Is the coin biased? Explain." → Not necessarily: 10 trials is too few; with more trials the relative frequency should approach 0.5 if it is fair.
- **Differentiation:** support: tally chart with a calculator for relative frequencies / stretch: expected number of heads in 500 flips, and how far from that would make you suspicious
- **Materials:** drawing pins (supervised) or bottle caps, coins, tally sheets, graph paper, spreadsheet
- **Joy:** "Cap-flip championship" — pool data to find the true probability

### Lesson 139 — Expected outcomes
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can predict the expected number of outcomes from a probability and a number of trials." (UK KS3 Probability; CCSS 7.SP.6 foundation)
- **Hook:** "A spinner lands on blue with probability 0.3. In 200 spins, how many blues do you expect?"
- **Key activity:** Expected = P × number of trials; compare expected and actual results with real spinners; use in context (a factory's faulty rate, weather forecasts).
- **Check for understanding:** "You expect 60 blues in 200 spins but get 54. Is the spinner wrong?" → No: expected results are an average; real results vary around them.
- **Differentiation:** support: whole-number results with probabilities as fractions / stretch: a game costs 1 to play; P(win 5) = 0.15 — is it worth playing in the long run? (expected win 0.75 per game, so no)

- **Joy:** Build and test your own spinner

### Lesson 140 — Fair and unfair games

**End-of-unit check:** (1) For 4, 7, 7, 9, 12, 15 find the mean (9), median (8), mode (7), range (11). (2) Draw a box plot from given quartiles. (3) Explain which average best represents house prices in a town with a few mansions (median). (4) Identify two ways a given graph misleads. (5) Write a comparison of two data sets using an average and a measure of spread.

## Unit 13 — Graduation problem solving & project

**Essential question:** How can I use all the mathematics I know to solve problems I have never seen before, and to improve something real?

**Key vocabulary:** strategy, model, assumption, conjecture, generalise, justify, evaluate, efficiency, precision

### Lesson 161 — Problem-solving strategies toolkit
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose from a toolkit of strategies (draw a diagram, work backwards, simplify, look for patterns, trial and improvement, be systematic) to tackle unfamiliar problems." (IB MYP Criterion A; Pólya's problem-solving method)
- **Hook:** "How many squares are on a chessboard? (Not 64!)"
- **Key activity:** Pólya's four steps (understand, plan, do, look back); solve the chessboard problem by simplifying (1 × 1, 2 × 2 boards…) and finding the pattern (sum of squares 1² + … + 8² = 204); make a strategy poster.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which strategy helped most on the chessboard problem and why?" → Simplifying to smaller boards revealed a pattern of square numbers that could be extended.
- **Differentiation:** support: a 3 × 3 and 4 × 4 board drawn to count on / stretch: how many rectangles are on a chessboard? (1,296)
- **Materials:** chessboard, squared paper, strategy poster template
- **Joy:** The chessboard reveal

### Lesson 162 — Multi-strand problems: number and algebra
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve problems that combine number, algebra and ratio." (IB MYP Criterion A; UK KS3)
- **Hook:** "A bakery sells croissants at 1.80 and muffins at 2.40. Sales yesterday were 132 for 65 items. How many of each?"
- **Key activity:** Solve with a table (systematic trial), then with algebra (1.8c + 2.4(65 − c) = 132 → c = 40, m = 25); compare methods.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the algebraic method more powerful than trial and error here?" → It finds the exact answer directly and works for any numbers, while trial may take many attempts.
- **Differentiation:** support: a systematic table scaffold / stretch: write and solve a similar problem with three items and an extra condition
- **Materials:** problem cards, calculators
- **Joy:** "Bakery owner" role play

### Lesson 163 — Multi-strand problems: geometry and measures
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve problems that combine geometry, measurement and algebra." (IB MYP Criterion A; UK KS3)
- **Hook:** "A goat is tied to the corner of a 4 m × 6 m barn with a 5 m rope. What area of grass can it eat?"
- **Key activity:** Model with a scale drawing and compasses; the goat can graze ¾ of a circle of radius 5 m (it can't pass through the barn) plus ¼ of a circle of radius 1 m around the 4 m wall's corner; calculate (≈ 58.9 + 0.8 ≈ 59.7 m²).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the main region three-quarters of a circle, not a whole circle?" → The barn occupies the quarter of the circle behind the corner the goat is tied to.
- **Differentiation:** support: a rope shorter than both walls (just ¾ of a circle) / stretch: extend to a rope of 7 m
- **Materials:** compasses, squared paper, calculators, string and a box model
- **Joy:** Model it with a toy animal, string and a box

### Lesson 164 — Multi-strand problems: data and probability
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use data and probability to make and defend a decision." (IB MYP Criterion D; CCSS 6.SP)
- **Hook:** "Your school sports day: should the relay team be chosen by mean time, best time or consistency?"
- **Key activity:** Analyse sprint data for candidates (mean, best, IQR or MAD); make a recommendation with evidence; debate.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might you choose a runner with a slightly slower mean but a much smaller spread?" → They are more reliable: less risk of a very slow run on the day.
- **Differentiation:** support: two candidates with a guided comparison / stretch: add a probability estimate of each runner beating a target time
- **Materials:** data sets, calculators, spreadsheet
- **Joy:** Run real 50 m sprints and choose a team from your own data (PE link)

### Lesson 165 — Graduation maths project: launch
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a real-world maths project that uses at least three strands of this year's maths." (IB MYP Criteria B, D)
- **Hook:** "Plan the school's (or family's) graduation celebration — on a budget, to scale, backed by data."
- **Key activity:** Choose a project: (a) plan a graduation event (budget with percentages, scaled floor plan, ratio recipes, survey of guests' preferences); (b) design a sustainable garden or rainwater system (area, volume, cost); (c) plan a trip abroad (currency, speed–distance–time, budget). Write a plan and success criteria.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which three strands of maths will your project use and where?" → A specific answer, e.g. ratio for recipes, area for the floor plan, statistics for the survey.
- **Differentiation:** support: project (a) with a structured planner / stretch: open brief with a real client (a family member or school leader)
- **Materials:** project planners, calculators, squared paper, spreadsheet
- **Joy:** A real project for a real audience

### Lesson 166 — Graduation maths project: build
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can carry out the calculations and representations for my project accurately and check them." (IB MYP Criteria A, D)
- **Hook:** "Engineers check everything twice. Build it, then check it."
- **Key activity:** Work on the project: calculations, scale drawings, charts; peer check against success criteria; teacher conferences.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show one calculation in your project and prove it's right with a different method or an estimate." → A calculation verified by estimation or an inverse operation.
- **Differentiation:** support: checklist and worked examples / stretch: add a sensitivity check ("What if costs rise by 10%?")
- **Materials:** project materials, calculators, spreadsheet

### Lesson 167 — Graduation maths project: present
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present my project clearly, justify my decisions with maths, and answer questions." (IB MYP Criterion C)
- **Hook:** "Pitch it": convince your audience your plan works.
- **Key activity:** 5-minute presentations with visuals; audience asks one "why" question each; self and peer evaluation against criteria.
- **Check for understanding:** "What assumption did your project make, and how would the result change if it were wrong?" → A specific assumption and its effect (e.g. "I assumed 40 guests; with 50 the cost rises by 25%").

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Integers:** calculates fluently with negative numbers in all four operations and explains a rule (e.g. why subtracting a negative adds) with counters or a pattern. *See it:* 4 − (−7) = 11 with a zero-pair explanation.
2. **Fractions:** divides fractions by fractions and explains with a diagram or story. *See it:* "How many 3/4 cup servings in 2¼ cups?" → 3, shown with a bar model.
3. **Percentages:** uses multipliers for increase/decrease and finds the original amount. *See it:* original price when 180 is after 25% off (240).
4. **Algebraic expressions:** writes, simplifies, expands and factorises expressions; finds the nth term of a linear sequence. *See it:* nth term of 5, 9, 13 is 4n + 1; 6x + 15 = 3(2x + 5).
5. **Equations and inequalities:** forms and solves linear equations (including brackets and unknowns on both sides) and simple inequalities. *See it:* 3(x − 2) = x + 8 → x = 7.
6. **Ratio and proportion:** simplifies and shares in ratios, uses unit rates and recognises direct proportion. *See it:* best-buy comparison and a scale-map calculation.
7. **Geometry:** finds missing angles with reasons (including parallel lines and polygons) and constructs triangles and bisectors. *See it:* a written chain of reasons; an accurate construction.
8. **Area and volume:** finds areas of triangles, parallelograms, trapezia and circles, volumes of prisms, and surface areas from nets. *See it:* surface area of a cuboid from its net.
9. **Probability:** calculates theoretical probabilities from sample spaces and estimates from experiments. *See it:* P(sum of 8 on two dice) = 5/36.
10. **Statistics:** carries out an investigation, summarises data with mean/median/mode and range/IQR, draws suitable charts and critiques misleading ones. *See it:* the Lesson 156–157 investigation report.

## Teacher guidance

- **Common misconceptions to watch for:** "subtracting always makes smaller" and "two negatives make a positive" over-applied to addition (−3 + −4 ≠ 7); "multiplying always makes bigger" and "dividing always makes smaller" (break with fractions less than 1); adding numerators and denominators; "20% up then 20% down returns to the start"; treating 2x² as (2x)²; 3(x + 2) = 3x + 2; confusing part : part with part : whole in ratio; using slant height instead of perpendicular height; mixing radius and diameter; treating histograms like bar charts; finding the median without ordering.
- **Concrete first, always:** double-sided counters (integers), algebra tiles (expressions and equations), a pan balance (equations), fraction strips and measuring cups (fraction division), multilink cubes (ratio, mean, volume). Move to pictorial (bar models, number lines, area models) and then abstract only when a child can explain the concrete model.
- **Fluency:** a daily 5-minute starter keeps squares to 15², primes to 50, fraction–decimal–percentage equivalents, and times tables automatic.
- **Reasoning language:** insist on "because…" and complete sentences in checks; use "always, sometimes, never" statements often.
- **International framing:** money is written without currency symbols unless the context names a country; use metric units throughout (with imperial only in explicit comparison lessons); draw contexts from many countries (train speeds in Japan, taxi fares in Kenya, temperatures in Mongolia).
- **Differentiation:** the "support" route keeps the same objective with more concrete scaffolding; the "stretch" route goes deeper (generalising, proving), not faster onto next year's content.
- **Vertical alignment:** Year 5 introduced ratio, formulae, simple equations, triangle/parallelogram area, cuboid volume and the mean; Year 6 extends every one (integers in every strand, fraction division, multipliers, nth term, equations with fractions and unknowns on both sides, ratio-change problems and inverse proportion, bearings, constructions, circles and prisms, probability, IQR/MAD and box plots) and deliberately opens each Year 5 topic with a short retrieval lesson rather than re-teaching it. Secondary maths (UK Year 8 / US Grade 7 / MYP 2) builds straight on: proportional relationships and percentage change, multi-step equations, the geometry of circles and prisms, and probability of compound events.
- **Wellbeing:** assessments are snapshots for teaching, never rankings; the personal-best graph measures growth against oneself.
