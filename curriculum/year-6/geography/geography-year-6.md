# Year 6 Geography (ages 11–12) — Scope & Sequence

> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Built to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Aligned to the **UK National Curriculum
> Key Stage 3 Geography** (locational knowledge, place knowledge, human and physical geography,
> geographical skills and fieldwork), the **US National Geography Standards** and C3 Framework
> (D2.Geo, grades 6–8), **IB MYP Year 1 Individuals & Societies** and the **UN Sustainable
> Development Goals** as a framing for global issues.

## Year overview

Year 6 Geography turns children into **global thinkers**. The year is organised around the big
questions of our time — hazards, climate change, population and cities, inequality and development —
studied through real places on every continent and through the geographer's own tools: maps at every
scale, satellite images, GIS layers, graphs, statistics and fieldwork. Each unit moves from **physical
processes** to **human impacts and responses**, and each asks children to weigh evidence and reach a
judgement about a real issue.

**What we assume from Year 5 (ages 10–11):** map mastery (projections, compass bearings, thematic
maps, contours, scale, a first use of GIS); a fair regional comparison (Kenya and the home region);
water as a resource (the Nile, water scarcity, local supply); trade and supply chains (chocolate,
T-shirts, fair trade); the polar regions and ice; and an environmental-quality fieldwork enquiry. Year 5
science introduced the atmosphere, the carbon cycle and climate change. From Year 4: latitude and
longitude, time zones, grid references, tectonics and hazards, rivers, the Amazon and Andes, and
settlements.

**What Year 6 adds:** precise location (degrees, minutes, GPS), projections and point of view, the
International Date Line, cross-sections and GIS analysis; plate tectonics with contrasting case studies; climate graphs, global circulation and responses to climate change;
population structure, migration and urbanisation; measuring development (GNI, HDI) and globalisation;
a regional study of China and India; and an independent fieldwork enquiry. **What secondary relies
on:** confident map and data skills, the ability to explain physical processes with diagrams, and
case-study-based extended answers.

**Sustainability and the SDGs** run through every unit. One lesson a week, 40–45 minutes.

## Unit 1 — The geographer's toolkit

**Essential question:** How can maps, coordinates and data help us see the world accurately?

**Key vocabulary:** latitude, longitude, equator, prime meridian, hemisphere, degree, coordinates, projection, Mercator, equal-area, distortion, time zone, International Date Line, GIS, layer, satellite image, contour, cross-section, relief

### Lesson 1 — Precise location: degrees, minutes and GPS
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can give locations in degrees and minutes, estimate distances from degrees of latitude and explain how GPS finds a position." (UK KS3 Geography — geographical skills; US Nat. Geog. Std. 1)
- **Hook:** "A ship in distress radios its position: 13° S, 72° W. Where is it — and who can reach it first?" (In the Andes near Cusco, Peru — so it can't be a ship. Spot the problem!)
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of Year 4's latitude and longitude in whole degrees. New: each degree divides into 60 minutes, so positions can be pinned to within about 2 km; 1° of latitude is about 111 km (40,000 km ÷ 360), but a degree of longitude shrinks towards the poles; read degree-and-minute positions for cities (Nairobi 1° 17′ S, 36° 49′ E) from an atlas gazetteer; compare with the decimal degrees a phone's map app shows; how GPS satellites let a receiver work out its position.
- **Check for understanding:** "Two towns are both at 30° E, one at 10° N and one at 12° N. About how far apart are they, and why can't you use the same rule for two towns 2° of longitude apart near the Arctic?" → About 222 km (2 × 111 km); lines of longitude get closer together towards the poles, so a degree of longitude there is much shorter.
- **Differentiation:** support: whole-degree and half-degree positions on a gridded map with a 111 km ruler / stretch: convert between degrees-and-minutes and decimal degrees (e.g. 1° 30′ = 1.5°) and locate the school to the nearest minute
- **Materials:** globe, atlas with gazetteer, world map with graticule, phones or tablets with a map app (optional), calculators
- **Joy:** "Pin the school" — who can give our classroom's position most precisely?

### Lesson 2 — Maps and power: choosing a projection and a point of view
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate map projections and orientations for different purposes and explain how map choices can shape how people see the world." (UK KS3 Geography — skills; US Nat. Geog. Std. 1)
- **Hook:** "Who decided that north goes at the top of the map — and that Europe goes in the middle?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 5's Mercator vs equal-area comparison in two minutes. New: compare a Pacific-centred map, a south-up map (such as McArthur's 1979 'Universal Corrective Map') and the Equal Earth projection (2018); the 1970s–80s debate about the Peters projection and why organisations switched maps; choose the best projection for four jobs (sailing a route, comparing country sizes, showing the Pacific, a polar flight path) and justify.
- **Check for understanding:** "A charity wants a world map to show which countries are largest in area. Which projection should it choose, why, and what would that map get wrong?" → An equal-area projection (e.g. Equal Earth), because it shows sizes correctly; it will distort shapes, especially near the edges and poles.
- **Differentiation:** support: a four-job matching sheet with projection pictures / stretch: argue whether any world map can be 'neutral'
- **Materials:** Mercator, equal-area, Pacific-centred and south-up world maps; globe; devices with "The True Size Of" (thetruesize.com)
- **Joy:** Redraw your home region on a south-up map — does it look different to you?

### Lesson 3 — Time zones and the International Date Line
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate time differences across the International Date Line and with half-hour offsets, and explain why time-zone borders are political choices." (UK KS3 Geography — skills; Maths link — negative numbers; builds on Year 4 time zones)
- **Hook:** "In 2011, Samoa skipped 30 December entirely. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Earth turns 360° in 24 hours = 15° per hour; use a time-zone map to calculate times in world cities (link to Maths Lesson 22); explain the Date Line and the Samoa example (moving closer in time to trading partners Australia and New Zealand).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is each standard time zone roughly 15° of longitude wide?" → Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours, and 360 ÷ 24 = 15° per hour.
- **Differentiation:** support: a time-zone number line / stretch: explain why some countries (e.g. India, UTC+5:30) use half-hour offsets, and why China uses a single time zone
- **Materials:** time-zone world map, clocks set to different cities, globe and torch
- **Joy:** "World clock" wall showing live times for family and friends abroad

### Lesson 4 — GIS: layers of the world
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a free online GIS or digital map to overlay data layers and describe a pattern." (UK KS3 Geography — GIS; US Nat. Geog. Std. 1; Computing link)
- **Hook:** "In 1854, John Snow mapped cholera deaths in London and found the source: one water pump. That was an early GIS."
- **Key activity:** Recreate a simplified John Snow map (plot cases on tracing paper over a street map); then use a digital map (e.g. Google Earth, OpenStreetMap or ArcGIS StoryMaps) to layer data (population density, rivers, earthquakes); describe patterns using compass directions and place names.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is overlaying layers of data more powerful than looking at each map separately?" → You can see relationships between things (e.g. cases clustering around one pump; settlements along rivers).
- **Differentiation:** support: a guided worksheet with the layers pre-selected / stretch: suggest a question the class could answer with a local GIS map
- **Materials:** tracing paper, Snow's cholera map (simplified), devices with internet access
- **Joy:** "Be John Snow" — solve the cholera mystery

### Lesson 5 — Relief, contours and cross-sections
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read relief from contour lines and draw a cross-section between two points on a topographic map." (UK KS3 Geography — skills; builds on Year 5 contours)
- **Hook:** "From a flat map, draw the shape of the land as if you had sliced it with a knife."
- **Key activity:** Two-minute retrieval of Year 5's contour model (no re-build). New: on a topographic map, place a paper strip along a line, mark contour heights, transfer to a graph with a sensible vertical scale and join to draw a cross-section; identify steep and gentle slopes, V-shaped valleys, ridges and spurs from the contour patterns; unit check.
- **Check for understanding:** "What do closely spaced contour lines tell you, and why?" → The slope is steep, because the height changes a lot over a short horizontal distance.
- **Differentiation:** support: a simple map with few contours and a pre-drawn axis / stretch: draw a cross-section across a valley and label the river and the valley shape
- **Materials:** topographic map extracts, graph paper, strips of paper, rulers
- **Joy:** "Mystery landscape" — draw a cross-section, then match it to a photograph of the real place

**End-of-unit check:** Give the coordinates of three cities; explain one distortion of the Mercator projection; calculate the time in Tokyo (UTC+9) when it is 10:00 in Nairobi (UTC+3) (16:00); draw a cross-section from a contour map.

## Unit 2 — Restless Earth: tectonic hazards

**Essential question:** Why do earthquakes and volcanoes happen where they do, and why do they affect some places far more than others?

**Key vocabulary:** crust, mantle, core, tectonic plate, convection, plate boundary, constructive, destructive, conservative, subduction, magma, lava, earthquake, focus, epicentre, magnitude, seismometer, tsunami, hazard, risk, vulnerability, prediction, preparation, mitigation

### Lesson 6 — Plate tectonics: the jigsaw Earth
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the theory of plate tectonics and the evidence for it." (UK KS3 Geography — physical geography; US Nat. Geog. Std. 7)
- **Hook:** "Look at South America and Africa. Do they fit? Alfred Wegener thought so in 1912 — and was laughed at."
- **Key activity:** Cut out continents and fit them together; evidence (matching fossils such as *Mesosaurus*, matching rocks, mid-ocean ridges); convection currents modelled with food colouring in warm water (adult); plot world earthquakes and volcanoes to reveal plate boundaries.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why was Wegener's idea rejected at first, and what evidence later convinced scientists?" → He couldn't explain what moved the continents; later, mapping of the ocean floor showed spreading ridges and convection in the mantle.
- **Differentiation:** support: continent cut-outs with fossil symbols / stretch: explain why the Atlantic is widening by a few centimetres a year
- **Materials:** continent cut-outs, world map of plate boundaries, earthquake and volcano data, clear container, warm water, food colouring
- **Joy:** "Pangaea puzzle"

### Lesson 7 — Plate boundaries
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the three main types of plate boundary and the landforms and hazards at each." (UK KS3 Geography; US Nat. Geog. Std. 7)
- **Hook:** "Iceland is being pulled apart. The Himalaya are still rising. California's San Andreas Fault slides sideways. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Model boundaries with foam or biscuits (constructive: pulling apart; destructive: one plate sinking under another — subduction — or collision; conservative: sliding past); match to real examples (Mid-Atlantic Ridge and Iceland, the Andes and Japan, the Himalaya, the San Andreas Fault).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are there volcanoes at destructive boundaries but not usually at conservative ones?" → At destructive boundaries, the sinking plate melts, creating magma that rises; at conservative boundaries, plates slide past without making magma.
- **Differentiation:** support: labelled boundary diagrams to match / stretch: explain why the Himalaya have earthquakes but few volcanoes (collision of two continental plates, little subduction)
- **Materials:** foam sheets or biscuits, boundary diagram cards, world plate map
- **Joy:** Biscuit boundaries (edible geography)

### Lesson 8 — Earthquakes: contrasting case studies
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare the causes, effects and responses of the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake (Japan) and the 2015 Gorkha earthquake (Nepal) and explain why the impacts differed." (UK KS3 Geography — human and physical interaction; US Nat. Geog. Std. 15)
- **Hook:** "Japan's 2011 earthquake was far more powerful than Nepal's in 2015. So why was building damage so much worse, relative to its strength, in Nepal?"
- **Key activity:** Case study cards: Tōhoku (magnitude 9.0–9.1; most of the roughly 18,000 deaths caused by the tsunami; the Fukushima nuclear accident; strong building codes and warning systems) and Gorkha (magnitude 7.8; about 9,000 deaths; many buildings of unreinforced brick and stone; remote mountain villages hard to reach); sort effects into primary/secondary, social/economic/environmental; explain the role of wealth, preparation and geography.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can a weaker earthquake cause more building collapse in one place than a stronger one elsewhere?" → Vulnerability matters: building quality, preparation, warning systems and access for rescue can differ greatly; wealthier countries can afford more protection.
- **Differentiation:** support: a comparison grid with key facts provided / stretch: explain why the tsunami, not the shaking, caused most deaths in Japan
- **Materials:** case-study cards, maps of Japan and Nepal, a comparison grid
- **Joy:** "Newsroom" — present a two-minute broadcast on one earthquake

### Lesson 9 — Volcanoes: hazard and resource
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe volcano types and explain why millions of people live near active volcanoes." (UK KS3 Geography; US Nat. Geog. Std. 15)
- **Hook:** "In 2010, an Icelandic volcano grounded flights across Europe for days. Yet Icelanders heat their homes with volcanic energy."
- **Key activity:** Compare shield volcanoes (Mauna Loa, Hawaii) and composite volcanoes (Mount Fuji, Merapi); benefits (fertile soils in Java, geothermal power in Iceland and Kenya, tourism, minerals) vs hazards (lava, ash, pyroclastic flows, lahars); build a baking-soda volcano to discuss viscosity.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give two reasons why people live near Mount Merapi in Indonesia despite the danger." → Volcanic soil is very fertile for farming; families have lived there for generations and may lack the money or land to move; they may have warning systems.
- **Differentiation:** support: benefit/hazard sorting cards / stretch: explain the link between magma viscosity and explosive eruptions
- **Materials:** images of volcano types, sorting cards, baking soda, vinegar, washing-up liquid, modelling clay
- **Joy:** Build and erupt a model volcano

### Lesson 10 — Living with hazards: predict, protect, prepare
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate ways of reducing the risk from tectonic hazards (monitoring, earthquake-resistant buildings, education and drills)." (UK KS3 Geography; US Nat. Geog. Std. 15; NGSS MS-ESS3-2)
- **Hook:** "Earthquakes can't yet be predicted. So how do Japan and Chile save so many lives?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4's earthquake-resistant building design in two minutes (no re-build). New: evaluate a range of strategies with data on cost and lives saved — Japan's earthquake early-warning alerts (seconds before strong shaking), Chile's strict building codes, retrofitting schools in Nepal, tsunami evacuation routes, hazard maps and land-use planning, community drills; rank them for a wealthy country and a lower-income country and explain why the rankings differ.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is preparation (drills and building codes) often more effective than trying to predict earthquakes?" → Scientists can't predict exactly when earthquakes will happen, but buildings and people can be made ready for whenever one does.
- **Differentiation:** support: a guided build with a design template / stretch: evaluate which strategies are affordable for poorer countries (e.g. low-cost bracing, education)
- **Materials:** strategy cards with cost and impact data, world hazard map, ranking grids
- **Joy:** "Ten seconds' warning" — plan what your family would do in the seconds after an early-warning alert

### Lesson 11 — Unit 2 enquiry: why do people live in hazardous places?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write an extended answer, using case studies, to explain why people live in hazardous places and how risk can be reduced." (UK KS3 Geography — extended writing; C3 D2.Geo.5.6-8)
- **Hook:** "About one in ten people in the world live within 100 km of a historically active volcano. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Plan and write an answer using the unit's case studies with a judgement (e.g. "Most people live in hazardous places because the benefits outweigh the risks — how far do you agree?").
- **Check for understanding:** "What makes your answer 'geographical' rather than just a story?" → It uses specific places, data and processes (plate boundaries, magnitude, vulnerability) to explain.
- **Differentiation:** support: a writing frame with case-study facts / stretch: consider how climate change or population growth may change hazard risk
- **Materials:** case-study cards, writing frames
- **Joy:** "Hazard-safe city" design sketch

**End-of-unit check:** Label a map of plate boundaries with examples of each type; explain one boundary with a diagram; compare the impacts of the Japan and Nepal earthquakes and explain the difference; evaluate one way of reducing risk.

## Unit 3 — Weather, climate & climate change

**Essential question:** What controls the climate of a place, and how should the world respond to climate change?

**Key vocabulary:** weather, climate, climate graph, precipitation, temperature range, climate zone, latitude, altitude, continentality, global atmospheric circulation, greenhouse effect, fossil fuels, sea-level rise, extreme weather, mitigation, adaptation, Paris Agreement, carbon footprint

### Lesson 12 — Reading and drawing climate graphs
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw and interpret climate graphs (temperature line and rainfall bars) and compare places." (UK KS3 Geography — skills; US Nat. Geog. Std. 7; Maths link)
- **Hook:** "Two cities, two graphs: one has a monsoon, one has almost no rain. Which is Mumbai and which is Cairo?"
- **Key activity:** Draw a climate graph from data for one city; interpret graphs for Mumbai, Cairo, Moscow and Singapore (seasonality, range, wettest month); match to photos.
- **Check for understanding:** "Singapore's temperature line is almost flat all year. What does that tell you about its location?" → It is near the equator, where the Sun is high all year, so there's little seasonal change.
- **Differentiation:** support: axes pre-drawn and data rounded / stretch: calculate annual temperature range and total rainfall and explain the differences
- **Materials:** climate data tables, graph paper, city photographs, atlas
- **Joy:** "Guess the city" climate graph quiz

### Lesson 13 — What controls climate? Latitude, altitude, oceans and circulation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how latitude, altitude, distance from the sea and global atmospheric circulation shape world climate zones." (UK KS3 Geography — weather and climate; US Nat. Geog. Std. 7)
- **Hook:** "Why are the world's great deserts (Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari, Australian) found around 30° north and south?"
- **Key activity:** Torch-on-globe model (same light spreads over more area near the poles); temperature falls with altitude (Quito vs Guayaquil); maritime vs continental climates; simplified global circulation (rising air at the equator = rain; sinking air near 30° = deserts); map climate zones.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is Quito, almost on the equator, much cooler than the coastal city of Guayaquil?" → Quito is at about 2,850 m altitude; temperature falls as altitude increases.
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled circulation diagram to complete / stretch: explain the monsoon as a seasonal shift of winds and rain
- **Materials:** globe, torch, climate zone map, circulation diagram cards
- **Joy:** "Desert belt" map colouring — the pattern pops out

### Lesson 14 — Evidence for climate change
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use evidence (temperature records, ice cores, CO₂ measurements, glaciers) to explain how and why the climate is changing." (UK KS3 Geography; NGSS MS-ESS3-5; Science link)
- **Hook:** "Kyoto in Japan has records of when its cherry trees blossomed going back more than 1,000 years. What could those records tell a geographer?"
- **Key activity:** The Keeling curve is analysed in Science Lesson 65 and Year 5 science covered the global temperature graph, so here pupils use geographical evidence: ice cores (Earth's past air in Antarctic ice); satellite images of a retreating glacier compared year by year (GIS link); tide-gauge sea-level records; and long historical records such as Kyoto's cherry-blossom dates. Distinguish natural and human causes and judge how strong each line of evidence is.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it convincing when very different kinds of evidence — ice cores, glaciers, sea level and blossom dates — all point the same way?" → Each is measured independently in a different way and place, so it is very unlikely they would all show warming by chance or by the same mistake.
- **Differentiation:** support: evidence cards with guided questions / stretch: evaluate one weakness of historical records such as blossom diaries and how scientists deal with it
- **Materials:** ice-core graph, satellite images of a glacier over time (e.g. NASA Earth Observatory), sea-level graph, Kyoto cherry-blossom data (simplified), evidence-strength grid
- **Joy:** "Ice core" model — layers of coloured ice in a cup to "read"

### Lesson 15 — Impacts of climate change around the world
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how climate change affects people and places differently, using examples from three continents." (UK KS3 Geography; US Nat. Geog. Std. 14)
- **Hook:** "The highest point of Tuvalu, a Pacific island nation, is only about 4.6 metres above sea level."
- **Key activity:** Case-study carousel: Bangladesh (low-lying delta, flooding, cyclones); Tuvalu (sea-level rise, saltwater intrusion); the Sahel (drought and farming); the Arctic (melting sea ice, Inuit communities); a European heatwave; sort impacts by social, economic and environmental.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are countries that produced the least greenhouse gases often the most affected?" → Many are low-lying, hot or dependent on farming, and have less money for protection — an issue of climate justice.
- **Differentiation:** support: case-study cards with key facts highlighted / stretch: evaluate which impact is most serious and why
- **Materials:** case-study cards, world map, photographs
- **Joy:** "Postcards from the front line" of climate change

### Lesson 16 — Responding to climate change: mitigation and adaptation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can distinguish mitigation from adaptation and evaluate responses at global, national, local and personal scales." (UK KS3 Geography; US Nat. Geog. Std. 14)
- **Hook:** "The Netherlands has lived below sea level for centuries. What can the world learn from it?"
- **Key activity:** Sort responses (renewable energy, reforestation, sea walls, floating houses, drought-resistant crops, cycling); learn the Paris Agreement (2015: limit warming to well below 2 °C, pursuing 1.5 °C); calculate a simple personal or family carbon footprint and plan changes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is building a sea wall mitigation or adaptation? Explain." → Adaptation: it helps people cope with the effects (rising seas) but does not reduce the cause (emissions).
- **Differentiation:** support: a sorting grid with definitions / stretch: evaluate which scale of response (global, national, individual) is most effective, with reasons
- **Materials:** response cards, carbon footprint calculator (e.g. a child-friendly online tool), Paris Agreement summary
- **Joy:** "Family climate pledge" (link to Life Skills)

### Lesson 17 — Unit 3 enquiry: who should do most to tackle climate change?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use data (emissions per country and per person) to argue who should do most to tackle climate change." (UK KS3 Geography — extended writing; C3 D2.Geo.12.6-8)
- **Hook:** "One country emits the most carbon dioxide in total; another emits the most per person. Which is 'most responsible'?"
- **Key activity:** Compare total, per-person and historical (cumulative) emissions for several countries (e.g. China, the USA, India, Germany, Nigeria, Qatar) using a reliable dataset such as Our World in Data; hold a mini climate summit; write an argued answer.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might total emissions and emissions per person give different answers about responsibility?" → A country with a huge population can have large total emissions but low emissions per person; a small rich country may emit little in total but a lot per person.
- **Differentiation:** support: a data table with key comparisons highlighted / stretch: include historical emissions and argue about fairness
- **Materials:** emissions data (Our World in Data), role cards for a mini summit, writing frames
- **Joy:** Mini "COP" climate summit role play

**End-of-unit check:** Draw and interpret a climate graph; explain why deserts form near 30°; describe two pieces of evidence for climate change; classify responses as mitigation or adaptation; argue a position using emissions data.

## Unit 4 — Population, migration & cities

**Essential question:** Where do the world's eight billion people live, why do they move, and how can cities be good places to live?

**Key vocabulary:** population, population density, distribution, birth rate, death rate, natural increase, population pyramid, ageing population, migration, push factor, pull factor, refugee, asylum seeker, urbanisation, megacity, informal settlement, sustainable city

### Lesson 18 — Eight billion people: growth and distribution
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe world population growth and explain why people are unevenly distributed." (UK KS3 Geography — population; US Nat. Geog. Std. 9)
- **Hook:** "The world reached 8 billion people in November 2022. It took until about 1804 to reach the first billion."
- **Key activity:** Plot a world population graph (1 billion ~1804 to 8 billion 2022); map population density (a night-lights satellite image); explain dense and sparse areas (climate, relief, water, jobs); calculate population density for two countries (people ÷ km²).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the Nile valley in Egypt densely populated while most of Egypt is almost empty?" → The river provides water and fertile land in a desert country, so people live along it.
- **Differentiation:** support: a partially completed density map / stretch: explain why world population growth is slowing
- **Materials:** population data, night-lights satellite image, world population density map, calculators
- **Joy:** "If the world were 100 people" activity (by continent)

### Lesson 19 — Population pyramids
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read population pyramids and explain what they show about a country's past and future." (UK KS3 Geography — population; Maths link — statistics)
- **Hook:** "Two pyramids: one wide at the bottom, one narrow at the bottom and wide at the top. Which is Japan and which is Niger?"
- **Key activity:** Read pyramids for Niger, India, the UK and Japan; describe shapes; link to birth and death rates; discuss challenges of youthful populations (schools, jobs) and ageing populations (care, pensions, workers).
- **Check for understanding:** "What problems might Japan's population pyramid suggest for its future?" → Fewer young people to work and pay taxes, and many older people needing care and pensions.
- **Differentiation:** support: pyramid-reading questions with guided steps / stretch: predict how India's pyramid might change by 2050 and why
- **Materials:** population pyramids (e.g. from populationpyramid.net or the UN), comparison sheets
- **Joy:** Build a class "pyramid" with sticky notes by age group of family members

### Lesson 20 — Migration: why people move
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain push and pull factors, distinguish voluntary from forced migration, and describe the experience of refugees." (UK KS3 Geography — population and migration; US Nat. Geog. Std. 9)
- **Hook:** "Almost every family has a migration story. What is yours?"
- **Key activity:** Map family migration stories (with consent, voluntary sharing only); sort push and pull factors; define refugee (UNHCR) and asylum seeker; case study of refugees from South Sudan (link to *A Long Walk to Water*) and Syria; discuss how migrants contribute to countries.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between a migrant and a refugee?" → A migrant chooses to move (often for work or family); a refugee is forced to flee their country because of war, persecution or disaster.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards for push and pull factors / stretch: explain the positive and negative effects of migration for both origin and destination countries
- **Materials:** world map, string and pins for migration stories, UNHCR facts, push/pull cards
- **Joy:** "Our class's journeys" map

### Lesson 21 — Urbanisation and megacities
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why cities are growing, especially in Asia and Africa, and describe what makes a megacity." (UK KS3 Geography — urbanisation; US Nat. Geog. Std. 12)
- **Hook:** "More than half of all people now live in cities. In 1950, less than a third did."
- **Key activity:** Map the world's largest cities (Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai, São Paulo, Cairo, Lagos, Dhaka); causes of urbanisation (rural–urban migration, natural increase); opportunities and challenges; satellite images of Lagos growing over time.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one reason why cities in Africa and Asia are growing fastest." → Many people move from the countryside for jobs, education and healthcare, and cities have young populations with high natural increase.
- **Differentiation:** support: a megacity map with labels / stretch: compare growth rates of a megacity in a high-income country and one in a lower-income country
- **Materials:** world city map, satellite images over time (e.g. Google Earth timelapse), data cards
- **Joy:** Google Earth "timelapse" of a growing city

### Lesson 22 — Life in an informal settlement: Dharavi, Mumbai
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the challenges and opportunities of life in an informal settlement, avoiding stereotypes." (UK KS3 Geography; US Nat. Geog. Std. 12)
- **Hook:** "Dharavi, in the middle of Mumbai, is home to perhaps a million people in about 2 square kilometres — and thousands of small businesses."
- **Key activity:** Examine photographs, maps and short accounts; list challenges (sanitation, space, flooding) and strengths (recycling industry, pottery, leather, strong communities); discuss redevelopment plans and whose voices should decide.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it misleading to describe Dharavi only as a place of poverty?" → It is also a place of enterprise and community with a large informal economy (e.g. recycling and manufacturing); a single story ignores residents' skills and agency.
- **Differentiation:** support: photograph analysis with a guided frame / stretch: evaluate a redevelopment plan from residents' point of view
- **Materials:** photographs, maps, short accounts, a "single story" discussion prompt
- **Joy:** "Recycling entrepreneurs" challenge — make something valuable from waste

### Lesson 23 — Sustainable cities and Unit 4 check
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate strategies that make cities more sustainable, using examples such as Curitiba, Singapore and Copenhagen." (UK KS3 Geography; SDG 11)
- **Hook:** "In the 1970s, the Brazilian city of Curitiba created a fast, cheap bus system that cities around the world later copied."
- **Key activity:** Study Curitiba (bus rapid transit, parks, recycling), Singapore (public housing, green buildings, water recycling) and Copenhagen (cycling); design a sustainable neighbourhood plan for your own town; short unit check.
- **Check for understanding:** "What makes a city 'sustainable'?" → It meets people's needs now (housing, transport, jobs) without harming the environment or the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
- **Differentiation:** support: a design template with categories / stretch: justify your design with data and a cost estimate
- **Materials:** case-study cards, large paper for neighbourhood plans, coloured pens
- **Joy:** Design your dream sustainable neighbourhood

**End-of-unit check:** Describe world population distribution with two reasons; interpret two population pyramids; explain push/pull factors and the term refugee; describe one megacity's challenges; evaluate one sustainable city strategy.

## Unit 5 — Development & a globalised world

**Essential question:** Why are some countries richer than others, and how are we all connected by what we buy?

**Key vocabulary:** development, GNI per capita, Human Development Index (HDI), life expectancy, literacy, inequality, globalisation, supply chain, multinational company, fair trade, aid, debt, resource, water security, Sustainable Development Goals

### Lesson 24 — Measuring development
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain and compare measures of development (GNI per capita, life expectancy, literacy, HDI)." (UK KS3 Geography — development; US Nat. Geog. Std. 11)
- **Hook:** "Is a country with more money always a better place to live?"
- **Key activity:** Compare indicators for several countries (e.g. Norway, Costa Rica, India, Nigeria, Qatar) using UN data; learn the HDI (health, education, income); discuss the weaknesses of single measures; Gapminder "Dollar Street" to see real homes at different incomes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is HDI a better measure of development than GNI per capita alone?" → It includes health and education as well as income, so it shows more of how people's lives are.
- **Differentiation:** support: a simplified data table / stretch: explain why Costa Rica has a high life expectancy despite a lower income than some richer countries
- **Materials:** development data tables, Dollar Street (gapminder.org/dollar-street), calculators
- **Joy:** Dollar Street exploration — compare toothbrushes, stoves and beds around the world

### Lesson 25 — Why is development uneven?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain physical, historical and economic reasons for uneven development." (UK KS3 Geography; US Nat. Geog. Std. 11; History link)
- **Hook:** "Why might a country rich in minerals remain poor?"
- **Key activity:** Sort causes (landlocked location, climate hazards, disease, colonial history and borders, trade in raw materials, conflict, corruption, debt, education); link to History Unit 5 (independence); rank and justify.
- **Check for understanding:** "How can exporting only raw materials keep a country poorer?" → Raw materials sell for less than finished products, and prices can fall suddenly; more value is added (and more jobs created) where things are manufactured.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cause cards / stretch: explain how causes interact (e.g. conflict reduces education, which reduces income)
- **Materials:** cause cards, a world map of HDI, diamond-ranking templates
- **Joy:** "The trading game" — groups with unequal resources compete to make paper products

### Lesson 26 — Globalisation: the journey of a smartphone
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can trace the global supply chain of a smartphone and explain the benefits and costs of globalisation." (UK KS3 Geography — economic activity; US Nat. Geog. Std. 11)
- **Hook:** "Your phone may contain materials from dozens of countries. Can we trace where they come from?"
- **Key activity:** Map the smartphone's journey (minerals such as cobalt from the Democratic Republic of the Congo and lithium from Australia or Chile; design in one country; components from several; assembly in another; sale worldwide; e-waste); discuss benefits (jobs, cheaper goods) and costs (working conditions, environment).
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one benefit and one cost of globalisation for a country where phones are assembled." → Benefit: jobs and investment; cost: low wages or poor working conditions, or pollution.
- **Differentiation:** support: a supply-chain map with the stages given / stretch: evaluate how consumers and companies could make supply chains fairer
- **Materials:** world map, supply-chain cards, an old phone to examine (not opened)
- **Joy:** Unravel "where my things come from" by checking labels at home

### Lesson 27 — Fair trade, aid and making a difference
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate fair trade and different types of aid as ways to reduce inequality." (UK KS3 Geography; US Nat. Geog. Std. 11)
- **Hook:** "Who gets the money when you buy a bar of chocolate — the cocoa farmer, the factory or the shop?" (Build on Year 5's chocolate and fair-trade study.)
- **Key activity:** Two-minute retrieval of Year 5's fair-trade minimum price and premium. New: types of aid (emergency, long-term development, bottom-up, top-down, loans vs grants) and remittances (money sent home by migrants, which in many countries is larger than aid); case studies of a small-scale project (e.g. community solar lamps) and a large project; evaluate which helps most in the long term and who decides.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might a small, local project sometimes help more than a large one?" → It is designed with local people, uses affordable technology they can maintain, and meets their actual needs.
- **Differentiation:** support: case-study cards with guiding questions / stretch: argue for and against "trade, not aid"
- **Materials:** fair trade products and labels, aid case-study cards
- **Joy:** Design a fair trade product label

### Lesson 28 — Water security
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why water is scarce in some places and evaluate solutions from different countries." (UK KS3 Geography — resources; SDG 6; links to English Unit 5)
- **Hook:** "In 2018 Cape Town, a city of about four million people, counted down to 'Day Zero' — the day the taps would be turned off. Day Zero never came. Why not?"
- **Key activity:** Brief retrieval of Year 5's water unit (fresh water is scarce; physical vs economic scarcity; village-scale solutions). New: water security at city and national scale — the Cape Town drought (strict limits of 50 litres per person per day, pressure reduction, public data dashboards); large solutions with their trade-offs: desalination in Israel and the Gulf (energy and brine), Singapore's recycled 'NEWater', drip irrigation, and big dams; link to Maths Lesson 133 (rainwater calculations).
- **Check for understanding:** "Cape Town avoided Day Zero mainly by cutting demand rather than finding new water. Why might cutting demand be quicker and cheaper than building new supply?" → Changing how much people and businesses use can happen in weeks and costs little, while desalination plants or dams take years and cost a great deal of money and energy.
- **Differentiation:** support: a Cape Town timeline with guided questions / stretch: evaluate one large-scale solution (desalination or a dam) for costs, benefits and who pays
- **Materials:** Cape Town dam-level graph and news extracts (simplified), case-study cards, world water-stress map
- **Joy:** "50-litre day" — plan a day's water use within Cape Town's limit

### Lesson 29 — The Sustainable Development Goals and Unit 5 check
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the 17 UN Sustainable Development Goals (adopted in 2015 for 2030) and evaluate progress on one." (UK KS3 Geography; SDGs)
- **Hook:** "In 2015, every UN member state agreed on 17 goals to reach by 2030. Are we on track?"
- **Key activity:** Explore the 17 SDGs; groups research progress on one goal using the UN's SDG data; present "good news, bad news, what next"; short unit check.
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose one SDG and give one piece of evidence of progress and one challenge." → A specific goal, one data-based piece of progress and one challenge.
- **Differentiation:** support: SDG fact cards with key data / stretch: explain how two SDGs are connected (e.g. clean water and education)
- **Materials:** SDG icons and fact cards, devices with internet access
- **Joy:** "SDG champions" poster gallery (links to the Life Skills capstone)

**End-of-unit check:** Compare two countries' development indicators and explain why HDI is useful; explain two causes of uneven development; trace a supply chain and give one benefit and one cost of globalisation; evaluate one approach to water security.

## Unit 6 — Regional study: China and India

**Essential question:** How are the world's two most populous countries changing, and why does it matter to everyone?

**Key vocabulary:** Himalaya, plateau, delta, monsoon, Yangtze, Ganges, hydroelectric power, economic growth, rural, urban, middle class, manufacturing, services

### Lesson 30 — Physical geography of China and India
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the main physical features of China and India and explain how they affect where people live." (UK KS3 Geography — place knowledge of Asia; US Nat. Geog. Std. 4)
- **Hook:** "The same mountain range — the Himalaya — separates these two giants and feeds rivers used by hundreds of millions of people."
- **Key activity:** Atlas work: relief (the Himalaya, the Tibetan Plateau, the Deccan Plateau), rivers (the Yangtze, Yellow River, Ganges, Brahmaputra), climate (monsoon); overlay population density; link to plate tectonics (the Himalaya from the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do so many people in India and China live in river valleys and on coastal plains?" → Flat, fertile land and water for farming, with easier transport and trade.
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled base map to complete / stretch: explain how the monsoon affects farming and flooding
- **Materials:** atlases, blank maps of China and India, population density map
- **Joy:** "River journey" — trace a raindrop from the Himalaya to the sea

### Lesson 31 — Human geography: cities, economies and change
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how China's and India's economies and cities have changed since 1980 and the effects on people." (UK KS3 Geography — economic activity; US Nat. Geog. Std. 11)
- **Hook:** "In 2023, India overtook China as the world's most populous country (UN estimates). How are these countries changing?"
- **Key activity:** Data comparison (population, urban share, GDP per capita, main industries — manufacturing in China, IT and services in India); case studies of Shenzhen (a fishing area that became a megacity after 1980) and Bengaluru (technology hub); discuss changes for rural families.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did Shenzhen grow so fast after 1980?" → It was made a Special Economic Zone, attracting foreign companies, factories and millions of workers.
- **Differentiation:** support: data tables with guided comparisons / stretch: evaluate the environmental costs of rapid growth
- **Materials:** comparison data, photographs of Shenzhen then and now, maps
- **Joy:** "Then and now" photo comparisons

### Lesson 32 — Big projects, big debates: the Three Gorges Dam
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate the benefits and costs of a large-scale development project from different viewpoints." (UK KS3 Geography — human–physical interaction; C3 D2.Geo.12.6-8)
- **Hook:** "The Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze is the world's largest power station by capacity. Over a million people had to move to make way for it."
- **Key activity:** Role play from different viewpoints (a relocated farmer, an engineer, an environmentalist, a city factory owner downstream, a government planner); list costs and benefits; reach a judgement; compare with a debate over a dam or project in India.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might a relocated farmer and a factory owner disagree about the dam?" → The farmer lost home and land; the factory owner gains reliable electricity and flood protection — costs and benefits fall on different people.
- **Differentiation:** support: role cards with key points / stretch: write a balanced judgement weighing short- and long-term effects
- **Materials:** role cards, maps and images of the dam, fact sheets
- **Joy:** Town-hall debate role play

**End-of-unit check:** Label key physical features of China and India; explain one link between physical geography and population; compare two economic changes; evaluate the Three Gorges Dam from two viewpoints.

## Unit 7 — Fieldwork enquiry

**Essential question:** How can I investigate a real geographical question in my own area and reach a conclusion from data?

**Key vocabulary:** enquiry question, hypothesis, primary data, secondary data, sampling, environmental quality survey, land-use map, pedestrian count, questionnaire, bias, conclusion, evaluation

### Lesson 33 — Planning a fieldwork enquiry
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a fieldwork enquiry with a question, hypothesis, sampling method and data-collection sheets." (UK KS3 Geography — fieldwork; C3 D2.Geo.1.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Is our local area a good place to live — for everyone? Let's find out with data."
- **Key activity:** Choose a question adaptable to any town and different from Year 5's environmental-quality enquiry (e.g. "Is our main street thriving?", "How far do people travel to use our local park, and how could it be improved?", "Does land use change along a transect from the centre to the edge of town?"); write a hypothesis; design a land-use map key, pedestrian counts and a short questionnaire (reusing Year 5's environmental quality survey only as one supporting method); plan a route, a sampling strategy and a risk assessment.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why should you collect data at several sites, not just one?" → To compare places and make the results more reliable and representative.
- **Differentiation:** support: a ready-made survey sheet / stretch: design a systematic sampling plan (e.g. every 100 m along a transect)
- **Materials:** local maps, survey templates, clipboards, risk-assessment form
- **Joy:** Choosing a real local question

### Lesson 34 — Collecting data in the field
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can collect accurate primary data safely using surveys, counts, photographs and maps." (UK KS3 Geography — fieldwork; C3 D2.Geo.1.6-8)
- **Hook:** "Geographers are detectives outdoors."
- **Key activity:** Supervised fieldwork walk (with adults): environmental quality scores at each site, pedestrian or traffic counts for 5 minutes, land-use mapping, geo-tagged photographs, short questionnaires with residents (with an adult present and polite script).
- **Check for understanding:** "What could make your data inaccurate, and how did you reduce it?" → e.g. time of day or weather affecting counts; different people scoring differently — reduced by fixed times and agreed scoring criteria.
- **Differentiation:** support: work in a pair with clear roles / stretch: collect an extra variable (e.g. noise with a phone decibel app)
- **Materials:** clipboards, survey sheets, tally counters, phone or camera, maps, high-visibility vests, adult supervisors
- **Joy:** The fieldwork trip

### Lesson 35 — Presenting, concluding and evaluating the enquiry
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present fieldwork data with maps and graphs, write a conclusion and evaluate the enquiry." (UK KS3 Geography — fieldwork; IB MYP Criterion C)
- **Hook:** "Was your hypothesis right? What does the evidence say?"
- **Key activity:** Present data (bar charts of scores, annotated photos, a map with proportional symbols or a GIS layer); write a conclusion answering the question; evaluate reliability; suggest one realistic improvement to the area and share it (e.g. with a local council or school leader); display at the graduation exhibition.
- **Check for understanding:** "Does your data support your hypothesis? Use one piece of evidence." → A clear yes/no/partly with a specific data point.
- **Differentiation:** support: a presentation template / stretch: add a GIS map layer and a recommendation backed by data
- **Materials:** graph paper or spreadsheet, maps, display boards, devices
- **Joy:** Presenting recommendations to a real decision-maker

**End-of-unit check:** The fieldwork enquiry report or display, assessed on question and hypothesis, data collection, presentation (maps and graphs), conclusion from evidence and evaluation.

## Books & resources

- ***Oxford School Atlas* or *Collins Student Atlas*** — an up-to-date physical and political atlas with thematic maps; the geographer's essential book at this age.
- ***Factfulness* by Hans Rosling (with Ola Rosling and Anna Rosling Rönnlund)** — for the teacher and strong readers; the best antidote to outdated views of the world, and the basis for Dollar Street work.
- **Gapminder and Dollar Street (gapminder.org)** — free data tools and photographs of real homes on every income level worldwide.
- **Our World in Data (ourworldindata.org)** — reliable, clearly charted data on population, emissions, development and more.
- ***Maps* by Aleksandra Mizielińska and Daniel Mizieliński** — a beautifully illustrated atlas of countries around the world, great for browsing and place knowledge.
- ***If the World Were a Village* by David J. Smith** — shrinks the world's population to 100 people; a memorable way into population and inequality.
- ***The Boy Who Harnessed the Wind* (Young Readers Edition) by William Kamkwamba and Bryan Mealer** — development, energy and ingenuity in rural Malawi.
- **NASA Earth Observatory and Google Earth (including Timelapse)** — satellite imagery to see hazards, glaciers and cities change.
- **The True Size Of (thetruesize.com)** — an online tool to explore map-projection distortion.
- **UN SDG resources (un.org/sustainabledevelopment) and UNHCR education materials** — official, up-to-date information on global goals and refugees.
- **Fieldwork kit:** clipboards, tally counters, compass, measuring tape, local maps at two scales, high-visibility vests.

1. **Locates precisely:** uses latitude and longitude, time zones and grid references; explains projection distortion. *See it:* coordinates and time-zone tasks.
2. **Explains physical processes with diagrams:** plate boundaries, earthquakes and volcanoes, global circulation and climate. *See it:* annotated boundary and circulation diagrams.
3. **Uses case studies:** compares the Japan and Nepal earthquakes, climate-change impacts on three continents and a megacity. *See it:* extended answers with specific facts.
4. **Interprets geographical data:** climate graphs, population pyramids, development indicators and emissions data. *See it:* graph and data interpretation tasks.
5. **Understands global issues and connections:** migration, urbanisation, development, globalisation, water security, the SDGs. *See it:* the Unit 5 and 4 checks.
6. **Evaluates responses and projects from multiple viewpoints:** hazard reduction, climate responses, sustainable cities, the Three Gorges Dam. *See it:* the role-play judgement.
7. **Carries out fieldwork independently:** question, data collection, presentation, conclusion, evaluation. *See it:* the Unit 7 enquiry.

## Teacher guidance

- **Common misconceptions:** Africa is a country, or uniformly poor (use Dollar Street and data by country); the equator is hot everywhere (altitude matters); weather and climate are the same; climate change only means "warmer" (it means more extremes, rain changes and sea-level rise); earthquakes kill people (collapsing buildings and tsunamis do — vulnerability is human); informal settlements are only places of poverty; migrants and refugees are the same; the Mercator map shows true sizes.
- **Avoid the "single story".** Every place studied has strengths and agency as well as challenges. Balance problems with solutions, and include voices from the places themselves.
- **Local adaptation.** Units 1 and 7 must be taught with your own area's maps and a real local question; add local examples in every unit (your country's hazards, climate data, population pyramid and development indicators).
- **Sensitive topics.** Migration and refugees may be personal for some children: sharing family stories is always optional. Hazards and climate change can cause anxiety — always pair problems with action and hope (solutions, success stories, what children can do).
- **Differentiation.** Support uses labelled diagrams, guided graph questions and writing frames; stretch adds data analysis, interacting causes and extended judgements with counter-arguments.
- **Vertical alignment.** Year 4 taught latitude and longitude, time zones, tectonics and rivers; Year 5 secured map skills, a Kenya regional study, water, trade and the polar regions (and climate change in science). Year 6 retrieves these briefly and builds global processes, data, case studies and fieldwork enquiry, preparing for secondary geography (UK Year 8 / US Grade 7 / MYP 2), which assumes confident map, graph and case-study skills.
- **Cross-curricular links:** Maths (coordinates, negative numbers and time zones, statistics, rainfall volumes), Science (plate tectonics, greenhouse effect, carbon cycle), History (empire, independence and development), English (*A Long Walk to Water*, research and balanced argument), Computing (GIS, spreadsheets), Life Skills (sustainability, SDGs, the graduation capstone).
