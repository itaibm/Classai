# Year 6 Science (ages 10–11, KS2) — Lesson Outlines

> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Source of truth: [`knowledge-base/subjects/science/year-6.md`](../../../knowledge-base/subjects/science/year-6.md).

## Year overview

Year 6 is the year the primary spiral **comes together**. A child learns to **classify all living things** into broad groups (plants, animals, micro-organisms, fungi) and animals into vertebrates and invertebrates; how the **circulatory system** pumps blood to deliver oxygen and nutrients; the powerful ideas of **evolution and inheritance** (variation, adaptation, and the fossil evidence); how **light travels in straight lines** and how we actually see; and how to control a circuit's behaviour with **voltage** and read **standard circuit symbols**. Every topic deliberately revisits and lifts earlier years — classification keys from Y4, digestion and nutrition from Y3–4, fossils and reproduction from Y3–5, light and circuits from Y3–4 — so the child leaves primary with one connected picture of science. The **working-scientifically thread** reaches its full primary form: this year the child **plans and carries out controlled investigations**, **uses results to make predictions and set up further tests**, and **reports with precise scientific language and causal explanations** ("the heart beats faster *because* the muscles need oxygen delivered more quickly"). This is the culmination of the whole spine, from Year 1's careful looking to a young scientist who can frame a question, control it, and explain the cause. Lessons run 30–35 min, with investigation lessons at the upper band.

## Unit 1 — Classifying living things

**Essential question:** How do scientists classify every living thing into broad groups?

**Key vocabulary:** classify, broad group, plant, animal, micro-organism, fungus, bacteria, yeast, vertebrate, invertebrate, backbone, fish, amphibian, reptile, bird, mammal, shared characteristic, related.

### Lesson 1 — The broad groups of living things
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can classify living things into plants, animals, micro-organisms and fungi."
- **Hook:** "Yeast makes your bread rise — is it alive? And what kind of living thing is it?"
- **Key activity:** Explore — sort living things into the broad groups: **plants** (make their own food), **animals** (eat, move), **micro-organisms** (tiny — bacteria, yeast; need a microscope), **fungi** (mushrooms, mould). Notice — we classify by **shared characteristics**, which also shows how living things are **related**. Fix "all micro-organisms are germs" — many are helpful (yeast, yoghurt bacteria).
- **Check for understanding:** "Give an example of a useful micro-organism." → Yeast (bread) or bacteria (yoghurt).
- **Differentiation:** support: sort into plant/animal/other; stretch: explain how yeast can be alive yet invisible to the eye.
- **Materials:** living-thing cards (incl. yeast, mushroom, bacteria), group mats.
- **Joy:** the reveal that the bubbles in bread dough come from a living thing.

### Lesson 2 — Vertebrates and invertebrates
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can sort animals into vertebrates and invertebrates and name the five vertebrate groups."
- **Hook:** "Feel the bumps down your back — that backbone puts you in a special club. Which animals aren't in it?"
- **Key activity:** Explore — split **animals** into **vertebrates** (have a **backbone**: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals) and **invertebrates** (no backbone: insects, spiders, worms, snails). Sort minibeasts and animals. Fix "all small crawling animals are insects" — spiders have 8 legs and are invertebrates but not insects.
- **Check for understanding:** "Name the five vertebrate groups." → Fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals.
- **Differentiation:** support: sort vertebrate vs invertebrate; stretch: explain why a spider isn't an insect.
- **Materials:** animal/minibeast cards, vertebrate/invertebrate mats.
- **Joy:** sorting a pile of garden minibeasts into the two big groups.

**End-of-unit 1 check:** Classify living things into the broad groups (incl. a useful microbe); sort animals into vertebrate/invertebrate and name the five vertebrate groups.

## Unit 2 — Animals, including humans: circulation

**Essential question:** How does the body deliver oxygen and nutrients to every part?

**Key vocabulary:** circulatory system, heart, blood, blood vessel, artery, vein, capillary, oxygen, lungs, nutrient, waste, carbon dioxide, pulse, diet, exercise, drugs.

### Lesson 3 — The circulatory system
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the heart, blood and vessels deliver what the body needs."
- **Hook:** "The air you breathe and the food you ate meet in one place — your blood. How?"
- **Key activity:** Explore — the **heart** pumps **blood** through **vessels**: **arteries** carry blood **away** from the heart, **veins** bring it **back**, **capillaries** reach every cell. Blood picks up **oxygen** at the **lungs** and **nutrients** from the gut, delivers them everywhere, and removes **waste** (carbon dioxide). Follow a breath of oxygen on its journey. Fix "blood just sits in the body" and "heart and lungs do the same job."
- **Check for understanding:** "Name two things blood transports." → Any two: oxygen, nutrients, water, waste (carbon dioxide).
- **Differentiation:** support: match three vessels to their jobs; stretch: trace oxygen from lungs to leg muscle and back.
- **Materials:** circulatory-system diagram, vessel–job matching cards.
- **Joy:** tracing the oxygen "delivery route" around a giant body map.

### Lesson 4 — Pulse, exercise and a healthy heart (investigation)
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can measure my pulse and explain why exercise raises it."
- **Hook:** "Your heart races after star jumps — but why exactly does it speed up?"
- **Key activity:** Investigate — measure pulse for 15s at rest, do 1 minute of star jumps, measure again; record both and compare (a fair, measured test). Notice — the heart **beats faster to deliver oxygen to the muscles more quickly**. Discuss healthy living: **diet, exercise, avoiding harmful drugs/smoking** strengthen the heart. Report a **causal explanation** (X happens *because* Y).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does your heart beat faster when you exercise?" → To deliver oxygen to the muscles more quickly.
- **Differentiation:** support: count and record one pulse with help; stretch: explain the rise as a causal chain (muscles need more oxygen → heart pumps faster).
- **Materials:** timer, pulse-recording sheet, space to do star jumps.
- **Joy:** feeling their own heart pound and proving it on paper.

**End-of-unit 2 check:** Explain how the heart, blood and vessels deliver oxygen and nutrients; measure a pulse before and after exercise and explain the rise causally; name two healthy-heart habits.

## Unit 3 — Evolution and inheritance

**Essential question:** How do living things change over time, and how do we know?

**Key vocabulary:** inheritance, variation, offspring, adaptation, adapted, environment, survive, generation, evolution, fossil, evidence, selective breeding.

### Lesson 5 — Inheritance and variation
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that offspring inherit features but vary from their parents."
- **Hook:** "You look a bit like your parents — but why not exactly like them?"
- **Key activity:** Explore — offspring **inherit** features but show **variation** (your dad's eye colour, your own height). Notice — variation is normal and important. Compare dog breeds as **selective breeding** (humans choosing features). Fix "offspring are exact copies of their parents."
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do offspring vary from their parents?" → They inherit a mix of features, so they're similar but not identical.
- **Differentiation:** support: spot inherited vs varied features in a family picture; stretch: explain how dog breeds were made.
- **Materials:** family-resemblance pictures, dog-breed pictures.
- **Joy:** spotting which features they "inherited" and which are uniquely theirs.

### Lesson 6 — Adaptation and evolution (investigation)
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain adaptation and how helpful features spread over generations."
- **Hook:** "On dark bark, which beetle survives — the pale one or the dark one?"
- **Key activity:** Investigate — a **camouflage hunt**: scatter coloured paper "beetles" on a patterned background and **time** how fast each colour is found. Notice — well-camouflaged ones survive longer. Build the idea: variation means some are born with **adaptations** that help them **survive and reproduce**; those features get **passed on**, so over many **generations** the species changes — **evolution**. **Fossils** are the evidence. Fix "an animal changes its own body in its lifetime (a giraffe stretched its neck)."
- **Check for understanding:** "What does 'adaptation' mean?" → A feature that helps a living thing survive in its environment.
- **Differentiation:** support: explain why camouflaged beetles last longer; stretch: explain the whole beetle-evolution chain across generations.
- **Materials:** coloured paper "beetles," a patterned background, timer.
- **Joy:** the fast-paced camouflage hunt and seeing camouflage really "win."

### Lesson 7 — Fossils as evidence (links to Year 3)
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how fossils show that living things have changed over time."
- **Hook:** "How do we know horses or whales looked completely different millions of years ago?"
- **Key activity:** Explore — recap how fossils form (Year 3), then compare fossils of different ages (e.g. ancient vs modern horse). Notice — older and younger fossils differ, showing gradual **change over time** = evidence for **evolution**. Order fossils from oldest to newest by their features.
- **Check for understanding:** "What evidence tells us living things have changed over time?" → Fossils (comparing them across ages).
- **Differentiation:** support: order two fossil ages; stretch: describe a change shown across a fossil sequence.
- **Materials:** fossil-sequence pictures (e.g. horse evolution), ordering cards.
- **Joy:** ordering a fossil "time machine" from oldest to newest.

**End-of-unit 3 check:** Explain inheritance and variation; explain adaptation and how features spread over generations; explain how fossils provide evidence of change over time.

## Unit 4 — Light: how we see

**Essential question:** How does light travel, and how do our eyes actually see?

**Key vocabulary:** light, straight line, source, reflect, bounce, into the eye, shadow, opaque, mirror, periscope, angle.

### Lesson 8 — Light travels in straight lines (investigation)
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show that light travels in straight lines and use it to explain shadows and reflection."
- **Hook:** "Why can you only see the torch when three holes line up perfectly straight?"
- **Key activity:** Investigate — line up three cards with holes; the torch is visible only when the holes are in a **straight line** (light travels straight). Use this to explain **shadows** (straight beams blocked by an **opaque** object) and **reflection** (light bounces off a mirror in a straight line at an angle). Build a simple two-mirror **periscope**. Fix "light can bend round corners by itself."
- **Check for understanding:** "Which way does light travel?" → In straight lines.
- **Differentiation:** support: line up the holes and observe; stretch: explain how a periscope lets you see over a wall.
- **Materials:** three cards with aligned holes, torch, two small mirrors, tube for a periscope.
- **Joy:** building a periscope and spying over the table edge.

### Lesson 9 — How we see (light into the eye)
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that we see because light reflects off objects into our eyes."
- **Hook:** "However hard you stare, you can't see a black cat in a pitch-dark room. Why not?"
- **Key activity:** Explore — seeing needs light to **travel from a source, bounce off the object, and go INTO the eyes**. The key correction: **nothing comes out of your eyes** — light goes in. That's why total darkness = no sight. Order the light's path: source → object → eye. Fix "rays come out of our eyes to see."
- **Check for understanding:** "Does light come out of your eyes when you look at something?" → No — light goes into your eyes.
- **Differentiation:** support: order the source→object→eye path; stretch: explain why you can't see in a totally dark room.
- **Materials:** light-path diagram, ordering cards; (optional) a blacked-out box to demonstrate "no light, no sight."
- **Joy:** the box demo proving you genuinely can't see with zero light.

**End-of-unit 4 check:** Show that light travels in straight lines and use it to explain a shadow; explain that we see because light reflects off objects into our eyes (nothing leaves the eye).

## Unit 5 — Electricity: voltage, brightness and symbols

**Essential question:** How do changes to a circuit affect it, and how do scientists draw circuits?

**Key vocabulary:** voltage, cell, push, brightness, brighter, dimmer, series, circuit diagram, circuit symbol, bulb, switch, buzzer, motor, wire, predict.

### Lesson 10 — Voltage and brightness (investigation)
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can change a circuit's voltage and predict the effect on brightness."
- **Hook:** "Why is a torch brighter with two batteries than one?"
- **Key activity:** Investigate — build one cell + one bulb; note the brightness. Add a **second cell** (more **voltage** = more push) → bulb **brighter**. Instead add a **second bulb** in **series** (sharing one cell's push) → both **dimmer**. Change one thing at a time, predict, then check. Fix "more cells is always better" (can burn out bulbs) and "more bulbs = brighter" (in series they share). **Safety: batteries only — never mains.**
- **Check for understanding:** "In a series circuit, what happens to two bulbs compared with one?" → Each is dimmer (they share the voltage).
- **Differentiation:** support: compare one vs two cells and report brightness; stretch: predict and explain both changes (more cells / more bulbs).
- **Materials:** cells, bulbs in holders, wires, switch; brightness-rating sheet.
- **Joy:** making a bulb glow brighter, then dimmer, on demand.

### Lesson 11 — Circuit symbols and diagrams
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read and draw a circuit using standard symbols."
- **Hook:** "Engineers around the world all draw circuits the same way — want the secret code?"
- **Key activity:** Explore — learn **standard symbols**: **cell** (long/short line), **bulb** (circle + cross), **switch** (open/closed), **buzzer**, **motor**, straight **wires**. Match component → symbol. Draw the Lesson 10 bright circuit as a proper **circuit diagram**, then build a partner's diagram to check it works.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do scientists use circuit symbols?" → To draw and share circuits clearly with standard, recognised diagrams.
- **Differentiation:** support: match three components to symbols; stretch: draw and then build a circuit from a diagram.
- **Materials:** symbol reference card, component–symbol matching cards, paper, circuit kit.
- **Joy:** writing circuits in the "secret engineer code" and swapping with a partner.

**End-of-unit 5 check:** Predict and explain how more cells vs more bulbs change brightness; read and draw a circuit using standard symbols; state the battery-only safety rule.

## Unit 6 — Working scientifically (primary capstone)

**Essential question:** Can I plan, run and explain a full controlled investigation like a scientist?

**Key vocabulary:** controlled investigation, variable, prediction, evidence, conclusion, causal explanation, further test.

### Lesson 12 — A full controlled investigation (primary capstone)
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan and run a controlled investigation and explain my results causally."
- **Hook:** "You're the lead scientist now — design a real experiment and prove your idea."
- **Key activity:** Capstone — choose a question from the year (e.g. "does the number of cells change brightness?" or "which surface gives the slowest parachute?"). **Plan** it: name the variable to **change**, the variables to **keep the same**, the **prediction**, and how to **record** data. Run it, record in a table, and report a **conclusion with a causal explanation** ("the bulb was brighter *because* more cells gave more push"). Suggest a **further test** the results raise.
- **Check for understanding:** "After your conclusion, name one new question you could test next." → Any sensible follow-up using the same controlled method.
- **Differentiation:** support: complete a planning frame and run with prompts; stretch: plan, run, explain causally and design a follow-up test independently.
- **Materials:** the relevant kit for the chosen question, a planning frame and results table.
- **Joy:** being the "lead scientist" who runs the whole experiment and presents the findings.

**End-of-unit 6 check:** Plan a controlled investigation (one variable changed, the rest kept the same); record results in a table; report a conclusion with a causal explanation; suggest one further test.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Classification:** Classify living things into the broad groups (including a useful microbe), and sort animals into vertebrate/invertebrate.
2. **Circulation:** Explain how the heart, blood and vessels deliver oxygen and nutrients to the body.
3. **Pulse & health:** Measure a pulse before and after exercise and explain the rise causally; name two healthy-heart habits.
4. **Inheritance & evolution:** Explain inheritance with variation, and how adaptation and natural selection make a species change over generations.
5. **Fossil evidence:** Explain how fossils provide evidence that living things have changed over time.
6. **Light:** Show that light travels in straight lines, and explain that we see because light reflects off objects into our eyes.
7. **Electricity:** Predict how more cells vs more bulbs change brightness, and read/draw a circuit using standard symbols.
8. **Capstone investigation:** Plan and run a controlled investigation, report a conclusion with a causal explanation, and suggest one further test.
