## Year overview

Year 6 is the bridge from primary science to the three secondary sciences. Children meet the **big
models** that the rest of their science education rests on: the **cell** as the unit of life, the
**particle model** of matter, **energy** as something stored and transferred, **forces** as
interactions that change motion, **ecosystems** as webs of interdependence, and the **Earth in space**.
Every unit is taught through **working scientifically**: asking questions, predicting, planning fair
tests, measuring carefully (with repeats and averages), presenting data in tables and graphs,
concluding and evaluating. Every unit has at least one full investigation.

**What we assume from Year 5 (ages 10–11, UK Y6 / US Grade 5):** classification of living things,
the human circulatory system, inheritance, adaptation and evolution with fossil evidence, light
travelling in straight lines and how we see, electrical circuits (voltage, brightness, symbols,
resistance), the particle model of solids, liquids and gases, signs of chemical reaction and
conservation of mass, Earth's atmosphere, the carbon cycle and the greenhouse effect, and a first
complete independent investigation. Where Year 6 returns to these (particles, reactions, the
atmosphere) it opens with a short retrieval and then goes deeper — word equations, pH,
oxidation, the Keeling curve — rather than re-teaching. From earlier years: states of matter, reversible and
irreversible changes, dissolving and separating by sieving/filtering/evaporating, forces (gravity,
friction, air resistance), the Solar System, food chains and habitats, rocks and soils.

**Sequence.** Autumn: working scientifically (Unit 1), cells (Unit 2) and particles (Unit 3). Spring:
chemical reactions (Unit 4), energy (Unit 5) and forces (Unit 6). Summer: ecosystems (Unit 7), Earth
and space (Unit 8), and an **independent investigation for a science fair** (Unit 9) that is the
science strand of the graduation capstone. The order is deliberate: particles (Unit 3) explain
diffusion in cells (Unit 2 is revisited) and reactions (Unit 4); energy (Unit 5) is needed for food
webs (Unit 7) and for understanding the Sun (Unit 8); forces (Unit 6) explain orbits (Unit 8).

**What secondary relies on:** the cell model and microscopy; the particle model and its use to
explain states, diffusion, pressure and dissolving; word equations and the idea that atoms are
rearranged, not created, in reactions; energy stores and transfers with conservation; balanced and
unbalanced forces, speed and graphs; and confident, independent investigation skills.

**Home and school labs.** Every investigation here can be run with household or low-cost equipment;
where a school lab is available, the lab version is noted. **Safety is taught explicitly**: eye
protection for any heating, chemicals or stretched springs; adult supervision for heat sources; no
tasting in science. Two recommended purchases: a basic compound microscope (40×–400×) with prepared
slides, and a digital kitchen scale reading to 0.1 g.

## Unit 1 — Working like a scientist

**Essential question:** How do scientists make sure their conclusions can be trusted?

**Key vocabulary:** hypothesis, prediction, independent variable, dependent variable, control variable, fair test, accuracy, precision, repeat, mean, anomaly, range, evaluate, risk assessment

### Lesson 1 — Asking scientific questions and making hypotheses
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can turn an observation into a testable scientific question and a hypothesis with a reason." (UK KS3 Working Scientifically; NGSS Science & Engineering Practice 1)
- **Hook:** Drop a crumpled and a flat sheet of paper at the same time: "Why did one land first? How could we find out properly?"
- **Key activity:** Observe the paper drop; generate questions; sort into testable and not testable; write hypotheses in the form "If… then… because…"; share the scientific method as a cycle, not a straight line.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 'Which paper is the best?' a testable question? Improve it." → No — "best" is not measurable; e.g. "Does the surface area of a paper sheet affect how long it takes to fall 2 m?"
- **Differentiation:** support: question stems ("Does… affect…?") / stretch: write a hypothesis that includes a scientific reason (air resistance)
- **Materials:** A4 paper, stopwatch, metre rule, question-sorting cards
- **Joy:** "Question storm": how many testable questions can the class generate from one simple demo?

### Lesson 2 — Variables and fair tests
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify independent, dependent and control variables and plan a fair test." (UK KS3 Working Scientifically; NGSS SEP 3)
- **Hook:** "Galileo is said to have timed a swinging cathedral lamp against his own pulse. Does a longer pendulum swing faster, slower or the same?"
- **Key activity:** Plan a pendulum investigation together (new to this year — Year 4 already investigated dissolving speed): independent (string length), dependent (time for one swing), control (mass of the bob, release angle, same timer); discover why timing 10 swings and dividing by 10 is more accurate than timing one; spot unfair tests in example plans.
- **Check for understanding:** "In a test of how string length affects swing time, why must you release the bob from the same angle each time — and why time 10 swings, not 1?" → If the angle changed as well, you couldn't be sure the length caused any difference; one swing is too short to time accurately with human reactions, so timing 10 and dividing spreads the reaction-time error over 10 swings.
- **Differentiation:** support: a variables table with the independent variable filled in / stretch: explain why a control variable can't always be controlled completely (e.g. air movement) and how to reduce its effect
- **Materials:** string, metal washers or a lump of modelling clay as the bob, tape, protractor, metre rule, stopwatch, a table edge or doorframe to hang from
- **Joy:** "Unfair test detective" — spot the flaws in silly experiments

### Lesson 3 — Measuring, repeating and averaging
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can take accurate measurements, repeat them, calculate a mean and spot anomalous results." (UK KS3 Working Scientifically; NGSS SEP 4; Maths link — the mean)
- **Hook:** "Five people time the same ball drop and get five different times. Which is right?"
- **Key activity:** Time a ball drop from 2 m five times; record in a results table with units; calculate the mean; identify and discuss an anomaly; talk about human reaction time as a source of error.
- **Check for understanding:** "Your repeats are 0.64 s, 0.66 s, 0.91 s and 0.65 s. What should you do with 0.91 s, and why?" → It is an anomaly (far from the others); check for a mistake, repeat it, and leave it out of the mean if there was a clear error.
- **Differentiation:** support: a results-table template with columns ready / stretch: calculate the range of the repeats and explain what it tells you about precision
- **Materials:** ball, metre rule, stopwatches or phone timers, results-table templates, calculators
- **Joy:** "Reaction-time challenge" (ruler-drop test) to see how human timing adds error

### Lesson 4 — Graphs, conclusions and evaluation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose between a bar chart and a line graph, plot data accurately, write a conclusion and evaluate a method." (UK KS3 Working Scientifically; NGSS SEP 4)
- **Hook:** "A graph is a picture of a pattern. What pattern will our pendulum data show?"
- **Key activity:** Carry out the pendulum investigation planned in Lesson 2 (five lengths, three repeats of 10 swings each); plot a line graph (continuous independent variable); write a conclusion ("As… increases, …"); evaluate (What went wrong? How could we improve?). Include a simple risk assessment (swinging masses, clear space).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a line graph right for string length vs swing time, but a bar chart right for 'bob material' vs swing time?" → Length is continuous (values between the points exist); material is categorical.
- **Differentiation:** support: axes pre-drawn and scaled / stretch: draw a line of best fit and use it to predict a value not tested
- **Materials:** pendulum kit from Lesson 2, stopwatches, graph paper, rulers, calculators
- **Joy:** "Science lab licence" awarded after completing the first full investigation

**End-of-unit check:** Plan a fair test for "Does the height of a ramp affect how far a toy car travels?", naming all three types of variable, a results table with units and repeats, the right type of graph, and one way to improve accuracy.

## Unit 2 — Cells: the building blocks of life

**Essential question:** What are living things made of, and how do tiny cells work together to make a whole organism?

**Key vocabulary:** cell, microscope, magnification, nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria, cell wall, chloroplast, vacuole, specialised cell, unicellular, multicellular, diffusion, tissue, organ, organ system, organism

### Lesson 5 — Discovering the invisible: microscopes
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a light microscope safely and correctly and calculate total magnification." (UK KS3 Biology — cells; NGSS MS-LS1-1)
- **Hook:** Robert Hooke's 1665 drawing of cork "cells" from *Micrographia*: "He named them after the little rooms monks lived in. What did he see?"
- **Key activity:** Learn the parts of the microscope; focus using the lowest power first; view prepared slides and everyday objects (a hair, newspaper print, salt); calculate magnification (eyepiece × objective, e.g. 10 × 40 = 400×); learn about Antonie van Leeuwenhoek's "animalcules".
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do you always start focusing with the lowest-power objective lens?" → It shows a wider field of view, making it easier to find the specimen, and the lens is further from the slide so you won't crash into it.
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture card of the microscope with numbered steps / stretch: calculate the real size of an object from its image size and magnification
- **Materials:** compound light microscope (40×–400×), prepared slides, slides and cover slips, cork, hair, printed text
- **Joy:** "Micro-mystery": identify everyday objects from their magnified images

### Lesson 6 — Animal cells: preparing a slide
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can prepare a slide, observe animal cells and label the nucleus, cytoplasm and cell membrane." (UK KS3 Biology — cells; NGSS MS-LS1-1, MS-LS1-2)
- **Hook:** "You shed about 30,000–40,000 skin cells every minute. Let's look at some from your mouth."
- **Key activity:** Gently swab the inside of the cheek with a clean cotton bud (own cheek only), smear onto a slide, add a drop of methylene blue stain (or blue food colouring as a safe alternative), cover slip; observe at 100× and 400×; draw and label with a sharp pencil.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we add a stain before looking at cheek cells?" → Cells are almost transparent; the stain colours the parts (especially the nucleus) so we can see them.
- **Differentiation:** support: a partly labelled diagram to complete / stretch: explain what mitochondria do and why we can't see them with a school microscope (they are too small at 400×)
- **Materials:** microscope, slides, cover slips, sterile cotton buds, methylene blue or food colouring, pipette, gloves, disinfectant for used buds
- **Joy:** Seeing your own cells for the first time

### Lesson 7 — Plant cells and their differences
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can observe plant cells and explain the functions of the cell wall, chloroplasts and vacuole." (UK KS3 Biology — cells; NGSS MS-LS1-2)
- **Hook:** "A plant has no skeleton. So how does a lettuce stand up?"
- **Key activity:** Peel a thin layer of onion epidermis and stain with iodine; view pondweed (Elodea) or a moss leaf to see chloroplasts; compare plant and animal cells in a table; model cells in a box vs a bag of jelly (cell wall vs membrane).
- **Check for understanding:** "Onion cells are from a plant but have no chloroplasts. Why?" → The onion bulb grows underground, where there's no light for photosynthesis, so its cells don't need chloroplasts.
- **Differentiation:** support: a Venn diagram of plant and animal cell parts / stretch: explain why a limp lettuce becomes crisp in water (the vacuole fills and pushes against the cell wall)
- **Materials:** microscope, onion, iodine solution (dilute, goggles), pondweed or moss, slides, cover slips, forceps
- **Joy:** Watch chloroplasts moving (cytoplasmic streaming) in live pondweed cells

### Lesson 8 — Modelling cells
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can build a scale-aware model of a plant or animal cell and evaluate what the model shows well and badly." (UK KS3 Biology; NGSS MS-LS1-2; IB MYP Criterion A)
- **Hook:** "If a cell were the size of a classroom, how big would the nucleus be?"
- **Key activity:** Build 3D cell models from food or craft materials (jelly in a zip bag for cytoplasm and membrane, a box for a cell wall, grapes, beans); write a key; evaluate the model's limitations.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one way your model is unlike a real cell." → e.g. real cells are 3D and constantly moving; parts are not to scale; the membrane is not a plastic bag and lets substances through.
- **Differentiation:** support: a parts checklist with suggested materials / stretch: add mitochondria and ribosomes and explain their jobs
- **Materials:** jelly, zip-lock bags, small boxes, grapes, beans, craft materials, labels
- **Joy:** Edible (or craft) cell-model showcase

### Lesson 9 — Specialised cells
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the structure of specialised cells (red blood cells, nerve cells, sperm and egg cells, root hair cells, ciliated cells) suits their function." (UK KS3 Biology; NGSS MS-LS1-3)
- **Hook:** "A nerve cell can be over a metre long. Why would a cell need to be that shape?"
- **Key activity:** Card sort matching cells to functions and features; view prepared slides of blood or root tips if available; write "structure → function" sentences.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a root hair cell long and thin with a large surface?" → A large surface area lets it absorb more water and minerals from the soil.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards with key features highlighted / stretch: explain why red blood cells have no nucleus (more room for haemoglobin to carry oxygen)
- **Materials:** cell cards, prepared slides (blood smear, root tip), microscope
- **Joy:** "Cell job interviews" — each cell explains why it's perfect for the job

### Lesson 10 — Unicellular organisms
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can observe and describe unicellular organisms and explain how a single cell carries out all life processes." (UK KS3 Biology; NGSS MS-LS1-1)
- **Hook:** Drop of pond water: "There's a whole zoo in here."
- **Key activity:** Observe pond water (or hay infusion prepared a week earlier) for amoeba, paramecium, euglena; observe yeast; compare with bacteria (images); discuss how one cell eats, moves, grows and reproduces.
- **Check for understanding:** "Euglena has chloroplasts and can also move. Is it a plant or an animal? Explain." → Neither neatly: it is a single-celled organism with features of both, showing that living things don't always fit simple categories.
- **Differentiation:** support: an identification chart with pictures / stretch: calculate the real size of an organism from the field-of-view diameter
- **Materials:** microscope, pond water or hay infusion, dried yeast and warm sugar water, pipettes, slides, identification charts
- **Joy:** "Microbe safari" — who can spot the most species?

### Lesson 11 — Diffusion: how substances get in and out of cells
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain diffusion as the movement of particles from high to low concentration and investigate how temperature affects its rate." (UK KS3 Biology/Chemistry; NGSS MS-LS1-2, MS-PS1-4)
- **Hook:** Spray perfume in one corner: "Put your hand up when you smell it. How did it travel?"
- **Key activity:** Investigation: drop a tea bag or a drop of food colouring into beakers of cold, room-temperature and hot water; time how long until evenly coloured; explain in terms of particles moving (preview of Unit 3); link to oxygen entering cells.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does food colouring spread faster in hot water?" → The particles have more energy and move faster, so they mix more quickly.
- **Differentiation:** support: a partly drawn particle diagram / stretch: explain how a large surface area helps diffusion in lungs and roots
- **Materials:** clear cups or beakers, food colouring or tea bags, thermometer, kettle (adult use), stopwatch, perfume spray
- **Joy:** Time-lapse video of the colour spreading on a phone

### Lesson 12 — Levels of organisation: cell to organism
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the levels of organisation (cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism) with examples from humans and plants." (UK KS3 Biology; NGSS MS-LS1-3)
- **Hook:** Russian nesting dolls: "What's the biggest 'doll' of your body, and the smallest?"
- **Key activity:** Sort examples into levels; build a class "zoom" poster from a muscle cell to the whole human, and a leaf cell to the whole plant; link to Year 5's circulatory system.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is the heart a tissue or an organ? Explain." → An organ: it is made of several tissues (muscle, nerve and connective tissue) working together.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards to sort / stretch: explain how two organ systems work together (e.g. digestive and circulatory)
- **Materials:** nesting dolls (optional), example cards, poster paper
- **Joy:** "Powers of ten" zoom animation from a person to a cell

### Lesson 13 — Unit 2 assessment and science in the news: cells and health
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show understanding of cells and apply it to a real health story (e.g. how vaccines or stem cells work)." (UK KS3 Biology; IB MYP Criterion D)
- **Hook:** "Scientists can grow skin in a lab to help burn patients. How?"
- **Key activity:** Short assessment (below); read an age-appropriate article on stem cells or cell research; discuss benefits and questions.
- **Check for understanding:** "Name three structures found in plant cells but not in animal cells, and say what each does." → Cell wall (support), chloroplasts (photosynthesis), permanent vacuole (keeps the cell firm, stores cell sap).
- **Differentiation:** support: labelled diagrams allowed / stretch: explain what makes stem cells special (they can become different types of cell)
- **Materials:** assessment sheet, article
- **Joy:** "Cell champion" quiz show

**End-of-unit check:** Label plant and animal cell diagrams with functions; calculate the magnification of a 10× eyepiece and 40× objective (400×); match three specialised cells to their adaptations; explain diffusion with a particle diagram; order cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism with an example.

## Unit 3 — Particles, mixtures & separation

**Essential question:** How can the idea of tiny moving particles explain everything from melting ice to perfume in the air?

**Key vocabulary:** particle model, solid, liquid, gas, melting point, boiling point, evaporation, condensation, sublimation, diffusion, gas pressure, density, pure substance, mixture, solute, solvent, solution, soluble, insoluble, saturated, filtration, evaporation, crystallisation, distillation, chromatography

### Lesson 14 — The particle model revisited: forces, expansion and limits
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use the particle model — including the forces between particles — to explain expansion, and say what the model gets wrong." (UK KS3 Chemistry — particulate nature of matter; NGSS MS-PS1-4)
- **Hook:** "Railway tracks and bridges have gaps built in. Why would engineers leave gaps on purpose?"
- **Key activity:** Five-minute retrieval of Year 5's solid/liquid/gas particle diagrams (no re-teaching). New: the attractive forces between particles; expansion when heated (ball-and-ring demo, or a balloon-topped bottle in warm water; a liquid thermometer as expansion in action); explain with particles vibrating more and moving slightly further apart — the particles themselves do not get bigger. Evaluate the model's limits with marbles in a tray.
- **Check for understanding:** "A pupil says metal expands when heated because its particles get bigger. What is wrong, and what is the correct explanation?" → The particles stay the same size; they vibrate more and move slightly further apart, so the whole object takes up more space.
- **Differentiation:** support: particle-diagram templates with 'before heating' and 'after heating' boxes / stretch: explain why the particle model is a model, not a picture (particles aren't coloured balls, and there is nothing — not even air — between them)
- **Materials:** marbles, trays, ball-and-ring kit or a bottle with a balloon, bowl of warm water (adult), liquid thermometer, photographs of bridge expansion joints
- **Joy:** "Expansion hunt" — spot expansion gaps in pavements, bridges and window frames on the way home

### Lesson 15 — Changes of state and heating curves
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can investigate the melting of ice (or stearic acid in a lab) and explain the flat section of a heating curve using particles." (UK KS3 Chemistry; NGSS MS-PS1-4)
- **Hook:** "When ice melts, it stays at 0 °C until it's all water — even though you keep heating it. Where does the energy go?"
- **Key activity:** Measure the temperature of crushed ice and water as it warms every minute (or melt stearic acid in a school lab); plot a temperature–time graph; explain the plateau (energy used to overcome forces between particles).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does the temperature stay the same while ice is melting?" → The energy goes into breaking the forces holding particles in place, not into making them move faster.
- **Differentiation:** support: axes pre-drawn and a partly completed table / stretch: sketch the cooling curve for water turning to ice and label each section
- **Materials:** crushed ice, beaker, thermometer or digital probe, stopwatch, graph paper, (lab: stearic acid, water bath, goggles)
- **Joy:** Watching the graph go flat as predicted

### Lesson 16 — Evaporation, boiling and condensation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the difference between evaporation and boiling and investigate factors affecting evaporation rate." (UK KS3 Chemistry; NGSS MS-PS1-4)
- **Hook:** "Puddles disappear without boiling. How?"
- **Key activity:** Investigate evaporation of equal water volumes in dishes of different surface area (or in sun vs shade); measure mass loss with a scale over two days; explain with particles (fastest particles escape from the surface).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between evaporation and boiling?" → Evaporation happens at any temperature, only from the surface; boiling happens at the boiling point, throughout the liquid (bubbles form inside).
- **Differentiation:** support: a guided method sheet / stretch: explain why sweating cools you down
- **Materials:** dishes of different sizes, measuring cylinder, digital scale (0.1 g), water
- **Joy:** "Evaporation race" — design the fastest-drying set-up for a wet cloth

### Lesson 17 — Diffusion and gas pressure
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain gas pressure as particles colliding with surfaces and describe how temperature affects it." (UK KS3 Physics/Chemistry; NGSS MS-PS1-4)
- **Hook:** Put a balloon over the neck of an empty bottle and place it in warm water, then cold water: "What's pushing the balloon?"
- **Key activity:** Balloon-bottle demo; crushing a can by condensing steam (teacher demo, goggles); explain using particle collisions; link to diffusion from Lesson 11.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a football feel harder on a hot day?" → Gas particles inside move faster and hit the inside of the ball harder and more often, increasing pressure.
- **Differentiation:** support: a sequence of pictures to explain the balloon demo / stretch: explain why aerosol cans warn "do not heat"
- **Materials:** balloons, plastic bottles, bowls of warm and cold water, (teacher: drinks can, tongs, heat source, cold water trough, goggles)
- **Joy:** The dramatic can crush demo

### Lesson 18 — Density
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can measure the density of regular and irregular objects and explain floating and sinking with density." (UK KS3 Physics — density; NGSS MS-PS1-2; Maths link)
- **Hook:** "A huge steel ship floats but a tiny steel nail sinks. How?"
- **Key activity:** Measure mass (scale) and volume (ruler for cuboids; water displacement for irregular objects); calculate density = mass ÷ volume in g/cm³; make a density column (honey, washing-up liquid, water, oil).
- **Check for understanding:** "An object has a mass of 60 g and a volume of 75 cm³. Will it float in water (density 1 g/cm³)? Explain." → Its density is 0.8 g/cm³, less than water's, so it floats.
- **Differentiation:** support: regular blocks only and a calculation frame / stretch: explain the ship puzzle (the ship's overall shape includes lots of air, so its average density is less than water's)
- **Materials:** digital scale, measuring cylinder, displacement can or jug, blocks of different materials, honey, washing-up liquid, oil, water, food colouring
- **Joy:** The rainbow density column

### Lesson 19 — Pure substances and mixtures
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can distinguish pure substances from mixtures, including using melting and boiling points to test purity." (UK KS3 Chemistry — pure and impure substances; NGSS MS-PS1-2)
- **Hook:** "'Pure orange juice' — is it pure in the scientific sense?"
- **Key activity:** Define pure (one substance) vs everyday "pure"; sort samples (salt water, distilled water, air, gold, milk); demonstrate that salt water boils above 100 °C (at sea level) while pure water boils at 100 °C.
- **Check for understanding:** "A sample of water boils at 102 °C at sea level. What does this suggest?" → It is not pure — something is dissolved in it, raising the boiling point.
- **Differentiation:** support: sorting cards with pictures / stretch: explain why chemists use melting point to check the purity of medicines
- **Materials:** samples in labelled containers, thermometer, kettle or heat source (adult), salt
- **Joy:** "Is it pure?" game show

### Lesson 20 — Dissolving and solubility
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain dissolving with particles, use the words solute, solvent and solution, and investigate how temperature affects solubility." (UK KS3 Chemistry; NGSS MS-PS1-2)
- **Hook:** "Can you keep dissolving sugar in a cup of tea forever?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of Year 5's mass-conservation result (20 g sugar + 100 g water → 120 g solution), now with the words solute, solvent and solution. New: add weighed spatulas of salt (or sugar) to 100 ml of water at different temperatures until no more dissolves (saturated); record the mass dissolved; plot a solubility graph and compare salt (changes little with temperature) with sugar (changes a lot).
- **Check for understanding:** "At 20 °C, 100 g of water can hold about 200 g of sugar; at 60 °C it can hold about 290 g. What would you see if you cooled a saturated 60 °C solution to 20 °C, and why?" → Crystals would form: the cooler water can hold less sugar, so about 90 g comes out of solution.
- **Differentiation:** support: a results table and guided steps / stretch: use a solubility graph to predict how much would crystallise out on cooling
- **Materials:** digital scale, sugar, salt, measuring jug, thermometer, warm water (adult), stirring rods
- **Joy:** "Sugar vanishing trick" with the mass reveal

### Lesson 21 — Separating mixtures: filtration, evaporation and crystallisation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can separate a mixture of sand and salt and produce salt crystals, explaining each step." (UK KS3 Chemistry — separation techniques; NGSS MS-PS1-2)
- **Hook:** "Your salt has been mixed with sand by a clumsy chef. Get it back!"
- **Key activity:** Dissolve the mixture in warm water; filter out sand; evaporate some water (gentle heat, adult supervision) and leave to crystallise on a windowsill; examine crystals with a hand lens.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does filtration separate sand from salt water but not salt from water?" → Sand grains are too big to pass through the filter paper; dissolved salt particles are tiny and pass through with the water.
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture method card / stretch: explain why slow evaporation gives bigger crystals
- **Materials:** sand, salt, warm water, filter paper, funnels, beakers, evaporating dish, heat source (adult), goggles, hand lens
- **Joy:** Growing and examining your own salt crystals

### Lesson 22 — Chromatography and distillation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use paper chromatography to separate inks and explain how distillation produces pure water." (UK KS3 Chemistry; NGSS MS-PS1-2)
- **Hook:** "A ransom note was written with one of these three pens. Which one?"
- **Key activity:** Crime-scene chromatography with felt-tip pens on filter paper; compare with the "ransom note" pattern; teacher demonstrates distillation (or shows a simple solar still) and children explain it with particles; link to desalination in dry countries.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do different dyes in an ink travel different distances up the paper?" → Each dye has a different solubility and attraction to the paper, so some are carried further by the water.
- **Differentiation:** support: a step-by-step picture guide / stretch: calculate Rf values (distance moved by dye ÷ distance moved by solvent)
- **Materials:** filter paper or coffee filters, water-soluble felt-tip pens, cups, pencils, rulers; (demo: distillation apparatus or a solar still — bowl, cup, cling film, stone)
- **Joy:** Solving the ransom-note mystery

### Lesson 23 — Unit 3 assessment: the clean-water challenge
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can apply particles and separation techniques to design a water-cleaning process and explain each stage." (UK KS3 Chemistry; NGSS MS-ETS1-1)
- **Hook:** "2 billion people lack safely managed drinking water (WHO/UNICEF). Can you design a way to clean muddy water?"
- **Key activity:** Groups design and build a filter from gravel, sand, cotton wool and charcoal in a cut plastic bottle; test on muddy water; explain why filtration alone doesn't make water safe to drink (microbes and dissolved substances remain — needs boiling or treatment). Then short written assessment.
- **Check for understanding:** "Your filtered water looks clear. Is it safe to drink? Explain." → Not necessarily: bacteria and dissolved substances can pass through; it needs boiling, chlorination or distillation.
- **Differentiation:** support: a design template / stretch: evaluate designs quantitatively (turbidity comparison, flow rate)
- **Materials:** cut plastic bottles, gravel, sand, cotton wool, activated charcoal, muddy water, measuring jugs (**do not drink the water**)

**End-of-unit check:** Draw particle diagrams for the three states; explain melting and evaporation with particles; calculate density from mass and volume; choose and justify separation methods for sand + salt, ink dyes and pure water from sea water.

**Key vocabulary:** atom, element, compound, molecule, symbol, periodic table, chemical reaction, reactant, product, word equation, conservation of mass, physical change, chemical change, acid, alkali, neutral, indicator, pH scale, neutralisation, combustion, oxidation, catalyst

### Lesson 24 — Atoms and elements

### Lesson 26 — Physical and chemical changes

### Lesson 27 — Word equations and conservation of mass

### Lesson 28 — Acids and alkalis around us

### Lesson 29 — Indicators and the pH scale

### Lesson 30 — Neutralisation

### Lesson 31 — Combustion and the fire triangle

### Lesson 32 — Oxidation: how much of the air does rusting use?
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain rusting as oxidation with a word equation and use a week-long investigation to estimate the fraction of air that is oxygen." (UK KS3 Chemistry — oxidation; NGSS MS-PS1-2)
- **Hook:** "The Eiffel Tower is repainted about every seven years. Why — and why has the water climbed up our measuring cylinder this week?"

## Unit 5 — Energy: stores, transfers & resources

**Essential question:** Energy can't be made or destroyed — so where does it go, and how should we power our world?

**Key vocabulary:** energy store (kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic, thermal, chemical, nuclear), energy transfer, pathway (mechanically, electrically, by heating, by radiation), joule, conservation of energy, dissipation, efficiency, power, watt, kilowatt-hour, renewable, non-renewable, fossil fuel, conduction, insulation

### Lesson 34 — Energy stores and transfers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe energy stores and the ways energy is transferred between them, using the correct names." (UK KS3 Physics — energy; NGSS MS-PS3-5)
- **Hook:** A wind-up toy, a stretched rubber band, a bouncing ball and a lit torch: "What do they all have in common?"
- **Key activity:** Circus of energy examples; identify the stores before and after and the transfer pathway (e.g. wind-up toy: elastic store → kinetic store, transferred mechanically); draw energy transfer diagrams.
- **Check for understanding:** "When you lift a book onto a shelf, which energy store increases, and where did the energy come from?" → The book's gravitational potential store increases; the energy came from the chemical store in your muscles (from food).
- **Differentiation:** support: store-name cards with pictures / stretch: energy diagrams for a three-step chain (e.g. food → muscles → bike → friction)
- **Materials:** wind-up toy, rubber bands, balls, torch, circus cards
- **Joy:** "Energy detectives" around the home

### Lesson 35 — Conservation of energy and dissipation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that energy is conserved but that some is dissipated (usually by heating the surroundings), and investigate a bouncing ball." (UK KS3 Physics; NGSS MS-PS3-5)
- **Hook:** "If energy can't be destroyed, why does a bouncing ball stop bouncing?"
- **Key activity:** Drop balls from 1 m and measure bounce heights (use slow-motion phone video against a metre rule); calculate the percentage of height regained; explain where the "lost" energy went (sound, heating the ball and floor).
- **Check for understanding:** "Where does the energy go when a ball bounces lower each time?" → It is transferred to the surroundings — heating the ball, floor and air slightly, and as sound — so it spreads out and is less useful.
- **Differentiation:** support: a results table and guided explanation / stretch: compare balls and relate bounce percentage to efficiency
- **Materials:** different balls (tennis, rubber, table tennis), metre rule, phone camera, graph paper
- **Joy:** "Super-bounce" league table

### Lesson 36 — Food and fuel energy
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare the energy in foods using labels and explain the energy stored in food and fuels as chemical energy." (UK KS3 Physics/Biology — energy in food; NGSS MS-PS3-3, MS-LS1-7)
- **Hook:** "How far could you walk on the energy in one banana?"
- **Key activity:** Read kJ values on food labels; compare with activities (walking uses about 1,000 kJ per hour for many adults); in a school lab, the teacher can demonstrate burning a crisp or nut under a test tube of water to show temperature rise (not with nut allergies present).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do food labels show energy in kilojoules (kJ)?" → The joule is the unit of energy; food contains a lot, so thousands of joules (kilojoules) are used.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-selected labels with round numbers / stretch: calculate how many minutes of cycling a snack's energy would power
- **Materials:** food packaging with energy labels, calculators; (lab demo: test tube, thermometer, clamp stand, mounted needle, crisp — no nuts if allergies)
- **Joy:** "Snack vs sport" calculator challenge

### Lesson 37 — Heating: conduction, convection and radiation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain conduction, convection and radiation with particles and investigate how surface colour affects heating by radiation." (UK KS3 Physics — heating and thermal equilibrium; NGSS MS-PS3-3)
- **Hook:** "Why are so many houses in Greece and Morocco painted white?"
- **Key activity:** Three quick demo stations (Year 4 already compared insulating wraps, so we go beyond it): conduction (metal, wooden and plastic spoons standing in hot water — which handle warms?), convection (a coloured ice cube melting in warm water), radiation. - **Materials:** identical cans or jars, black paint or paper, foil, thermometers, lamp or sunshine, metal/wooden/plastic spoons, hot water (adult), food colouring, ice-cube tray, stopwatch
- **Joy:** "Design the ultimate flask" challenge using all three ideas

### Lesson 38 — Power, energy bills and saving energy

- **Materials:** appliance labels, a plug-in energy meter (if available), calculators, a recent household energy bill (with permission)
- **Joy:** "Energy audit" of your home

### Lesson 39 — Energy resources: fossil fuels
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how fossil fuels formed, how they are used to generate electricity, and their advantages and disadvantages." (UK KS3 Physics — energy resources; NGSS MS-ESS3-1, MS-ESS3-3)
- **Hook:** "The energy in coal came from sunlight that fell on Earth hundreds of millions of years ago."

### Lesson 40 — Renewable energy: build a wind turbine

### Lesson 41 — Unit 5 assessment: power my town

## Unit 6 — Forces & motion

**Essential question:** How do forces make things start, stop, speed up, slow down, turn and change shape?

**Key vocabulary:** force, newton (N), force meter, contact force, non-contact force, weight, mass, gravitational field strength, friction, air resistance, drag, upthrust, balanced, unbalanced, resultant force, speed, distance–time graph, extension, Hooke's law, pressure, moment, pivot

### Lesson 42 — Forces as interactions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe forces as pushes or pulls between two objects, measure them in newtons and draw force arrows." (UK KS3 Physics — forces; NGSS MS-PS2-1)
- **Hook:** "When you push a wall, the wall pushes you back. Really?"

### Lesson 43 — Mass and weight
- **Duration:** 40 min

- **Hook:** "An astronaut weighs about one-sixth as much on the Moon. Has she lost mass?"
- **Key activity:** Hang known masses on force meters (1 kg ≈ 10 N on Earth, using g ≈ 10 N/kg; more precisely 9.8); plot mass vs weight (straight line); calculate weights on the Moon (g ≈ 1.6 N/kg) and Jupiter (g ≈ 25 N/kg).

### Lesson 44 — Friction investigation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can investigate how the surface affects friction, using a force meter and a fair test." (UK KS3 Physics; NGSS MS-PS2-2)
- **Hook:** "Why do football boots have studs and ice skates have smooth blades?"

- **Check for understanding:** "Why must you pull the shoe at a steady speed when measuring friction?" → At a steady speed the pull balances friction, so the reading equals the friction force; if speeding up, the reading would be larger.
- **Differentiation:** support: a results table and one surface change at a time / stretch: investigate whether mass on the shoe affects friction (a line graph)

### Lesson 45 — Air resistance and water resistance: parachute challenge

### Lesson 46 — Balanced and unbalanced forces
- **Duration:** 40 min

### Lesson 47 — Speed and distance–time graphs

### Lesson 48 — Springs and Hooke's law

### Lesson 50 — Moments and levers
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate moments (force × distance from pivot) and use the principle of moments to balance a see-saw." (UK KS3 Physics — moments; NGSS MS-PS2)
- **Hook:** "Archimedes said: 'Give me a place to stand and I will move the Earth.' What did he mean?"
- **Key activity:** Balance a metre rule on a pivot; hang masses at different distances; find the rule (clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment); apply to see-saws, spanners and scissors.
- **Check for understanding:** "A 30 kg child sits 2 m from the pivot. Where must a 40 kg child sit to balance?" → 1.5 m: 30 × 2 = 40 × d, so d = 1.5 m.
- **Differentiation:** support: equal masses first, counting distances / stretch: balance three masses and explain why a long spanner makes loosening a nut easier
- **Materials:** metre rule, pivot (triangular block or pencil), slotted masses or coins, string
- **Joy:** "Balance the family on a see-saw" calculation, then test in a playground

### Lesson 51 — Unit 6 assessment: engineering challenge
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can apply force concepts to design, build and test a solution and explain it in a short assessment." (UK KS3 Physics; NGSS MS-ETS1-1–4)
- **Hook:** "Build a vehicle powered only by a balloon (or elastic band). Longest distance wins."
- **Key activity:** Design, build and test; identify forces (thrust, friction, air resistance); improve one variable; short written assessment.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain, using forces, why your vehicle eventually stopped." → When the thrust ended, friction and air resistance were unbalanced forces acting against the motion, slowing it to a stop.
- **Differentiation:** support: a kit with pre-made axles / stretch: calculate the average speed of the run and draw a force diagram at three stages
- **Materials:** card, straws, bottle caps, skewers, balloons, elastic bands, tape, tape measure
- **Joy:** Vehicle race day

**End-of-unit check:** Draw and label force diagrams with resultant forces; explain mass vs weight and calculate weight on the Moon; interpret a distance–time graph; use Hooke's law to predict an extension; calculate pressure and moments in simple cases.

## Unit 7 — Ecosystems & interdependence

**Essential question:** How does every living thing depend on others — and what happens when one part of the web changes?

**Key vocabulary:** ecosystem, habitat, community, population, producer, consumer, herbivore, carnivore, omnivore, decomposer, food chain, food web, photosynthesis, interdependence, competition, predator, prey, adaptation, biodiversity, pollination, bioaccumulation, quadrat, sampling

### Lesson 52 — Photosynthesis: where plants get their food
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain photosynthesis with a word equation and show that plants produce oxygen and starch." (UK KS3 Biology — photosynthesis; NGSS MS-LS1-6)
- **Hook:** "A tree can weigh tonnes. Where does all that mass come from? Mostly from the air!"
- **Key activity:** Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → (light) → glucose + oxygen; observe pondweed (Elodea) producing oxygen bubbles near a lamp at different distances and count bubbles per minute; (school lab: starch test on a leaf with iodine, teacher-led with hot ethanol safety).
- **Check for understanding:** "Where does most of a tree's mass come from — the soil, water or air? Explain." → Mostly from carbon dioxide in the air, which is turned into glucose and then into wood; water contributes too; the soil provides only small amounts of minerals.
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled leaf diagram with inputs and outputs / stretch: explain why bubbles slow down as the lamp moves further away
- **Materials:** pondweed, test tube or beaker of water with a pinch of bicarbonate, lamp, ruler, stopwatch
- **Joy:** "Bubble counting" race as the light changes

### Lesson 53 — Food chains and food webs
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can construct food webs from real ecosystems and explain how energy flows through them." (UK KS3 Biology — interdependence; NGSS MS-LS2-3)
- **Hook:** "In the Arctic, what would happen to polar bears if all the small fish disappeared?"
- **Key activity:** Build food webs with string and cards for two ecosystems (e.g. the Serengeti and a coral reef); arrows show energy flow; identify producers, consumers (primary, secondary, tertiary) and decomposers.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which direction do the arrows in a food web point, and why?" → From the food to the feeder — the direction energy flows (e.g. grass → zebra).
- **Differentiation:** support: a simple chain first, then combine into a web / stretch: explain why there are rarely more than four or five links in a food chain (energy is lost at each step)
- **Materials:** organism cards for two ecosystems, string, pins or tape
- **Joy:** "Web of life" game — pull one string and see who is affected

### Lesson 54 — Interdependence and population changes
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can predict and explain how a change in one population affects others in a food web, using real examples." (UK KS3 Biology; NGSS MS-LS2-1, MS-LS2-4)
- **Hook:** "When wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995–96, some people claimed they even changed the rivers. Could that be true?"
- **Key activity:** Study the Yellowstone wolves case (a proposed trophic cascade: fewer elk browsing by rivers → willows and aspens recover → banks stabilise), including the honest twist that scientists still debate how big the wolves' effect was compared with other causes (bears, human hunting, drought); analyse predator–prey population graphs (Canada lynx and snowshoe hare, from Hudson's Bay Company fur records); predict changes in class food webs.
- **Check for understanding:** "If the number of hares increases, what happens to the lynx population, and then what happens to the hares?" → The lynx population increases with more food; then more hares are eaten, so the hare population falls — the cycle repeats.
- **Differentiation:** support: a simple arrow-chain prediction sheet / stretch: explain why the lynx peaks come after the hare peaks
- **Materials:** Yellowstone case-study cards, lynx–hare graph, food web cards
- **Joy:** "Science courtroom" — weigh the evidence for and against the claim that "wolves changed the rivers"

### Lesson 55 — Competition and adaptation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how organisms compete for resources and how adaptations help them succeed, building on Year 5's evolution work." (UK KS3 Biology; NGSS MS-LS2-2)
- **Hook:** "Why don't lions and cheetahs hunt at the same time on the Serengeti?"
- **Key activity:** Plant competition investigation (Year 5 used the beak game for natural selection, so we move to real organisms): compare pots of cress sown two weeks earlier at different densities (10, 50 and 200 seeds per pot); measure the stem height and mass of a sample; relate crowding to competition for light, water and minerals. Then case studies of how animals reduce competition: lions and cheetahs hunting at different times of day, and the warbler species that Robert MacArthur found feeding in different parts of the same spruce trees.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why were the seedlings in the most crowded pot thinner and paler, even though every pot got the same water and light?" → Each seedling had to share the light, water and minerals with many others, so each got less and grew less well — that is competition.
- **Differentiation:** support: a guided results table and picture prompts / stretch: explain how competition could lead two species to specialise over time, using the warblers
- **Materials:** cress seeds, identical pots of compost sown two weeks earlier at three densities, rulers, digital scale (0.1 g), scissors, case-study cards
- **Joy:** "Seedling survivor" — predict which pot will produce the tallest single plant, then measure

### Lesson 56 — Pollination and seed dispersal
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how plants depend on animals for pollination and seed dispersal, and why pollinator decline matters." (UK KS3 Biology — reproduction in plants; NGSS MS-LS1-4)
- **Hook:** "About three-quarters of the world's leading food crops benefit from animal pollination. What if bees disappeared?"
- **Key activity:** Dissect a flower (lily or tulip) to find stamens, anthers, stigma and ovary; model pollination with cheese puffs or powder on cotton buds; seed-dispersal design challenge (spinning seeds like sycamore).
- **Check for understanding:** "How does a bee help a plant to reproduce?" → Pollen sticks to the bee at one flower and is carried to the stigma of another, allowing fertilisation.
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled flower diagram / stretch: research one pollinator from another continent (e.g. hummingbirds, fruit bats)
- **Materials:** flowers, hand lenses, cutting boards, blunt knives or scissors, cotton buds, powder or cheese puffs, card for seed models
- **Joy:** Spinning "sycamore seed" drop competition

### Lesson 57 — Fieldwork: sampling with quadrats
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use quadrats to estimate the population of a plant species and compare two habitats." (UK KS3 Biology — sampling; NGSS MS-LS2-1; Maths link)
- **Hook:** "How many daisies are on the school field? Counting all of them would take days."
- **Key activity:** Random sampling with 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrats in two areas (e.g. shaded and sunny); count a chosen species; calculate the mean per quadrat and scale up to estimate the total; compare habitats and explain differences.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must quadrats be placed randomly?" → To avoid bias — choosing spots with more plants would overestimate the population.
- **Differentiation:** support: a results table and a calculation frame / stretch: estimate the population of a field of known area and suggest how to make the estimate more reliable
- **Materials:** quadrats (0.5 m frames or string and pegs), tape measure, random number generator, identification guide, clipboards
- **Joy:** Outdoor fieldwork

### Lesson 58 — Human impact and bioaccumulation
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how human activity affects ecosystems, including how toxic substances build up along food chains." (UK KS3 Biology; NGSS MS-LS2-4, MS-ESS3-3)
- **Hook:** "In the 1950s–60s, the pesticide DDT was sprayed on crops. Soon eagles' eggshells were so thin they broke. How did a pesticide on plants reach eagles?"
- **Key activity:** Bioaccumulation simulation (counters as toxins passed up a food chain: plankton → small fish → big fish → bird); discuss Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962); explore plastics in oceans, deforestation and conservation success stories (e.g. the recovery of the bald eagle after DDT was banned in the US in 1972).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do top predators end up with the highest levels of toxins?" → They eat many prey, each containing some toxin, which isn't broken down or excreted, so it builds up.
- **Differentiation:** support: the counters model with step-by-step prompts / stretch: evaluate a real conservation project's evidence of success
- **Materials:** counters in two colours, cups labelled with organisms, case-study cards
- **Joy:** The toxin "pass-along" game

### Lesson 59 — Unit 7 assessment: ecosystem in a jar
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can design a sealed ecosystem, explain the interdependence inside it and show my understanding in a short assessment." (UK KS3 Biology; NGSS MS-LS2-3)
- **Hook:** "Could a jar of plants survive for years with the lid closed?"
- **Key activity:** Build a terrarium (gravel, charcoal, soil, moss and small plants, a little water, sealed lid); label producers, decomposers and cycles (water, carbon dioxide/oxygen); observe over the term; short written assessment.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can the plants in a sealed jar survive?" → Light enters; plants photosynthesise, making oxygen and glucose; decomposers recycle nutrients; water evaporates and condenses in a cycle.
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled terrarium diagram to annotate / stretch: predict what would happen if the jar were kept in the dark, with reasons
- **Materials:** large clear jars with lids, gravel, activated charcoal, potting soil, moss, small plants, spray bottle
- **Joy:** Your own living ecosystem to keep

**End-of-unit check:** Write the word equation for photosynthesis; construct a food web and predict the effect of removing one species; explain a predator–prey graph; estimate a population from quadrat data; explain bioaccumulation.

## Unit 8 — Earth & space

**Essential question:** How does Earth's place in space shape our days, seasons and the planet we live on?

**Key vocabulary:** orbit, axis, tilt, rotation, revolution, season, hemisphere, equator, phases of the Moon, gravity, satellite, light-year, galaxy, crust, mantle, core, tectonic plate, rock cycle, igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic, weathering, erosion, atmosphere, greenhouse effect, carbon cycle

### Lesson 60 — Beyond the Solar System: light-years, stars and galaxies
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use light travel time to describe distances in space and explain what a light-year measures." (UK KS3 Physics — space; NGSS MS-ESS1-3)
- **Hook:** "The light you see from the nearest star after the Sun left it more than four years ago. Are you looking into the past?"
- **Key activity:** Two-minute retrieval of Year 4's outdoor Solar System scale model (not rebuilt). New: light travel times (Sun → Earth about 8 minutes; Moon → Earth about 1.3 s; Proxima Centauri about 4.2 years); the light-year as a distance; the Sun as one star among roughly 100–400 billion in the Milky Way; the Andromeda galaxy (about 2.5 million light-years away) as the most distant object most people can see with the naked eye; write a "cosmic address" from home out to the Local Group of galaxies.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is a light-year a measure of time or distance? Explain." → Distance: the distance light travels in one year (about 9.5 trillion km).
- **Differentiation:** support: a "cosmic address" template and a ready-made light-travel-time table / stretch: calculate how far light travels in a year (300,000 km/s × the number of seconds in a year) and explain why a telescope is a kind of time machine
- **Materials:** light-travel-time cards, calculators, star map or planetarium app, images of the Milky Way and Andromeda
- **Joy:** Write your "cosmic address" and find Andromeda on a star map (or in a dark sky)

### Lesson 61 — Day, night and the seasons
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the seasons using Earth's 23.5° tilt, not its distance from the Sun." (UK KS3 Physics — Earth and space; NGSS MS-ESS1-1)
- **Hook:** "Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January. So why is January winter in Europe but summer in Australia?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 4's tilted-orbit model with a lamp and globe, then go deeper: shine a torch on squared paper at different angles and count the lit squares to show that steeper sunlight is more concentrated; compare day lengths in different cities (e.g. Oslo, Singapore, Buenos Aires) across the year.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it summer in the southern hemisphere when it is winter in the northern hemisphere?" → Earth's tilt means the southern hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun then — it gets more direct sunlight and longer days.
- **Differentiation:** support: a four-position diagram to label / stretch: explain why the equator has little seasonal temperature change and why the poles have midnight sun
- **Materials:** lamp, globe or ball on a skewer, torch, squared paper, day-length data for world cities
- **Joy:** "Midnight sun" and "polar night" data exploration

### Lesson 62 — The Moon: phases and eclipses
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the phases of the Moon and the difference between solar and lunar eclipses." (UK KS3 Physics; NGSS MS-ESS1-1)
- **Hook:** "The Moon doesn't make its own light — so why does it change shape?"
- **Key activity:** Hold a ball on a stick at arm's length and turn around a lamp in a darkened room to see the phases; record a month of Moon observations (started earlier as homework); model eclipses.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why don't we see an eclipse every month?" → The Moon's orbit is tilted slightly relative to Earth's, so the Sun, Earth and Moon line up exactly only occasionally.
- **Differentiation:** support: a phase-sequence card to match / stretch: explain why we always see the same side of the Moon
- **Materials:** lamp, polystyrene balls on sticks, darkened room, Moon diary
- **Joy:** The Moon-phase dance in the dark

### Lesson 63 — Gravity and orbits
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that gravity keeps planets and satellites in orbit and that gravity depends on mass and distance." (UK KS3 Physics — gravity; NGSS MS-ESS1-2)
- **Hook:** "The International Space Station is falling towards Earth all the time — and never hits it. How?"
- **Key activity:** Whirl a soft ball on a string (outdoors, safely) to model gravity as the "string"; discuss Newton's cannonball thought experiment; compare gravity on planets (link to Lesson 43).
- **Check for understanding:** "What would happen to the Moon if gravity suddenly switched off?" → It would travel off in a straight line (in the direction it was moving at that moment).
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture sequence of Newton's cannonball / stretch: explain why astronauts in orbit feel weightless even though gravity acts on them
- **Materials:** soft ball, string, open space; Newton's cannonball animation
- **Joy:** "Satellite" whirling (outdoors)

### Lesson 64 — Earth's structure and the rock cycle
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe Earth's layers and explain how igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks form and change in the rock cycle." (UK KS3 Chemistry — Earth and atmosphere; NGSS MS-ESS2-1)
- **Hook:** "The rock under your feet might once have been lava, a seabed or a mountain."
- **Key activity:** Examine rock samples (granite, basalt, sandstone, limestone, marble, slate) with hand lenses; classify by crystals, grains or layers; wax-crayon rock cycle model (shavings → pressed "sedimentary" → warmed and pressed "metamorphic" → melted "igneous", adult for heat); Earth's layers with a boiled-egg model.
- **Check for understanding:** "How can you tell granite formed from slowly cooling magma?" → It has large, interlocking crystals, which grow big when cooling is slow underground.
- **Differentiation:** support: an identification key with photos / stretch: explain why fossils are found in sedimentary rock but not igneous rock
- **Materials:** rock sample set, hand lenses, wax crayons, grater, foil, warm water (adult), boiled egg
- **Joy:** The crayon rock cycle

### Lesson 65 — The atmosphere, the carbon cycle and climate
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the composition of the atmosphere, the carbon cycle and how human activity increases the greenhouse effect." (UK KS3 Chemistry — Earth and atmosphere; NGSS MS-ESS3-5)
- **Hook:** "Carbon dioxide is less than half of one-tenth of a percent of the air. How can such a small amount warm the planet?"
- **Key activity:** Brief retrieval of Year 5's atmosphere composition, carbon cycle and greenhouse-jar model (not repeated). New: this year's chemistry applied — the combustion, respiration and photosynthesis word equations as the arrows of the carbon cycle; read and analyse the Keeling curve (Mauna Loa, from 1958: about 315 ppm then, over 420 ppm now); ocean acidification demo — blow gently through a straw into red cabbage indicator (Lesson 29) and watch it shift towards acidic as carbon dioxide dissolves, then discuss what that means for shells and corals.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does burning fossil fuels change the carbon cycle?" → It releases carbon stored underground for millions of years as carbon dioxide, faster than photosynthesis and oceans can absorb it, so levels in the air rise.
- **Differentiation:** support: a partly completed carbon-cycle diagram / stretch: interpret the Keeling curve's yearly wiggle (seasonal plant growth in the northern hemisphere)
- **Materials:** carbon-cycle cards, Keeling curve graph (NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory or Scripps), red cabbage indicator, clear cups, straws (one per pupil — blow out only, never suck), goggles
- **Joy:** Build the carbon cycle as a board game

### Lesson 66 — Unit 8 assessment: space mission briefing
- **Duration:** 45 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can apply Earth and space science to plan a mission briefing and show understanding in a short assessment." (UK KS3 Physics; IB MYP Criterion D)
- **Hook:** "Your team is planning a crewed mission to the Moon. Brief the astronauts."

- **Check for understanding:** "Explain the seasons to a younger child without using the word 'distance'." → An explanation using the tilt and direct/indirect sunlight.
- **Differentiation:** support: a briefing template / stretch: include a calculation of the astronauts' weight on the Moon and the time for a radio signal to reach Earth (about 1.3 s)
- **Materials:** fact cards, briefing templates, presentation materials
- **Joy:** Mission-control role play

**End-of-unit check:** Explain the seasons with a tilted-Earth diagram; sequence the Moon's phases and explain them; explain orbits with gravity; identify the three rock types and their formation; draw the carbon cycle and explain the enhanced greenhouse effect.

## Teacher guidance

- **Common misconceptions:** seasons caused by distance from the Sun (it's the tilt); plants get their food/mass from the soil (mostly from air — CO₂); particles in solids don't move (they vibrate); substances "disappear" when they dissolve (mass is conserved); heavier objects fall faster (in the absence of air resistance, all fall at the same rate); a moving object needs a force to keep moving (only to change its motion); mass and weight are the same; energy is "used up" (it is transferred and dissipated); cells are flat (they are 3D); the Moon's phases are caused by Earth's shadow (they're caused by our viewing angle of its sunlit half).
- **Safety first:** eye protection for all heating, chemicals and springs; adults handle boiling water, flames and hot ethanol; check for allergies (nuts, latex, pollen); no tasting in science except in dedicated kitchen lessons; wash hands after fieldwork and microbes; dispose of cheek-cell swabs hygienically.
- **Working scientifically in every unit:** every unit has a full investigation with variables, repeats and graphs; expect written conclusions that use the unit's scientific model.
- **International framing:** examples span the world — Yellowstone and the Serengeti, Iceland's geothermal power, Costa Rica's renewables, day length in Oslo and Singapore, water access in South Sudan. Adapt fieldwork to your local environment (any park, field, beach or garden works for quadrats).
- **Differentiation:** support uses more concrete models and structured templates for the same investigation; stretch adds quantitative analysis (rates, gradients, Rf values, error bars) and explanation with the model — deeper, not faster.
- **Vertical alignment:** Year 5 covered classification, circulation, evolution, light and electricity; Year 6 introduces the unifying models (cell, particle, energy, force) that secondary biology, chemistry and physics build on. Secondary (UK Year 8 / US Grade 7 / MYP 2) expects the particle model, word equations, energy stores and forces to be secure; they are all mastery checks here.
- **Cross-curricular links:** Maths (means, graphs, density, speed, pressure and moments calculations), English (*A Long Walk to Water* and the clean-water challenge; research skills), Geography (tectonics, climate change), Computing (spreadsheets for data), Life Skills (sustainability, fire safety).
