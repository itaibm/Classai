# Year 1 History (ages 6–7) — Scope & Sequence

> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Built to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Standards reference:
> `knowledge-base/subjects/history-geography/history-ks1.md` (UK Key Stage 1 = our Foundation and Year 1).
> Codes: **UK NC KS1 History** (LM = changes within living memory, EV = significant events beyond living memory,
> SP = significant people, LO = local history, HS = historical skills: chronology, sources, questions).

## Year overview

Year 1 history teaches six-year-olds to think like **history detectives**. Every unit asks the same two questions:
**"When did it happen?"** (chronology — placing events on a class timeline that grows all year) and **"How do we
know?"** (sources — photos, objects, diaries, paintings, books written at the time, and people's memories).

**What this year assumes (from Foundation, ages 5–6):** the words *now, then, past, long ago*; ordering a few
objects from oldest to newest; asking an older person about their childhood; a first local-history walk.

**How the year runs.** *Unit 1* builds the tools: timelines, living memory and sources, using the children's own
families. *Unit 2* studies one great event beyond living memory in depth — the Great Fire of London (1666) —
through the diary of an eyewitness, then compares it with the Great Chicago Fire (1871) to ask how cities learn
from disaster. *Unit 3* follows four explorers from four continents and six centuries: Ibn Battuta (Morocco),
Zheng He (China), Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary (Nepal/New Zealand) and Neil Armstrong (USA). *Unit 4* asks
what makes a person *significant* through people who made lives better: Florence Nightingale, Mary Seacole, Louis
Braille and Rosa Parks. *Unit 5* is a local-history study of the children's own town — written for any country,
with a worked example. *Unit 6* turns the year into a class museum and a finished timeline.

**What Year 2 relies on:** placing events on a timeline using years; the language *before, after, century*;
knowing that we learn about the past from sources and that sources can disagree; describing why a person or
event is significant. Year 2 (ages 7–8) begins the long view of world history — prehistory, and the first
civilisations of Egypt, Sumer and the Indus Valley — for which this year's timeline and source skills are the
foundation.

## Unit 1 — Time detectives: timelines, memories and sources

**Essential question:** How can we find out about the past, and how do we put it in order?

**Key vocabulary:** past, present, future, now, then, long ago, timeline, order, before, after, year, decade, century, old, new, change, living memory, source, evidence, clue, photograph, object, interview

### Lesson 1 — My life on a timeline
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can order events from my own life on a timeline and use the words before and after." (UK NC KS1 HS — chronology)
- **Hook:** "Which happened first — you learned to walk or you started school? How can we show it?"
- **Key activity:** Model the teacher's life timeline with photos. Children order 5 picture cards of their own life stages on a washing line, then draw their timeline with years (born 20__, walked, started school, now).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does 'baby' go at the left of the timeline and 'now' at the right?" → A timeline goes in time order — the earliest events come first (left) and the most recent last (right).
- **Differentiation:** support: 3 picture cards / stretch: add the year to each event and count years between two events
- **Materials:** washing line, pegs, life-stage cards, photos (with family permission), timeline strips
- **Joy:** "Guess who?" with teachers' baby photos.

### Lesson 2 — Old or new? Reading objects as clues
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use clues to decide whether an object is old or new and order objects by age." (UK NC KS1 HS — sources; LM)
- **Hook:** A mystery box with an old camera, a film roll, a smartphone and a vinyl record: "Which is oldest? How can you tell?"
- **Key activity:** Handle objects; list clues (materials, wear, how it works, style); order them; check with dates on labels.
- **Check for understanding:** "What clue told you the film camera is older than the phone?" → e.g. it needs film and can't show the photo straight away; phones came later.
- **Differentiation:** support: 3 objects with an adult / stretch: explain what job each object did and what replaced it
- **Materials:** old and new objects (camera, phone, telephone, record, torch, iron), labels with dates
- **Joy:** The mystery box.

### Lesson 3 — Living memory: toys then and now
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare toys from my grandparents' childhood with my own and say what has changed and stayed the same." (UK NC KS1 LM)
- **Hook:** "Your grandparents had no tablets. What did they play with?"
- **Key activity:** Explore photos and real toys from 50–60 years ago (spinning tops, skipping ropes, tin cars, marbles, yo-yos) and today; sort into Venn "then / now / both".
- **Check for understanding:** "Name one toy that is in both circles. Why do you think it has lasted so long?" → e.g. a ball or skipping rope — it's simple, cheap and fun.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture sort / stretch: explain what materials tell us about when toys were made (tin/wood vs plastic/electronic)
- **Materials:** old and modern toys or photos, Venn hoops
- **Joy:** Playing traditional games from different countries (hopscotch, jacks, marbles).

### Lesson 4 — Asking someone who was there: oral history
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can ask good questions to find out about someone's childhood and record their answers." (UK NC KS1 LM; HS — ask and answer questions)
- **Hook:** "An older visitor is coming to be interviewed. What do you want to know?"
- **Key activity:** Plan open questions (What did you… How did you… What was different…?). Interview a grandparent or older visitor (in person or by video call); record answers with drawings and key words.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'What games did you play?' a better question than 'Did you play games?'" → It gets a longer, more useful answer, not just yes or no.
- **Differentiation:** support: question cards to choose from / stretch: ask a follow-up question in the moment
- **Materials:** question cards, clipboards, recording device (with permission)
- **Joy:** A real interview with a guest.

### Lesson 5 — Photographs as sources
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find clues in an old photograph and say what it tells us about life then." (UK NC KS1 HS — sources)
- **Hook:** "A photo is a window into the past. Let's zoom in!"
- **Key activity:** Look at old photos of schools, homes and streets from different countries (black and white and early colour). Use a "zoom frame" to find clues (clothes, transport, buildings) and write "I can see… so I think…".
- **Check for understanding:** "What clue in the photo tells us it was taken a long time ago?" → e.g. horses instead of cars, clothes styles, black-and-white film.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided "I can see" only / stretch: ask one question the photo can't answer
- **Materials:** photo sets (school, street, market, kitchen), zoom frames, magnifiers
- **Joy:** Zoom-frame detectives.

### Lesson 6 — Unit check: my family history timeline
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make a timeline showing me, my parents' childhood and my grandparents' childhood and say how I know about each." (UK NC KS1 LM/HS — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Three generations on one line!"
- **Key activity:** Place three generations on a timeline with a changed object or toy for each; label the source for each fact (photo, interview, object).
- **Check for understanding:** "How do you know what your grandparent played with? What kind of source is that?" → From talking to them — an interview (memory) — or a photo.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture timeline with 3 slots / stretch: add dates and a "then vs now" comparison sentence
- **Materials:** timeline templates, interview notes
- **Joy:** Family timeline display.

**End-of-unit check:** Order six objects or photos from oldest to newest, explaining the clues, and name two kinds of source that help us find out about the past.

## Unit 2 — A great event: the Great Fire of London, 1666

**Essential question:** What happened in the Great Fire of London, how do we know, and what changed because of it?

**Key vocabulary:** century, 1666, London, Pudding Lane, baker, fire, spread, timber, thatch, wind, fire hook, leather bucket, eyewitness, diary, Samuel Pepys, source, cause, consequence, rebuild, brick, stone, St Paul's Cathedral, Christopher Wren, Chicago, 1871

### Lesson 7 — A fire over 350 years ago
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can place the Great Fire of London (1666) on our timeline and describe what happened." (UK NC KS1 EV; HS — chronology)
- **Hook:** Crackling fire sounds and a painting of the fire: "What is happening here? When?"
- **Key activity:** Locate London on a map; place 1666 on the class timeline (far to the left of grandparents!); tell the story: it began on 2 September 1666 in a baker's shop on Pudding Lane and burned for about four days.
- **Check for understanding:** "Was the Great Fire within living memory? How do you know?" → No — it was over 350 years ago, so nobody alive now saw it.
- **Differentiation:** support: 4-picture story sequence / stretch: count centuries on the timeline back to 1666
- **Materials:** painting of the Great Fire (e.g. from the Museum of London collection), map of London, class timeline
- **Joy:** Storytelling with sound effects.

### Lesson 8 — Why did the fire spread so fast?
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain three reasons why the fire spread so quickly." (UK NC KS1 EV; HS — cause)
- **Hook:** "Houses of wood, a dry summer, a strong wind… what could possibly go wrong?"
- **Key activity:** Model a street of 1666: timber houses close together, thatched roofs, a hot, dry summer, a strong east wind, and firefighting with buckets and hooks. Sort cause cards; build a "cause web".
- **Check for understanding:** "Which reason do you think mattered most, and why?" → A reasoned choice, e.g. houses close together, so flames jumped from house to house.
- **Differentiation:** support: 3 cause cards with pictures / stretch: explain how two causes worked together (wind + wooden houses)
- **Materials:** cardboard-box street model, cause cards, pictures of fire hooks and leather buckets
- **Joy:** Build the 1666 street (and see why it was a fire trap).

### Lesson 9 — How do we know? Samuel Pepys's diary
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what an eyewitness source is and find information in extracts from Samuel Pepys's diary." (UK NC KS1 HS — sources)
- **Hook:** "One man wrote about the fire while it was happening — and buried his cheese in the garden to save it!"
- **Key activity:** Read simplified diary extracts; discuss what an eyewitness is; find three facts; compare with a painting made later.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is Pepys's diary such a useful source?" → He saw the fire himself and wrote about it at the time.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture-supported extract / stretch: find something the diary can't tell us
- **Materials:** simplified Pepys extracts, a quill and ink (or feather pen) for role play
- **Joy:** Write a diary line with a feather pen.

### Lesson 10 — What changed afterwards?
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how London changed after the fire (brick and stone buildings, wider streets, fire insurance and firefighters)." (UK NC KS1 EV; HS — consequence)
- **Hook:** "St Paul's Cathedral burned down. Look at it now!"
- **Key activity:** Compare pictures of London before and after; learn about Christopher Wren's new St Paul's, new building rules (brick and stone), the Monument, and early fire insurance companies with their own firefighters.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did the new rules say houses must be built of brick or stone?" → Brick and stone don't burn like wood, so a fire can't spread as easily.
- **Differentiation:** support: before/after picture sort / stretch: explain how one change still keeps us safe today
- **Materials:** before/after pictures, photo of St Paul's today, photo of the Monument
- **Joy:** Build a "fire-safe" street from brick-pattern blocks.

### Lesson 11 — Another city, another fire: Chicago, 1871
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare the Great Fire of London with the Great Chicago Fire and spot similarities and differences." (UK NC KS1 EV — compare; HS)
- **Hook:** "205 years later, in America, another city burned. Did people learn anything?"
- **Key activity:** Locate Chicago; place 1871 on the timeline; learn the story (wooden buildings, dry weather, wind). Compare in a table with London. Discuss the story of Mrs O'Leary's cow — a legend, not proven — and why we must check sources.
- **Check for understanding:** "Some people said a cow kicked over a lantern and started the fire. Why do historians say this is a legend?" → Nobody could prove it; the story was told afterwards and may not be true.
- **Differentiation:** support: 3 comparison points with pictures / stretch: explain what both cities did after their fires (rebuilt with safer materials)
- **Materials:** world map, pictures of Chicago in 1871 and today, comparison table
- **Joy:** "Myth or fact?" game.

### Lesson 12 — Unit check: the fire detectives' report
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can retell the Great Fire in order, give causes and consequences, and name a source." (UK NC KS1 EV/HS — assessment)
- **Hook:** "The king wants a report from his best fire detectives!"
- **Key activity:** Create a 4-part report (what happened, why it spread, how we know, what changed) as a zig-zag book.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one cause and one consequence of the Great Fire." → e.g. cause: wooden houses close together; consequence: rebuilding in brick and stone.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture-and-caption book / stretch: add a comparison with Chicago
- **Materials:** zig-zag book templates
- **Joy:** Scroll-sealed "report to the king".

**End-of-unit check:** Place 1666 on the class timeline, explain two reasons the fire spread, name one eyewitness source and one change made afterwards, and say one way the Chicago fire was similar.

## Unit 3 — Explorers from around the world

**Essential question:** Why do people explore, and what makes an explorer important?

**Key vocabulary:** explorer, journey, voyage, expedition, discover, travel, route, ship, compass, map, Morocco, China, Nepal, Moon, astronaut, summit, significant, similar, different, century

### Lesson 13 — What is an explorer?
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can say what an explorer is and why people have explored." (UK NC KS1 SP)
- **Hook:** "Pack a bag for a journey to somewhere nobody you know has ever been. What would you take?"
- **Key activity:** Brainstorm reasons people explore (curiosity, trade, science, adventure); look at a world map; introduce the four explorers the unit will meet and place their dates on the timeline.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give two different reasons people have gone exploring." → e.g. to find new places to trade and to learn about the world.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture reason cards / stretch: think of an explorer today (e.g. deep-sea or space)
- **Materials:** world map, timeline, rucksack with items
- **Joy:** Packing the explorer's rucksack.

### Lesson 14 — Ibn Battuta: the great traveller from Morocco
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe Ibn Battuta's travels and explain how we know about them." (UK NC KS1 SP; HS — sources)
- **Hook:** "In 1325 a young man left his home in Tangier for a journey. He didn't come home for almost 30 years!"
- **Key activity:** Read *Traveling Man: The Journey of Ibn Battuta, 1325–1354* by James Rumford. Trace his route on a map across North Africa, the Middle East, India and China. Learn that he told his story to a writer who recorded it in a book.
- **Check for understanding:** "How do we know about Ibn Battuta's journey 700 years later?" → He told his story to a writer who wrote it down in a book that people still read.
- **Differentiation:** support: trace route with stickers / stretch: suggest why travelling took so long then
- **Materials:** *Traveling Man* by James Rumford, world map, string, stickers
- **Joy:** Following his route with a string "caravan".

### Lesson 15 — Zheng He and the treasure ships
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe Zheng He's voyages from China and compare his ships with ships today." (UK NC KS1 SP)
- **Hook:** "Imagine a wooden ship much bigger than any other in the world at the time — and a whole fleet of them!"
- **Key activity:** Learn about the seven voyages (1405–1433) of the Ming admiral Zheng He across the Indian Ocean to Arabia and East Africa, and the giraffe brought back to the emperor. Map the route; compare ship pictures then and now.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did Zheng He's voyages bring back to China, and what does that tell us about why he travelled?" → Gifts and animals like a giraffe, and goods — the voyages were for trade and to show China's power.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture storyboard / stretch: compare his routes with Ibn Battuta's
- **Materials:** world map, picture cards of treasure ships, modern ship photos
- **Joy:** Folding paper boats and sailing them in a tray.

### Lesson 16 — Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary: the top of the world
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary reached the summit of Everest in 1953 and why teamwork mattered." (UK NC KS1 SP)
- **Hook:** "The highest mountain on Earth — how would you get to the top?"
- **Key activity:** Locate Everest (Nepal/China border); learn about the 1953 expedition and the two climbers from Nepal (Tenzing, a Sherpa) and New Zealand (Hillary); discuss equipment, danger and teamwork.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why couldn't either climber have done it alone?" → They needed each other (and a large team) for safety, carrying supplies and making decisions.
- **Differentiation:** support: order the climb in pictures / stretch: compare their equipment with Zheng He's sailors' tools
- **Materials:** world map, photos of Everest, climbing equipment pictures (or a real rope and helmet)
- **Joy:** "Team climb" challenge — cross the classroom "mountain" roped together.

### Lesson 17 — Neil Armstrong and the Moon landing, 1969
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the first Moon landing and explain how we know it happened." (UK NC KS1 SP; HS — sources)
- **Hook:** Play the famous 1969 moon-walk footage: "What are we watching?"
- **Key activity:** Learn about Apollo 11 (July 1969) with Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins; sources: photos, film, rocks brought back, astronauts' accounts. Place 1969 on the timeline — within grandparents' living memory!
- **Check for understanding:** "Name two sources that show us the Moon landing happened." → e.g. film and photographs taken at the time, and Moon rocks brought back to Earth.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture sequence / stretch: explain why this event is within living memory but the Great Fire is not
- **Materials:** *Moonshot: The Flight of Apollo 11* by Brian Floca, video clip, photos
- **Joy:** Moon-walk in slow motion.

### Lesson 18 — Same and different: comparing explorers
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare two explorers from different times, using how they travelled, why and what they found." (UK NC KS1 SP — compare lives)
- **Hook:** "If Ibn Battuta met Neil Armstrong, what would they talk about?"
- **Key activity:** Comparison grid (when, where from, how they travelled, why, what they found, how we know) for two chosen explorers; order all four on the timeline.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is one thing that is the same about all our explorers, even though they lived centuries apart?" → They were brave and curious and went somewhere people had not been (or knew little about).
- **Differentiation:** support: grid with picture options / stretch: compare all four
- **Materials:** comparison grids, timeline
- **Joy:** "Explorers' dinner party" role play.

### Lesson 19 — Unit check: what makes an explorer significant?
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose an explorer and explain why they are significant, using evidence." (UK NC KS1 SP — significance)
- **Hook:** "The Explorers' Hall of Fame has one space left. Who gets it?"
- **Key activity:** Make a Hall of Fame card: who, when, where, why significant, how we know. Present and vote.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is your explorer significant — what changed because of them?" → A reason linked to impact (e.g. Armstrong showed people could reach the Moon).
- **Differentiation:** support: frame with picture prompts / stretch: argue against a classmate's choice politely
- **Materials:** Hall of Fame card templates
- **Joy:** Hall of Fame vote.

**End-of-unit check:** Place the four explorers on the timeline in order, compare two of them in three ways, and explain one source that tells us about each.

## Unit 4 — People who made a difference

**Essential question:** What makes a person significant — and how did these people make lives better?

**Key vocabulary:** significant, nurse, hospital, hygiene, war, Crimea, Jamaica, blind, braille, raised dots, fair, unfair, rights, protest, bus boycott, bravery, legacy, compare, source

### Lesson 20 — Hospitals long ago and Florence Nightingale
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe what hospitals were like in the 1850s and how Florence Nightingale improved them." (UK NC KS1 SP)
- **Hook:** A picture of a crowded army hospital in the 1850s: "Would you want to be a patient here? Why not?"
- **Key activity:** Learn about Scutari hospital in the Crimean War (1854): dirty wards, little food. Nightingale's changes: cleaning, fresh air, food, keeping records. "The Lady with the Lamp".
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did cleaning the hospital save lives?" → Dirt spreads germs that cause illness, so cleaner wards meant fewer soldiers died of disease.
- **Differentiation:** support: before/after picture sort / stretch: explain how her records (charts) helped persuade others
- **Materials:** pictures of Scutari hospital, *Florence Nightingale* by Demi, a lamp prop
- **Joy:** Hot-seating "the Lady with the Lamp".

### Lesson 21 — Mary Seacole and the British Hotel
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how Mary Seacole cared for soldiers and why her story is important." (UK NC KS1 SP)
- **Hook:** "She was turned away when she offered to help — so she travelled there herself."
- **Key activity:** Learn about Mary Seacole from Jamaica: her herbal and nursing skills, her journey to the Crimea at her own expense, and the "British Hotel" near the battlefield where she fed and nursed soldiers. Source: her own autobiography.
- **Check for understanding:** "How do we know about Mary Seacole's life?" → She wrote a book about her own life (an autobiography), and soldiers wrote about her too.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture sequence / stretch: explain why she paid for the journey herself
- **Materials:** portrait of Mary Seacole, map (Jamaica, Britain, Crimea), picture cards
- **Joy:** Setting up a "British Hotel" role-play corner.

### Lesson 22 — Comparing two nurses
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare Florence Nightingale and Mary Seacole and explain why both are significant." (UK NC KS1 SP — compare)
- **Hook:** "Two women, same war, very different stories."
- **Key activity:** Venn diagram: where they came from, what they did, how they helped, how people remember them. Discuss why Mary Seacole was less well remembered for a long time.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one similarity and one difference between the two nurses." → e.g. both cared for soldiers in the Crimean War; Nightingale worked in a big army hospital, Seacole ran her own hotel near the battlefield.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture Venn / stretch: explain why statues of both now stand in London
- **Materials:** Venn hoops, fact cards, photos of their statues
- **Joy:** Statue design — create a plaque for each nurse.

### Lesson 23 — Louis Braille: reading with fingertips
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Louis Braille's invention changed life for blind people." (UK NC KS1 SP)
- **Hook:** Close your eyes and feel raised dots: "Could you read this?"
- **Key activity:** Read *Six Dots: A Story of Young Louis Braille* by Jen Bryant. Learn he lost his sight as a small child in France and invented a raised-dot alphabet as a teenager. Make your name in braille with glue dots.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why was braille such an important invention?" → It let blind people read and write for themselves.
- **Differentiation:** support: make first initial only / stretch: explain why dots are better than raised letters for fingertips
- **Materials:** *Six Dots* by Jen Bryant, braille alphabet cards, glue or split peas, card
- **Joy:** Writing your name in braille.

### Lesson 24 — Rosa Parks and the bus
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what Rosa Parks did in 1955 and why it helped change unfair laws." (UK NC KS1 SP; link: Life skills — fairness)
- **Hook:** "What would you do if a rule was unfair?"
- **Key activity:** In age-appropriate language: in Montgomery, Alabama, in 1955, laws kept Black and white people apart; Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat; many people then refused to ride the buses (the bus boycott) until the rule changed. Discuss courage and peaceful protest.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why was Rosa Parks' action important for many people, not just herself?" → Her brave, peaceful action led many people to protest together, which helped change the unfair law.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture sequence / stretch: explain what "peaceful protest" means with an example
- **Materials:** *Rosa Parks* (Little People, Big Dreams) by Lisbeth Kaiser, timeline
- **Joy:** "Courage chain" — paper links naming brave acts.

### Lesson 25 — What makes someone significant?
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a simple test (made a change, helped many people, remembered today) to decide if a person is significant." (UK NC KS1 SP — significance)
- **Hook:** "Is a famous singer significant in history? Is your grandma?"
- **Key activity:** Build a class significance test; apply it to the four people; order them on the timeline; apply it to someone the children choose (from any country).
- **Check for understanding:** "Using our test, explain why Louis Braille is significant." → He made a big change (braille), it helped many people, and it's still used today.
- **Differentiation:** support: tick-box test / stretch: argue about a borderline case
- **Materials:** significance test cards, timeline
- **Joy:** "Significance court" debate.

### Lesson 26 — Unit check: a museum label
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a museum label about a significant person, with when they lived, what they did and why they matter." (UK NC KS1 SP — assessment)
- **Hook:** "The museum needs labels for its new gallery!"
- **Key activity:** Write and illustrate a label; include one source that tells us about the person; place on the timeline.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does your label show the person is significant?" → It explains the change they made and who it helped.
- **Differentiation:** support: label frame / stretch: compare with another person
- **Materials:** label templates, pictures
- **Joy:** Museum gallery walk.

**End-of-unit check:** Choose two people from the unit, place them on the timeline, explain what each did to make lives better, and use the class significance test to explain why they are remembered.

## Unit 5 — History where I live

**Essential question:** What is the story of our own place, and how can we find it out?

**Key vocabulary:** local, town, village, city, street, building, old, new, change, map, photograph, archive, museum, plaque, memorial, significant, then and now, evidence

### Lesson 27 — Clues all around us
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find clues about the past in our local area (old buildings, dates, plaques, street names)." (UK NC KS1 LO; HS — sources)
- **Hook:** "Our town is a history book — you just have to know how to read it."
- **Key activity:** History walk: look for date stones, old signs, memorials, plaques and old street names; photograph and record. *Worked example:* in a town like York (UK), street names ending in "-gate" come from an old Viking word for street; in Mexico City, the Templo Mayor ruins lie right beside the cathedral. Find your own town's equivalent.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did one clue tell us about the past here?" → e.g. a date stone shows when a building was built; a street name tells what used to be there.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture checklist / stretch: suggest what a clue might mean and how to check it
- **Materials:** clipboards, cameras, local clue checklist
- **Joy:** Local history treasure hunt.

### Lesson 28 — Then and now: old photographs of our town
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare an old photo of our area with how it looks now and describe changes." (UK NC KS1 LO; LM)
- **Hook:** Old and new photos side by side: "Spot the difference — across 100 years!"
- **Key activity:** Match old photos (from a local archive, library or online historic collection) with the same place today; list changes and things that stayed the same. Read Jeannie Baker's *Window* to see change over time.
- **Check for understanding:** "What has changed most, and why do you think it changed?" → e.g. more cars and roads because more people own cars now.
- **Differentiation:** support: 2 photo pairs / stretch: order three photos of the same place by date
- **Materials:** old/new photo pairs, *Window* by Jeannie Baker
- **Joy:** "Then and now" photo game.

### Lesson 29 — A significant local person, place or event
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find out about a significant person, place or event from our local area." (UK NC KS1 LO; SP)
- **Hook:** "Someone from our town did something remarkable. Who?"
- **Key activity:** Research (with teacher-prepared sources) a local figure, building or event; apply the significance test from Unit 4.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which source helped you most and why?" → A named source and why it was useful.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture fact cards / stretch: find two sources that agree
- **Materials:** local sources (photos, newspaper cuttings, museum leaflets)
- **Joy:** A visit or video call with a local museum or historian.

### Lesson 30 — Why did our town change?
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can suggest reasons why our local area changed (new transport, jobs, more people)." (UK NC KS1 LO; HS — cause)
- **Hook:** "Once this was fields. Why is it houses now?"
- **Key activity:** Look at maps or photos from different times; list changes; match to reasons (a railway came, a factory opened, the town grew).
- **Check for understanding:** "Give a reason for one change you noticed." → e.g. a railway station meant people could travel, so more houses were built.
- **Differentiation:** support: match change to reason cards / stretch: predict how the area might change in the future
- **Materials:** old maps, photos, reason cards
- **Joy:** Build a "then and now" model street.

### Lesson 31 — Interviewing a local elder
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use an interview to learn how our area has changed in living memory." (UK NC KS1 LO; LM)
- **Hook:** "Our visitor has lived here for 60 years. What do they remember?"
- **Key activity:** Prepare questions; interview; compare memories with photos — do they agree?
- **Check for understanding:** "Did our visitor's memory match the photos? Why might memories differ?" → Sometimes; memories can be different because people remember what mattered to them.
- **Differentiation:** support: question cards / stretch: ask a follow-up question
- **Materials:** question cards, photos
- **Joy:** A real guest's stories.

### Lesson 32 — Presenting our local history
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present one thing I learned about our local history and how I know." (UK NC KS1 LO — assessment)
- **Hook:** "You're the local history tour guides!"
- **Key activity:** Groups create a "then and now" poster or short guided talk with a source for each fact.
- **Check for understanding:** "What source tells us your fact?" → Named source (photo, plaque, interview).
- **Differentiation:** support: poster frame / stretch: guided talk with a question time
- **Materials:** poster paper, photos
- **Joy:** Tour-guide presentations.

**End-of-unit check:** Describe two ways our local area has changed, give a reason for one change, and name the source that shows each.

## Unit 6 — Our class museum and timeline

**Essential question:** How does everything we learned this year fit together in time?

**Key vocabulary:** timeline, chronology, century, before, after, museum, exhibit, source, significant, change

### Lesson 33 — Building the year timeline
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can place all the events and people we studied on one timeline in the right order." (UK NC KS1 HS — chronology)
- **Hook:** "Ibn Battuta, the Great Fire, Mary Seacole, the Moon… who came first?"
- **Key activity:** Order event cards (1325, 1405, 1666, 1809, 1854, 1871, 1953, 1955, 1969, plus family and local events) on the class timeline; count centuries.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which happened first: the Great Fire of London or the Moon landing? How many centuries apart are they, roughly?" → The Great Fire (1666); about 3 centuries (300 years).
- **Differentiation:** support: order 4 cards / stretch: count the years between two events
- **Materials:** event cards, class timeline
- **Joy:** Human timeline — children hold cards and arrange themselves.

### Lesson 34 — Setting up our class museum
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose an exhibit and write a label that says what it is, when it's from and what it tells us." (UK NC KS1 HS — sources)
- **Hook:** "Museums tell stories with objects. What story will your exhibit tell?"
- **Key activity:** Each child prepares an exhibit (an object, replica, drawing or source) with a label; arrange the museum in time order.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does your exhibit tell visitors about the past?" → A clear link between object and past life/event.
- **Differentiation:** support: label frame / stretch: add a question for visitors
- **Materials:** exhibits, label cards, tables
- **Joy:** Curating a real museum.

### Lesson 35 — Museum opening and year review
- **Duration:** 25 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can guide visitors around our museum and answer their questions about the past." (UK NC KS1 — end-of-year review)
- **Hook:** "The museum is open! Tickets, please."
- **Key activity:** Families or another class visit; children act as guides; final reflection: "The most interesting thing I learned in history this year is…"
- **Check for understanding:** "How do historians find out about the past? Give two ways." → From sources like diaries, photos and objects, and from people's memories.
- **Differentiation:** support: guide with a partner / stretch: answer an unexpected visitor question
- **Materials:** museum set-up, tickets
- **Joy:** The museum opening.

**End-of-unit check:** Guide a visitor through the class timeline in order, explaining for each of three exhibits when it is from and how we know.

## Books & resources

- *Window* by Jeannie Baker — a wordless picture book showing one view changing over 20 years; the best book for "change over time" (Units 1 and 5).
- *Peepo!* by Janet and Allan Ahlberg — a baby's day in the 1940s, full of period objects to spot (Unit 1).
- *Vlad and the Great Fire of London* by Kate Cunningham — the fire through a flea's eyes, historically careful and funny (Unit 2).
- *Traveling Man: The Journey of Ibn Battuta, 1325–1354* by James Rumford — beautifully illustrated, accurate account of the great Moroccan traveller (Unit 3).
- *Moonshot: The Flight of Apollo 11* by Brian Floca — precise, poetic, and a model of a non-fiction picture book (Unit 3).
- *Man on the Moon (a day in the life of Bob)* by Simon Bartram — a playful companion for the Moon lesson (Unit 3).
- *Florence Nightingale* by Demi — a clear picture-book biography with rich illustrations (Unit 4).
- *Six Dots: A Story of Young Louis Braille* by Jen Bryant — Louis's own determination, told for young children (Unit 4).
- *Rosa Parks* (Little People, Big Dreams) by Lisbeth Kaiser — the right level of detail for six-year-olds (Unit 4).
- **Resources:** Museum of London online collections (Great Fire paintings and objects); the diary of Samuel Pepys (simplified extracts); NASA image and video library (Apollo 11); BBC Bitesize KS1 History; a local archive, library or museum for Unit 5; a class timeline running the full width of a wall.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. Orders events from own life, family memory and the distant past on a timeline, using *before, after, year* and *century*. *(Seen: timeline task.)*
2. Explains the difference between living memory and "beyond living memory" with an example of each. *(Seen: oral.)*
3. Names types of sources (diary, photo, object, interview, painting) and explains what one source tells us and what it can't. *(Seen: source task.)*
4. Retells the Great Fire of London, with two causes and one consequence, and compares it with the Chicago fire. *(Seen: report.)*
5. Compares two explorers from different times and places. *(Seen: comparison grid.)*
6. Explains why a person is significant using the class test (made a change, helped many, remembered today). *(Seen: museum label.)*
7. Describes two changes in the local area and gives a reason. *(Seen: poster or talk.)*

## Teacher guidance

**Misconceptions:** "The past is all one time" (the Great Fire and the dinosaurs "at the same time") → use the whole-wall timeline constantly and count centuries; "old photos are in black and white because the world was grey" → it was the camera; "everything in a source is true" → the Chicago cow legend; "significant = famous" → use the significance test.

**Sensitive topics.** War (Crimea), disability (Braille) and racism (Rosa Parks) are taught in age-appropriate, factual, hopeful language, focusing on the courage of the people and the changes they made. Invite questions and answer honestly and simply.

**Making it international and local.** The spine visits Africa, Asia, Europe, North America and Oceania. Unit 5 must be your own place — use the worked examples as a model. If you teach outside the UK, you may swap the Great Fire of London for a great event from your own country's history, keeping the same lesson shape (what happened, why, how we know, what changed, compare with another city).

**Vertical alignment.** Foundation introduced *now and then*, living memory and a first local walk. Year 2 (ages 7–8) moves to prehistory and the first civilisations, using this year's timeline and source skills.
