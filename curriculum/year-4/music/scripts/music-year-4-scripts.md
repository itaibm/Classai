# Year 4 Music — Tutor Scripts (Lessons 1–11)

Complete tutor scripts for [Year 4 Music](../music-year-4.md), built to the [scripting guide](../../../SCRIPTING-GUIDE.md) and grounded in `knowledge-base/subjects/art-music/music-ks1-ks2.md`. Music is active: every lesson runs **listen/imitate → practise → perform** and cycles **Listen → Perform → Compose/Appraise**. The CHECK is an *audible success criterion* plus a question. Each beat ⏱ sums to the duration; the mandatory "if the child is stuck" support steps back to **body and voice** and a smaller pattern. A recorder is ideal for Unit 1; voice, body and household sounds cover everything else. Named listening pieces carry the year-long **notice → feel → judge** appraising thread across eras and cultures.

**Year 4 in one line:** put the notes **on the stave**, then zoom out to how whole pieces are **built** — texture, ostinato, AB/ABA/rondo — and place music in time by its **timbre**.

## Unit 1 — Pitch on the stave (reading melodies)

## Lesson 1 — Five lines, four spaces
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Pitch on the stave)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name the line notes (E-G-B-D-F) and space notes (F-A-C-E) on the treble stave."

**You will need:** a stave whiteboard or large stave cards; note-head counters (buttons/coins work); a recorder or any pitched instrument (optional); the **"Ode to Joy"** clip (Beethoven) ready.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 — higher on the page = higher pitch; a stave is five lines; notation writes music down to play it again.
**Key vocabulary:** **stave** (the five lines), **treble clef** (the G-clef sign), **line note** (sits *on* a line), **space note** (sits *in* a gap), **EGBDF**, **FACE**, **ledger** (an extra little line), **pitch**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** the stave is a **ladder** — climb it and the pitch goes **up**. **Lines = E G B D F** ("Every Good Boy Deserves Football"); **spaces = F A C E** (spells "face"). Pre-empt the classic slip: reading the page left-to-right only and forgetting that **up = higher**.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "All sheet music in the world hides a secret ladder — five lines. Climb the ladder and the notes get *higher*. Today we crack the code that lets you read which note to play. Ready to break in?"
**Show/Do:** draw an empty five-line stave; run a finger up it making a rising "wheee" with your voice.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If a note sits higher up the page, is it higher or lower in pitch?" → *listen for:* "higher."
*(If shaky: hum low, slide your hand and voice up high — "up the page = up in pitch.")*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "First, this curly sign at the start — the **treble clef**. It curls around *this* line and names it **G**, so people call it the G-clef. Now the **lines**, bottom to top: **E, G, B, D, F** — I remember them with *Every Good Boy Deserves Football*. And the **spaces**, bottom to top, spell a word: **F, A, C, E** — *face*. Lines are a sentence, spaces are a face."
**Show/Do:** label each line E-G-B-D-F bottom-up, then each space F-A-C-E; place a note-head counter on a line, then in a space, naming each.
**Emphasize:** "**Lines: Every Good Boy Deserves Football. Spaces: FACE.** Bottom to top, always."

### ⏱ 9–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn to place. Put a counter on the very bottom line — what's its name? Now jump up to the first space — what's that? Let's chant the lines together, bottom up."
**Do:** child places counters and names them; together chant "E-G-B-D-F" then "F-A-C-E."
**Ask:** "Is this note (point to a space) a line note or a space note, and what's it called?" → *listen for:* names the space and uses FACE.

### ⏱ 15–20 min · CHECK (audible + question)
**Ask:** "I'll place three note-heads — name each, then tell me which is the highest *pitch* and how you know." (Place one low line, one middle, one near the top.)
- ✅ **Expected:** correct names; "the top one is highest because it's higher up the ladder." — probe: *"Line note or space note — how can you tell?"* (on a line vs in a gap).
- ❌ **If reads them left-to-right as 'first, second, third' ignoring height:** the page-reading habit → **fix:** "Forget the order — look *up and down*. Which sits highest on the ladder? That one's the highest sound."
- ❌ **If muddles lines and spaces:** mnemonic mixed → **fix:** "Lines use the *sentence* (Every Good Boy…); spaces spell the *word* (FACE). Is this note touching a line or floating in a gap?"

### ⏱ 20–26 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Tutor places single note-heads; child names each (mix lines and spaces). Once secure, interleave: child *places* a note you name ("show me B"), then names two notes and says which is higher.
**Observe / watch for:** counting from the top instead of the bottom; guessing without using a mnemonic.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't name it for them; 2) name *why* — lines/spaces confused or counting from wrong end; 3) step back to body/voice — point low on the stave and sing low, point high and sing high, so "up = higher" is felt; 4) cover the spaces and read **only** the lines E-G-B-D-F bottom-up; 5) re-ask one bottom line note, then build up one rung at a time; 6) keep it safe — "Mistakes show us what to practise. Let's take a smaller step up the ladder."

### ⏱ 26–30 min · RECAP + listening seed
> **Say:** "Tell me the lines and the spaces, bottom to top." → *listen for:* "E-G-B-D-F; F-A-C-E."
**Listen (10–15 sec):** play the opening of **"Ode to Joy"** — "Watch the tune step gently up and down the ladder. We'll *read* this one soon."
**Process praise:** "You stopped guessing and used the ladder — that's how real musicians decode the page."
**Revisit later:** tomorrow we match these stave-notes to your recorder fingers. Bring back EGBDF/FACE as a 30-second warm-up next lesson.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** name just the four **FACE** spaces first; give a guaranteed early win on the bottom space (F).
- **Stretch (challenge):** name any note placed, line or space, at speed; introduce a **ledger** note (Middle C just below the stave) — "why does it need its own little line?"

**Extension (if time remains):** write your own name's *initials* as stave notes where they exist (e.g. an "A", "C", "E", "F", "G", "B", "D") and read them back.
**Assessment evidence:** child names line and space notes on the treble stave and states that higher on the stave = higher pitch.

## Lesson 2 — Read your recorder notes on the stave
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Pitch on the stave)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can read G, A, B, C, D, E on the stave and play them on the recorder."

**You will need:** a recorder for the child (plus one for the tutor if available); a fingering chart for G-A-B-C-D-E; stave flashcards showing single notes G up to E; the **"Ode to Joy"** clip ready.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L1 — treble-clef lines E-G-B-D-F and spaces F-A-C-E; from Year 3, recorder fingerings for B-A-G (and beyond).
**Key vocabulary:** **stave**, **line note / space note**, **fingering** (which holes you cover), **pitch**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** each **written spot** has **one fingering** — read the height, find the fingers, play the note. Pre-empt playing the right *finger* but the wrong *octave/note* by always naming the note aloud first ("read it, name it, finger it, play it").

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Your fingers already secretly know these notes from last year. Today we give each one its home on the ladder — so the page can *tell* your fingers what to do. Recorder resting on your chin, ready position."
**Show/Do:** quick warm-up: name the lines and spaces from L1.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "On the lines, what's the second one up?" → *listen for:* "G."
*(If shaky: re-chant EGBDF pointing rung by rung.)*

### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch my routine for every note: **read** where it sits, **name** it, **finger** it silently, then **play**. Here's **G** — second line up. Fingers… and play: *gentle warm air, not a blow.* Here's **A** — the space above. **B** — the middle line. We're climbing G, A, B, C, D, E up the ladder."
**Show/Do:** on the stave, point to G then A then B; show each fingering on the chart; play each with a soft, steady tone.
**Emphasize:** "**Read it, name it, finger it, play it** — four steps, every note."

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together now. I point, you name, then we finger and play. G… name it. A… name it. B… Let's add C, D, E."
**Do:** tutor points to flashcards; child names, fingers silently, then plays each pitch.
**Ask:** "Where does B sit — line or space — and which is higher, B or D?" → *listen for:* "B is a line; D is higher (it's higher up the ladder)."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK (audible + question)
**Ask:** "I'll show you a single written note — name it, then play it." (Show D, then A.)
- ✅ **Expected:** correct name *and* correct pitch sounds. — probe: *"How did you know it was D?"* (its place on the stave).
- ❌ **If plays a neighbouring note (e.g. C for D):** misread the rung → **fix:** "Count up from a note you're sure of — find G, then step up the ladder to it."
- ❌ **If names right but the pitch is wrong/squeaky:** fingering or breath → **fix:** "Cover every hole fully; warm gentle air, like fogging a window. Try just that note three times."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child reads a set of single-note flashcards (mixed G-A-B-C-D-E), naming and playing each. Once secure, interleave a tiny two-note step ("G then B — read both, then play").
**Observe / watch for:** skipping the "name it" step and finger-guessing; rushing past squeaks.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't play it for them; 2) name *why* — misread rung or fingering; 3) step back to **voice** — sing the note's pitch and *say* its name before touching the recorder; 4) shrink the set to just three notes G-A-B; 5) re-ask one note from the small set, then add one rung at a time; 6) keep it safe — "Squeaks are part of learning. Let's master G first, then climb."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP + listening
> **Say:** "Read me this note and play it." (Show one.) → *listen for:* correct name and pitch.
**Listen (15 sec):** **"Ode to Joy"** again — "Next lesson you'll read and play *this* tune. Notice it mostly steps to its neighbours."
**Process praise:** "You used your four steps — read, name, finger, play — instead of guessing. That's reading music for real."
**Revisit later:** next lesson we read pitch *and* rhythm together to play a whole melody. Warm-up the six notes G-A-B-C-D-E tomorrow.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** read just **three** notes (G-A-B); letter the notes lightly under the stave; secure a clean G first.
- **Stretch (challenge):** sight-read a **4-note** stave phrase with no letters; add the lower D or high F#.

**Extension (if time remains):** write a 4-note pattern of your own on the stave and play it to the tutor.
**Assessment evidence:** child reads G-A-B-C-D-E on the stave and plays each at the correct pitch.

## Lesson 3 — Play a notated melody
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Pitch on the stave)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can play a short notated melody reading both pitch and rhythm from the stave."

**You will need:** recorders; a one-line notated melody — the **"Ode to Joy"** opening in recorder range (with a note-lettered version for support); a gentle backing track / steady pulse source; the recording of **"Ode to Joy"** (Beethoven).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L2 — reading G-A-B-C-D-E and playing them; Year 3 rhythm reading (ta / ti-ti, the shape says *how long*).
**Key vocabulary:** **melody** (a tune — a journey of pitches), **pitch** (height = which note), **rhythm** (shape = how long), **pulse** (steady beat), **sight-read** (play it the first time).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** real reading uses **two clues at once** — the **height** says *how high*, the **shape** says *how long*. Pre-empt rushing the notes off the pulse by *always* clapping the rhythm and naming the pitches **before** playing.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Today, both clues at the same time — the *height* tells your fingers how high, the *shape* tells you how long. That's real reading. By the end you'll sound out a tune millions of people know, straight off the page."
**Show/Do:** play the very first two notes of "Ode to Joy."
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does the *height* of a note tell us, and what does its *shape* tell us?" → *listen for:* "height = how high (pitch); shape = how long (rhythm)."
*(If shaky: point to one note — "name says the pitch; the filled-vs-tailed shape says the length.")*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "My way into any new tune — four passes, no playing yet for three of them. One: **clap the rhythm** off the shapes. Two: **name the pitches** along the line. Three: **finger silently** while saying the names. Four: **play** with the steady pulse. Watch me prepare the first line of 'Ode to Joy'."
**Show/Do:** clap the rhythm; name the pitches (B B C D D C B A…); finger silently; then play slowly to the pulse.
**Emphasize:** "**Clap it, name it, finger it, then play it** — prepare before you play."

### ⏱ 9–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together. Clap the rhythm with me first… now name the pitches along the line… now finger silently… now we play it slowly with the pulse."
**Do:** child works through the four passes with the tutor on the first phrase.
**Ask:** "This tune mostly *steps* to its neighbours — does it leap about or move smoothly?" → *listen for:* "smoothly / steps to neighbours."

### ⏱ 15–21 min · CHECK (audible + question)
**Ask:** "Sight-read this 2-bar melody — prepare it your way, then play it in time." (Give a fresh 2-bar phrase in G-A-B-C-D-E.)
- ✅ **Expected:** correct pitches *and* rhythm, steady to the pulse. — probe: *"Which pass helped most before you played?"*
- ❌ **If pitches right but rhythm rushed/uneven:** skipped the clap-pass → **fix:** "Clap the rhythm on its own first, with the pulse, then add the fingers."
- ❌ **If rhythm right but a wrong pitch:** misread a rung → **fix:** "Find a sure note nearby and step up or down the ladder to the tricky one."

### ⏱ 21–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child plays the "Ode to Joy" opening with the backing track, aiming for steady pulse and correct notes. Once secure, interleave: play it again from a copy with **no** letters.
**Observe / watch for:** stopping dead at a mistake (teach "keep the pulse, catch the next note"); jabbing notes off the beat.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't play it for them; 2) name *why* — pitch slip, rhythm rush, or both colliding; 3) step back to **body/voice** — *sing* the phrase on letter-names to a steady tap, no recorder; 4) shrink to the **first two notes** only, looped to the pulse; 5) re-add one note at a time, rebuilding the phrase; 6) keep it safe — "Let's nail two notes in time, then grow the tune."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP + listening (notice→feel→judge)
> **Say:** "How do you prepare a new tune before playing it?" → *listen for:* "clap the rhythm, name the pitches, finger it, then play."
**Listen (20 sec):** **"Ode to Joy"** in full texture — **notice** ("the melody steps up then comes back down"), **feel** ("it builds and feels joyful"), **judge** ("I liked it because…"). 
**Process praise:** "You prepared instead of diving in, and kept the pulse through a slip — that's a reader's discipline."
**Revisit later:** Unit 1 done — you can read pitch *and* rhythm. Next unit: hearing many layers at once (texture). Bring back "Ode to Joy" as a warm-up.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** play with the note-letters written in; tutor claps the pulse aloud.
- **Stretch (challenge):** play from the stave alone, no letters; add the second phrase of the tune.

**Extension (if time remains):** add a soft dynamic shape — start quieter, grow louder as the tune rises.
**Assessment evidence:** child sight-reads a short notated melody, playing correct pitch and rhythm to a steady pulse.

> **End-of-unit 1 check:** Child names line and space notes on the treble stave and plays a short notated melody on the recorder, reading both pitch and rhythm.

## Lesson 4 — Texture: thin and thick
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Elements together)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can tell a thin texture (few layers) from a thick one (many layers)."

**You will need:** a solo clip (one voice or one instrument) and a full-band/choir clip; the tutor and child to build live texture (willing family members add extra layers); the **"Mambo" from *West Side Story*** clip (Bernstein) ready.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 — the dimensions (pulse, tempo, dynamics, pitch, timbre); timbre = *what kind* of sound.
**Key vocabulary:** **texture** (how many layers play at once), **thin** (few — e.g. a solo), **thick** (many — a band/choir), **layer**, **melody**, **accompaniment**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **texture = how many layers**, *not* how loud and *not* what kind of sound. Pre-empt confusing **texture (how many)** with **timbre (what kind)** and with **dynamics (how loud)** — a thin texture can still be loud.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "One singer, all alone… *(hum a single line)* …then a whole choir crashes in around them. Your ears just felt the music get *thicker*. Today: how many layers can you count?"
**Show/Do:** play 5 sec of the solo clip, then 5 sec of the full-band clip.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's timbre — the number of sounds, or the *kind* of sound?" → *listen for:* "the kind of sound."
*(If shaky: tap a pan then a cushion — same number, different *kind* = timbre.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "New word: **texture** — *how many layers* of sound are playing together. One layer is a **thin** texture, like a solo singer. Lots of layers stacked up is a **thick** texture, like a whole band. Careful — texture is *how many*, not *how loud* and not *what kind*. A single loud trumpet is still thin: one layer."
**Show/Do:** clap one steady layer alone (thin); then add a stamp, a hum, a tap — feel it thicken.
**Emphasize:** "**Texture = how many layers. Thin = few, thick = many.**"

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's build texture and *feel* it. You clap a steady beat — that's one thin layer. I add a hum… now a stamp… now a tap. Hear the room fill up?"
**Do:** child holds one layer while tutor (and any family members joining in) stack more; pause and ask how many layers.
**Ask:** "Did it get thicker or thinner as we added parts?" → *listen for:* "thicker — more layers."

### ⏱ 13–18 min · CHECK (audible + question)
**Ask:** "Listen to these two clips — which is thin and which is thick, and why?" (Play the solo, then the full-band clip.)
- ✅ **Expected:** "solo = thin (one layer), band = thick (many layers)." — probe: *"Is one singer alone thin or thick?"* ("thin").
- ❌ **If says the louder clip is 'thick' because it's loud:** texture/dynamics muddled → **fix:** "Forget loud — *count the layers*. How many different things are making sound?"
- ❌ **If counts instrument *types* not layers:** texture/timbre muddled → **fix:** "Texture is *how many* sounds at once, even if they're the same kind."

### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child sorts several short clips into thin / thick. Once secure, interleave: count and *name* a couple of the layers in one clip ("a drum, a singer, a guitar — three layers, thick").
**Observe / watch for:** "thick = better" assumptions; defaulting to loud=thick.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't label it for them; 2) name *why* — counting volume or kind, not layers; 3) step back to **body** — rebuild texture live, adding one layer at a time so "how many" is felt; 4) shrink to two clips that are obviously one-layer vs many; 5) re-ask "how many things do you hear?" then build back up; 6) keep it safe — "A thin solo can be just as beautiful as a thick choir."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP + listening (notice→feel→judge)
> **Say:** "What does texture mean, and what's a thin texture?" → *listen for:* "how many layers; thin = few/one (a solo)."
**Listen (20 sec):** **"Mambo"** from *West Side Story* — **notice** ("lots of layers — brass, percussion, rhythm — a thick texture"), **feel** ("wild, exciting energy"), **judge** with a *because*.
**Process praise:** "You counted layers instead of guessing from loudness — that's a sharp musical ear."
**Revisit later:** next, a *repeating* layer that holds a song together — the ostinato.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** thin vs thick on two very clear clips; raise one hand for thin, two for thick.
- **Stretch (challenge):** count *and* name the layers in a richer clip; judge whether texture changes within one piece.

**Extension (if time remains):** find the thinnest *and* the thickest moment in one song.
**Assessment evidence:** child reliably distinguishes thin from thick texture and explains it as the number of layers.

## Lesson 5 — Ostinato (a layer that repeats)
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Elements together)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can play a repeating ostinato pattern and keep it going under a melody."

**You will need:** tuned or untuned percussion, or recorders; a simple known melody for the tutor to play on top; the **"Mambo"** clip ready (its driving repeated patterns).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L4 — texture and layers (two layers = thicker); Year 3 — keeping a steady pulse; a short rhythm pattern.
**Key vocabulary:** **ostinato** (a short pattern that repeats over and over), **layer**, **melody**, **accompaniment**, **loop**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** an **ostinato repeats *exactly* and *steadily*** — same pattern, locked to the pulse, even while a melody changes on top. Pre-empt the ostinato drifting, speeding up, or changing each time the melody moves.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "A short pattern, looped forever, can hold a whole song together. Pop songs, film music, African drumming — all built on these loops. It's called an **ostinato**. Let's lock into one."
**Show/Do:** loop a simple 4-beat pattern (e.g. tap-tap-rest-tap) twice.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If we play two layers at once, is the texture thinner or thicker?" → *listen for:* "thicker."
*(If shaky: clap alone (thin), then add a stamp (thicker) — feel the layers.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "**Ostinato** means 'stubborn' — a short pattern that *stubbornly repeats*, exactly the same, over and over. Here's mine: *boom-boom-rest-boom*, four beats, then again — and again. The trick: it stays locked to the pulse and never changes, even when something happens on top."
**Show/Do:** play a 4-beat ostinato, loop it four times rock-steady; then hum a melody over your own loop.
**Emphasize:** "**An ostinato repeats exactly the same, locked to the pulse.**"

### ⏱ 9–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "You take the ostinato — *boom-boom-rest-boom*. Loop it steadily. I'll play a tune on top. Don't let me pull you off your loop!"
**Do:** child loops the ostinato; tutor plays a melody over it; pause, then **swap roles**.
**Ask:** "When my tune changed, did your ostinato change?" → *listen for:* "no — it stayed the same."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK (audible + question)
**Ask:** "Keep your ostinato going steadily while I play a melody for eight beats — then tell me what an ostinato is."
- ✅ **Expected:** the loop stays exact and steady under the melody; "a short pattern that repeats over and over." — probe: *"Did it change when my tune did?"* (no).
- ❌ **If the ostinato changes/drifts when the melody enters:** distracted by the top layer → **fix:** "Eyes down, count your four beats out loud at first — your job is *same, same, same*."
- ❌ **If it speeds up or slows:** lost the pulse → **fix:** "Lock to a steady tap; whisper the pattern to keep it even."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child keeps the ostinato through a whole short tune; then swap so the child plays the melody over the tutor's ostinato. Once secure, interleave a *melodic* ostinato (e.g. G-G-A-G on recorder).
**Observe / watch for:** the loop morphing each cycle; rushing when the melody gets busy.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't play it for them; 2) name *why* — pattern changing or tempo drifting; 3) step back to **body/voice** — *say* the pattern as words ("ba-na-na… stop") to a tap, no instrument; 4) shrink to a **one-note/one-clap** ostinato on every beat; 5) loop the tiny version under a slow hum, then grow it; 6) keep it safe — "Lock one simple loop first — then we add the tune."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP + listening (notice→feel→judge)
> **Say:** "What is an ostinato?" → *listen for:* "a short pattern that repeats over and over."
**Listen (20 sec):** **"Mambo"** — **notice** the repeated driving patterns underneath; **feel** the groove; **judge** ("the looping rhythm makes it feel relentless and exciting").
**Process praise:** "You held your loop *exactly* while a whole tune danced on top — that's real duet control."
**Revisit later:** next, we put *all* the dimensions together to describe a clip like a detective.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** a **one-note / one-clap** ostinato on each beat; tutor taps the pulse alongside.
- **Stretch (challenge):** keep a **melodic** ostinato (a short pitched riff) steady under a *changing* tune.

**Extension (if time remains):** invent your own 4-beat ostinato and teach it to the tutor to play over.
**Assessment evidence:** child sustains an exact, steady ostinato as one layer under a melody and defines it.

## Lesson 6 — Music detective (describe with the elements)
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Elements together)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe a clip using at least four dimensions, including texture."

**You will need:** a rich short clip (the **"Mambo"** from *West Side Story* works well); a written **dimension checklist** (pulse/tempo, dynamics, pitch, timbre, texture); pencil/paper.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L4–L5 texture and ostinato; all the dimensions from Year 3 (pulse, tempo, dynamics, pitch, timbre).
**Key vocabulary:** **dimensions**, **pulse/tempo**, **dynamics**, **pitch**, **timbre**, **texture**, **appraise** (describe and judge with reasons).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** a real description names **several dimensions**, not one word — and now includes **texture**. Pre-empt the "it's just *good* / *fast*" answer; insist on a *because* with a dimension word.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "A music *detective* never says just 'it's good'. They list *everything* going on — the pulse, the speed, the volume, the tune, the instruments, the layers. Today you become that detective. Got your magnifying ears?"
**Show/Do:** show the checklist; read the five clue-words aloud.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Name two dimensions of music we can listen for." → *listen for:* any two of pulse/tempo, dynamics, pitch, timbre, texture.
*(If shaky: point along the checklist and read each clue-word.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch me detect. *(play 15 sec)* Pulse — fast. Dynamics — loud. Pitch — a high melody jumps about. Timbre — lots of brass and percussion. Texture — *many* layers, so it's thick. Now I join them up: 'Fast and loud, a high jumpy tune, brassy, with a thick texture.' Five clues, one description."
**Show/Do:** tick each box on the checklist as you name it; then say the joined-up sentence.
**Emphasize:** "**Name several dimensions — and always include texture.**"

### ⏱ 9–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn with me. Listen again. Pulse — fast or slow? Loud or quiet? High or low melody? What instruments? Many layers or few?"
**Do:** play the clip; child answers each checklist item; tutor scribes ticks.
**Ask:** "Now join three of those into one sentence about the music." → *listen for:* a multi-dimension sentence (e.g. "fast, loud, thick texture").

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK (audible + question)
**Ask:** "Describe this clip using at least **four** dimensions, one of them texture — and add a *because*."
- ✅ **Expected:** four+ dimensions named (incl. texture) plus a reasoned judgement. — probe: *"Why does the texture feel thick here?"* (many layers).
- ❌ **If gives one word ('it's exciting'):** small vocabulary → **fix:** "Use the checklist — give me a *because* with a music word: exciting *because* the pulse is…?"
- ❌ **If forgets texture:** new dimension not embedded → **fix:** "How *many* layers — thin or thick? Add that to your description."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child appraises a fresh short clip with the checklist, aiming for four dimensions including texture. Once secure, interleave: describe a clip from *memory* with no checklist in front of them.
**Observe / watch for:** stalling after one or two dimensions; "good/bad" with no reason.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't supply the words; 2) name *why* — relying on one word, or texture missing; 3) step back to **body/voice** — clap the pulse together so *tempo* is felt first, then add one clue at a time; 4) shrink to **two** dimensions, securely; 5) re-ask "and how many layers?" to add texture, then build up; 6) keep it safe — "Detectives gather one clue at a time. Let's add one more."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP + listening (notice→feel→judge)
> **Say:** "Name three dimensions you'd use to describe a song." → *listen for:* any three of tempo, dynamics, pitch, timbre, texture.
**Listen (20 sec):** **"Mambo"** in full — run **notice** (the five clues) → **feel** → **judge** with a *because*.
**Process praise:** "You gave a long, confident description with real music words — that's how a critic listens."
**Revisit later:** next unit — how whole pieces are *built*: verse, chorus and AB shapes.

- **Stretch (challenge):** describe with **five** dimensions unaided; compare two contrasting clips.

**Extension (if time remains):** write a one-paragraph review of the clip a friend could read.
**Assessment evidence:** child describes a clip using at least four dimensions including texture, with a reasoned judgement.

## Lesson 7 — Verse, chorus and AB
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Structure)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can hear and label verse/chorus and an AB structure in songs."

**You will need:** a clear verse-chorus pop/folk song; a simple **AB** instrumental clip; **A** and **B** cards (or just hands — one for A, two for B).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 singing — a chorus is the catchy bit that returns; a verse tells the story and changes.
**Key vocabulary:** **structure** (how a piece is built), **verse** (changes, tells the story = A), **chorus** (returns, the catchy bit = B), **AB**, **section**, **repeat**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** a song is a **built shape** — the **chorus returns** (the same), the **verse changes**. Pre-empt thinking the loudest/most familiar bit is "the song" — listen for **return vs change** to spot the sections.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Why does the catchy bit keep coming *back*? It's not an accident — someone *designed* the shape of the song. Today you'll see the hidden plan behind your favourite tunes."
**Show/Do:** play 10 sec of a verse, then the chorus, of the chosen song.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Which part of a song *repeats* — verse or chorus?" → *listen for:* "chorus."
*(If shaky: hum a well-known chorus — "this returns; the words-changing bit is the verse.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Music has **structure** — a built shape. The **verse** tells the story and the words *change* each time — call it **A**. The **chorus** is the catchy bit that *returns* the same — call it **B**. A song that goes verse, chorus, verse, chorus is a simple **A-B-A-B** shape. I listen for *change* (verse) versus *return* (chorus)."
**Show/Do:** play through one verse + chorus, holding up **A** then **B**.
**Emphasize:** "**Verse changes (A); chorus returns (B).**"

### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Now you hold up A or B with me. When the story-words change, show A. When the catchy bit comes back, show B."
**Do:** play the song; child raises A/B cards in real time with the tutor.
**Ask:** "How did you *know* that was the chorus?" → *listen for:* "it came back / repeated the same / it's the catchy bit."

### ⏱ 13–18 min · CHECK (audible + question)
**Ask:** "Listen to this AB instrumental — call out A and B as they happen, then tell me the difference between a verse and a chorus."
- ✅ **Expected:** correct A/B calls; "verse tells the story and changes; chorus repeats and is the catchy returning part." — probe: *"What's your clue for the chorus?"* (it returns).
- ❌ **If labels every new sound a new section:** over-splitting → **fix:** "A new *section* is a real change of music *and* it returns or contrasts — not just one new instrument."
- ❌ **If can't tell verse from chorus:** listening for words not shape → **fix:** "Ignore the words — does this *music* return later? If yes, it's the chorus (B)."

### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child maps the A/B order of a whole song (e.g. A-B-A-B). Once secure, interleave a second song with a different order.
**Observe / watch for:** raising A/B late; calling an instrumental break a verse.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't map it for them; 2) name *why* — listening to words not return/contrast; 3) step back to **voice** — *sing* the chorus together so the "returning" feel is in the body; 4) shrink the job — "just raise your hand on the chorus this time"; 5) re-add the verse label, then map the whole order; 6) keep it safe — "Start by spotting just the part that comes back."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What's the difference between a verse and a chorus?" → *listen for:* "verse changes/tells the story; chorus repeats and is the catchy returning part."
**Process praise:** "You heard the song as a *built shape*, not just sound — that's structure."
**Revisit later:** next, a three-part sandwich shape: A-B-A.

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** raise a hand on the **chorus only**; tutor signals verses.
- **Stretch (challenge):** map the **full A/B order** of a whole song and predict what comes next.

**Extension (if time remains):** find a song that *breaks* the simple pattern (e.g. has a bridge) and describe it.
**Assessment evidence:** child labels verse/chorus and an AB structure, justifying by return vs change.

## Lesson 8 — ABA (sandwich form)
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Structure)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise and perform an ABA structure (a contrasting middle that returns to the start)."

**You will need:** an **ABA** recording (e.g. a minuet & trio, or any clear A-B-A piece); recorders / percussion; a known recorder phrase for **A**.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L7 — sections, return vs change; from Year 3 — dynamics and tempo create contrast.
**Key vocabulary:** **ABA**, **section**, **contrast** (different — louder/faster/higher), **theme** (a musical idea), **return**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **ABA = idea, *contrasting* middle, then the idea returns**. The **B section must clearly contrast** (dynamics/tempo/pitch) and the **A must return the same**. Pre-empt a B that sounds the same as A — the contrast is the point.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Think of a sandwich — bread, a *different* filling, then the same bread again. Music does it too: a tune, a contrasting middle, then *home* to the first tune. We call it **A-B-A**."
**Show/Do:** play a known A phrase, then a contrasting B, then A again.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "In a song, which part returns the same?" → *listen for:* "the chorus / the repeating part."
*(If shaky: clap a pattern, a *different* one, then the first again — "that's A-B-A.")*

### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "**ABA** has three sections. **A** is my first idea. **B** is a *contrasting* middle — I'll make it different on purpose: quieter, slower, or higher. Then **A returns** — exactly like the start, so it feels like coming home. The magic is the *contrast* in the middle, and the *return* at the end."
**Show/Do:** play A (steady, mezzo), then B (clearly softer/slower or higher), then A again identical; label each aloud.
**Emphasize:** "**A — a contrasting B — then A comes home.**"

### ⏱ 10–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "We'll build one. A = our known phrase, together. Now B = make it *contrast* — let's play it softer and slower. Now back to A, just like the start. Hear the sandwich?"
**Do:** child performs A, then a contrasting B, then A again with the tutor.
**Ask:** "What did we change to make B contrast?" → *listen for:* "softer / slower / higher — a clear difference."

### ⏱ 17–23 min · CHECK (audible + question)
**Ask:** "Perform an ABA — make B *clearly* different — then tell me what ABA structure is."
- ✅ **Expected:** A, a contrasting B, A returns the same; "a first section, a contrasting middle, then the first section returns." — probe: *"How did you make B contrast?"*
- ❌ **If B sounds the same as A:** no contrast → **fix:** "B must *feel different* — change the dynamics or the speed. Make me hear the filling."
- ❌ **If A doesn't return / they invent a new ending:** missed the 'home' → **fix:** "The last A must match the first A *exactly* — play it like the start again."

### ⏱ 23–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child performs their ABA cleanly, B contrasting, A returning. Once secure, interleave: make B contrast in **two** ways (e.g. softer *and* slower).
**Observe / watch for:** B drifting into a copy of A; the final A wandering off.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't perform it for them; 2) name *why* — B not contrasting or A not returning; 3) step back to **body** — clap a loud A, a *quiet* B, a loud A so the contrast is felt with no notes; 4) shrink to just **A and B** (drop the return), make the contrast obvious; 5) add the return A back; 6) keep it safe — "Let's make A and a *very* different B first, then bring A home."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP + listening (notice→feel→judge)
> **Say:** "What is ABA structure?" → *listen for:* "a first section, a contrasting middle, then the first section returns."
**Listen (20 sec):** the ABA recording — **notice** the contrast in the middle; **feel** the "return home"; **judge** with a *because*.
**Process praise:** "You designed a clear *return home* — that takes real control of your playing."
**Revisit later:** next, what if the tune came back after *every* new idea? That's a rondo.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** play **A and B only**; tutor handles the return.
- **Stretch (challenge):** make B contrast in **dynamics, tempo *and* pitch** at once.

**Extension (if time remains):** compose your own short B section to fit between two A's.
**Assessment evidence:** child performs an ABA with a clearly contrasting B and an accurate returning A, and defines the form.

## Lesson 9 — Rondo (the theme keeps returning)
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Structure)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise a rondo (ABACA) where one theme keeps coming back."

**You will need:** a rondo recording (e.g. **Mozart, *Rondo alla Turca***); recorders/percussion; a simple shared **A** theme; optionally a **West African call-and-response** song clip.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L8 — ABA, return and contrast; the idea of a returning theme.
**Key vocabulary:** **rondo** (ABACA), **theme** (the main returning idea = A), **episode** (a different bit in between = B, C…), **return**, **improvise** (make up on the spot).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** in a **rondo, the A theme keeps coming back** between *different* episodes — A-B-A-C-A. Pre-empt confusing it with ABA (a rondo has **more than one** different episode, and A returns after **each**).

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Yesterday the tune came back *once*. But what if the catchy theme came back after *every* new idea? A… new bit… A… another new bit… A again. That's a **rondo** — A-B-A-C-A. Listen for the theme that keeps returning."
**Show/Do:** play the A theme of the rondo recording, then a snippet of an episode, then A again.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "In ABA, what comes back at the end?" → *listen for:* "the first section, A."
*(If shaky: clap A, clap a new pattern, clap A — "now imagine more new bits, A every time.")*

### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **rondo** is built on a **theme** — the **A** idea — that *keeps returning*. Between each return are **episodes**: a B bit, then later a C bit — each different. So the shape is **A-B-A-C-A**. The rule: whatever happens, A comes back. I listen for that one theme popping up again and again."
**Show/Do:** play/hum a simple A theme; map A-B-A-C-A on a strip, pointing as the recording's theme returns.
**Emphasize:** "**In a rondo, the A theme keeps coming back between different episodes.**"

### ⏱ 10–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's build our own rondo. We *both* play the A theme together. Then you improvise a short B episode — anything you like. Back to A together. Now a different C episode. Back to A!"
**Do:** tutor and child alternate the shared A theme with child-improvised B and C episodes.
**Ask:** "After your B episode, what came next?" → *listen for:* "A — the theme came back."

### ⏱ 17–23 min · CHECK (audible + question)
**Ask:** "Listen — raise your hand every time the **A theme** returns, then tell me what keeps coming back in a rondo."
- ✅ **Expected:** hand up on each A return; "the main theme (A), between different episodes." — probe: *"How many *different* episodes did you hear?"*
- ❌ **If calls it ABA:** heard only one return → **fix:** "Keep listening — does A come back *more than once*, after *different* bits? Then it's a rondo."
- ❌ **If raises hand on the episodes:** theme not fixed in ear → **fix:** "Hum the A theme to yourself; raise your hand only when *that* tune returns."

### ⏱ 23–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child performs the rondo with the tutor — A together, an improvised B, A, an improvised C, A. Once secure, interleave: make each episode *contrast* with the theme.
**Observe / watch for:** forgetting to return to A; episodes copying the theme.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't play it for them; 2) name *why* — A not returning, or episode = theme; 3) step back to **voice/body** — *sing* the A theme together so it's secure, then clap any 4 beats as the episode; 4) shrink to a single **A-B-A** first; 5) add one more episode to grow it to ABACA; 6) keep it safe — "Lock the A theme first; your episodes can be anything between the returns."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP + listening (notice→feel→judge)
> **Say:** "In a rondo, what keeps coming back?" → *listen for:* "the main theme (A), between different episodes."
**Listen (20 sec):** **Mozart *Rondo alla Turca*** *and/or* a **West African call-and-response** song — **notice** the returning theme / repeating response; **feel** it; **judge** with a *because*. "Different cultures, same idea — something keeps coming back."
**Process praise:** "You held the theme in your head and improvised real episodes between the returns — that's a composer's ear."
**Revisit later:** next unit — placing music in *time* by the instruments you hear.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** just play the **returning A theme** each time; tutor covers the episodes.
- **Stretch (challenge):** **improvise** a contrasting episode each time, varying dynamics/tempo/pitch.

**Extension (if time remains):** add a D episode (A-B-A-C-A-D-A) and keep the theme exact each return.
**Assessment evidence:** child recognises a rondo's returning theme by ear and performs an ABACA with A returning each time.

> **End-of-unit 3 check:** Child labels verse/chorus and AB, performs an ABA with clear contrast, and recognises a rondo's returning theme.

## Lesson 10 — Four eras by their sound
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 4 (Music history)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name four eras/genres and a typical sound for each."

**You will need:** four contrasting clips — **classical/orchestral** (e.g. Mozart or Beethoven), **jazz** (e.g. Duke Ellington), **pop/rock** (a clear guitar-band hit), **electronic/dance** (synths & beats); match cards (genre ↔ typical instrument).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 timbre — instrument families (strings, woodwind, brass, percussion); timbre = the *kind* of sound.
**Key vocabulary:** **genre** (a style/family of music), **era** (a time period), **classical/orchestral**, **jazz**, **pop/rock**, **electronic**, **timbre clues**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** you can often **guess the era from the instruments (timbre)** — no electric guitar/synth means it's *older*. Pre-empt "classical = old and boring" — classical is one *genre*, and it can be dramatic and exciting.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's a detective trick: you can often *guess how old music is* without being told — just by the instruments. No electric guitar? Probably old. Computer beats? Definitely modern. Let's become time-detectives."
**Show/Do:** play 5 sec each of the orchestral and the electronic clip back-to-back.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's timbre?" → *listen for:* "the *kind* / character of a sound (what makes instruments sound different)."
*(If shaky: a drum and a flute on the same note — different *timbre*.)*

### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **genre** is a style or family of music. Here's a simple map across time. **Classical / orchestral** — strings, woodwind, brass, no electric instruments; old. **Jazz** — swing, trumpets and saxophones, double bass; about 100 years ago. **Pop / rock** — a singer with electric guitar, bass and drum kit; the last 70 years. **Electronic** — synths, computers and beats; modern. The *instruments* are your clues."
**Show/Do:** play each clip in turn; name the genre and its tell-tale instrument; lay out a match card for each.
**Emphasize:** "**Match the genre to its typical instrument (timbre).**"

### ⏱ 10–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn. Listen — what instruments do you hear, and which genre is that? Match the card."
**Do:** play two of the clips; child names instruments, picks the genre, places the match card.
**Ask:** "What's your *evidence* for that genre?" → *listen for:* a named instrument ("electric guitar → pop/rock").

### ⏱ 17–23 min · CHECK (audible + question)
**Ask:** "Listen to this clip — name the genre, and tell me what a genre *is*." (Play the jazz clip.)
- ✅ **Expected:** "jazz" (with evidence — sax/trumpet/swing); "a genre is a style or family of music, e.g. jazz, pop, classical." — probe: *"Which instrument gave it away?"*
- ❌ **If guesses from how the song *feels* old, not the sound:** wrong evidence → **fix:** "Use the *instruments* as your clue, not a feeling — what do you actually *hear*?"
- ❌ **If 'classical = old and boring':** label confusion → **fix:** "Classical is one *style* — and it can be loud and dramatic. Listen again: boring, or exciting?"

### ⏱ 23–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child matches all four genres to their typical instruments using the cards and clips. Once secure, interleave: hear a *new* clip and place it into the right genre.
**Observe / watch for:** guessing without naming evidence; muddling jazz brass with classical brass (use swing + sax as the jazz tell).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't tell them the genre; 2) name *why* — not using instrument evidence; 3) step back to **timbre** — "name *one* instrument you hear first," then map that to a genre; 4) shrink to **two** very different clips (orchestra vs electronic); 5) add a third, then the fourth; 6) keep it safe — "Start with the easiest pair, then grow your map."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP + listening (notice→feel→judge)
> **Say:** "What is a genre of music? Name two." → *listen for:* "a style/family; e.g. jazz, pop, classical, electronic."
**Listen (20 sec):** one clip — run **notice** (instruments/timbre) → **feel** → **judge**, then name the genre with evidence.
**Process praise:** "You named the *evidence* for each genre, not just guessed — that's real listening."
**Revisit later:** next, we put them in a *time-line*, oldest to newest, and prove the order.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match **two** genres to their instruments (orchestra vs electronic).
- **Stretch (challenge):** match all **four**, and name a second tell-tale instrument for each.

**Extension (if time remains):** find a song you know and decide its genre with two pieces of instrument evidence.
**Assessment evidence:** child names four genres/eras and a typical instrument (timbre) for each.

## Lesson 11 — Order them oldest to newest
**Music · Year 4 · Unit 4 (Music history)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can order clips oldest → newest using the instruments (timbre) as evidence."

**You will need:** the four era clips from L10 — a **Baroque/Classical orchestral** piece, a **jazz** standard, a **pop/rock** hit, an **electronic/dance** track; a **time-line strip** (OLDEST → NEWEST) to lay clips along.

## End-of-year outcomes (what a Year 4 child can do)

By the end of these eleven lessons a child should be able to:

1. **Read pitch on the stave** — name line and space notes on the treble stave (EGBDF / FACE) and know higher = higher pitch.
2. **Play notated melodies** — read both pitch and rhythm from the stave and play a short tune (e.g. "Ode to Joy") on the recorder.
3. **Hear texture** — distinguish thin from thick textures and count layers.
4. **Play an ostinato** — keep a repeating pattern going as one layer under a melody.
5. **Describe with the elements** — appraise a clip using at least four dimensions including texture.
6. **Recognise structure** — identify and perform verse/chorus, AB, ABA, and recognise a rondo's returning theme.
7. **Place music in time** — name four eras/genres, match them to typical instruments, and order clips oldest → newest using timbre as evidence.
8. **Appraise across eras & cultures** — listen to a diverse range (Beethoven, Bernstein, Mozart, jazz, pop, electronic, West African call-and-response) and respond with notice → feel → judge, naming texture and structure.

---

### Closing note for the tutor

Year 4 is the year the page finally tells the child *both* clues — pitch *and* rhythm — and the year they learn to hear a whole piece as a **built shape**. Keep every lesson **active**: listen/imitate → practise → perform, and let the CHECK be something you can *hear* succeed, not just a spoken answer. When the child stumbles, always step back to **body and voice** and a **smaller pattern** before climbing back up — feeling the idea in the body comes before reading or performing it. Hold the year-long appraising thread (**notice → feel → judge**) across eras and cultures, and keep naming **texture** and **structure** alongside the older dimensions. Part-singing and composing with notation grow in **Year 5**; tutor-and-child duet performance and the full music-history story arrive in **Year 6**. Default to *best, not fast* — a child who can read a melody, hold an ostinato, build a rondo and place music in time has the foundations of a real musician.
