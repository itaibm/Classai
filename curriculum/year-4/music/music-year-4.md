# Year 4 Music (ages 9–10) — Scope & Sequence

> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Written to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Standards reference:
> `knowledge-base/subjects/art-music/music-ks1-ks2.md` and `extension-topics.md`.
> Codes: **UK NC KS2 Music** (perform, improvise and compose, listen with attention, use staff notation, appreciate a wide range of music) and **NCAS** (US National Core Arts Standards, Music, Grade 4: MU:Cr, MU:Pr, MU:Re, MU:Cn).

## Year overview

Year 4 music is the year children **play chords, sing in harmony and hear the world's music**. The instrument pathway moves from the recorder (Years 2–3) to the **ukulele**: every child learns to hold, tune, strum and change between the chords C, A minor, F and G7, then accompanies songs, including "La Bamba" (links to Spanish). Singing grows from unison into **rounds, partner songs and simple two-part harmony** with songs from Australia, South Africa, the Hebrew tradition and Latin. Notation extends to **time signatures (2/4, 3/4, 4/4 and a taste of 5/4), dotted rhythms, semiquavers and rests**. A unit of **world music** takes children to traditions they have not yet met — the steel pan of Trinidad and Tobago, flamenco from Andalusia, Chinese instruments, Arabic maqam and Mongolian throat singing (samba, West African drumming, raga, taiko and gamelan were covered in Years 2–3) — and the year ends with children **composing and performing a class piece**.

Every lesson includes **listening** to a named piece (with the composer or tradition, the place and the period), **singing or playing**, and **talking about music** using the elements: pitch, duration (rhythm), dynamics, tempo, timbre, texture and structure.

**What Year 4 assumes (from Years 2–3):** reading pitch on the treble stave from low C to high D, with F sharp and a key signature; semibreves, minims, crotchets, quavers and rests; ostinato, drone, ground bass and phasing; structures (verse–chorus, ABA, rondo); rounds (e.g. *Shalom Chaverim* in Year 2); world traditions already met (gamelan, samba, West African drumming, raga, taiko, Andean music, Aka polyphony); a first map of music history (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, 20th century).
**What Year 5 relies on:** chord playing and steady strumming (for ensemble work and keyboard chords); part-singing; secure rhythm notation in different time signatures; confident listening vocabulary for appraising genres; experience of composing in a structure.

**Instrument pathway across the school:** untuned percussion (Year 1) → recorder (Years 2–3) → **ukulele (Year 4)** → keyboard basics and ensembles (Years 5–6). Schools without ukuleles can substitute guitars (the same chord shapes on the top four strings are different — use a ukulele-style simplified guitar chart) or chime bars/keyboards for chords.

## Unit 1 — Singing in parts: rounds, partner songs and harmony

**Essential question:** How can voices singing different things at the same time make something beautiful?

**Key vocabulary:** unison, round, canon, partner song, harmony, part, blend, breath, posture, dynamics, call and response, a cappella

### Lesson 1 — Voice warm-up and singing in unison
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can sing in tune in unison with good posture, breathing and blend." (UK NC KS2 sing with accuracy and control; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.4)
- **Hook:** A recording of a children's choir singing a cappella: "How do they sound like one voice?"
- **Key activity:** Posture, breathing (sniff–hold–hiss), sirens and vocal slides; learn a unison song (e.g. "Kookaburra Sits in the Old Gum Tree", written by the Australian teacher Marion Sinclair in 1932); focus on starting and ending together.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does 'blend' mean, and how did you help the class blend?" → Voices sound like one; I listened to others and didn't sing louder than them.
- **Differentiation:** support: sing along with a strong leader. / stretch: lead a warm-up.
- **Materials:** recording of a children's choir, lyric sheets.
- **Joy:** Vocal "siren" warm-ups.

### Lesson 2 — Rounds: "Kookaburra"
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can hold my part in a two- and four-part round." (UK NC KS2 play and perform in ensemble; NCAS MU:Pr4.3.4)
- **Hook:** "What happens if we start the same song at different times?"
- **Key activity:** Sing "Kookaburra" as a round in two, then four groups; discuss how the lines fit (harmony appears).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why don't the parts clash when we sing the same tune at different times?" → The song is written so each line fits in harmony with the others.
- **Differentiation:** support: stand next to a confident singer. / stretch: sing the round in a small group of four (one per part).
- **Materials:** lyric sheets, recording.
- **Joy:** Four-part round performance.

### Lesson 3 — Major and minor: "Dona Nobis Pacem" and "Zum Gali Gali"
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can sing in parts in a major and a minor song and describe how the key changes the mood." (UK NC KS2 listen and appraise; NCAS MU:Re7.2.4)
- **Hook:** "Two songs, two moods — one in Latin, one in Hebrew. Can you hear which is major and which is minor?"
- **Key activity:** Learn "Dona Nobis Pacem" ("Grant us peace", a traditional Latin round in a major key) and "Zum Gali Gali" (a Hebrew work song from the 1930s, in a minor key), layering the repeated "zum gali gali" chant as an ostinato under the verse. Compare the moods; sing each in parts. (Year 2 already sang the Hebrew round "Shalom Chaverim".)
- **Check for understanding:** "One song is major and one is minor. Which is which, and how does the key change the mood even at the same speed?" → "Dona Nobis Pacem" is major and sounds calm and bright; "Zum Gali Gali" is minor and sounds darker and more serious — the key changes the colour of the tune, not its speed
- **Differentiation:** support: sing the "zum gali gali" ostinato only. / stretch: sing "Dona Nobis Pacem" in three parts.
- **Materials:** lyric sheets with translations, recordings.
- **Joy:** Candle-lit (LED) round in three parts.

### Lesson 4 — Partner songs
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can sing one song while another group sings a different song that fits with it." (UK NC KS2 ensemble; NCAS MU:Pr4.3.4)
- **Hook:** "Can two completely different songs fit together?"
- **Key activity:** Learn two partner songs (e.g. "When the Saints Go Marching In" and "This Train Is Bound for Glory", which share a chord pattern); sing together.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do these two songs fit together?" → They use the same chords at the same time, so the melodies harmonise.
- **Differentiation:** support: sing the simpler song. / stretch: switch parts halfway through.
- **Materials:** lyric sheets, backing track.
- **Joy:** Partner-song "battle" that becomes harmony.

### Lesson 5 — "Siyahamba": call and response and harmony
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can sing a South African freedom song in parts with call and response." (UK NC KS2 music from different traditions; NCAS MU:Cn11.0.4)
- **Hook:** Recording of "Siyahamba" ("We are marching in the light of God", a Zulu hymn sung during the struggle against apartheid — links to *Journey to Jo'burg*).
- **Key activity:** Learn call and response; add a simple harmony line; add movement (stepping in time).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is call and response, and why does it work well for a group?" → A leader sings and the group answers; it makes songs easy to learn and join in.
- **Differentiation:** support: response only. / stretch: sing the call as leader.
- **Materials:** recording, lyrics with translation.
- **Joy:** Singing and stepping together.

### Lesson 6 — Unit 1 performance and appraisal
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform part-songs and appraise our performance using musical vocabulary." (UK NC KS2 perform and appraise; NCAS MU:Pr5.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Mini-concert for another class."
- **Key activity:** Perform two part-songs; record; listen back and appraise (blend, tuning, dynamics).
- **Check for understanding:** "What was one strength and one thing to improve in our recording?" → Specific comments using vocabulary (e.g. "the parts stayed together, but the dynamics were all the same").
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence stems. / stretch: suggest a rehearsal plan.
- **Materials:** recording device.
- **Joy:** Mini-concert.

**End-of-unit check:** Hold a part in a round or partner song in a group of four; describe the performance using blend, dynamics and harmony.

## Unit 2 — Ukulele I: first chords and strumming

**Essential question:** How do chords make harmony — and how do I play them on the ukulele?

**Key vocabulary:** ukulele, string, fret, fretboard, neck, body, tuning (G C E A), chord, strum, down-strum, up-strum, chord box, C major, A minor, F major, steady beat, chord change

### Lesson 7 — Meet the ukulele
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can hold the ukulele, name its parts and strings, and strum open strings in time." (UK NC KS2 play musical instruments; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.4)
- **Hook:** A recording of Jake Shimabukuro (Hawaiian ukulele virtuoso): "Can four strings really sound like that?"
- **Key activity:** History (developed in Hawaii in the 1880s from small Portuguese instruments brought by immigrants from Madeira); hold, name parts, strings G–C–E–A ("Good Cats Eat Anchovies"); strum open strings to a steady beat.
- **Check for understanding:** "Name the strings from the top (nearest your face) to the bottom." → G, C, E, A.
- **Differentiation:** support: coloured stickers on strings. / stretch: tune a string by ear against a reference note.
- **Materials:** class set of ukuleles, tuner (clip-on or app), string mnemonic poster.
- **Joy:** First strums together.

### Lesson 8 — The C chord and strumming
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play the C chord and strum it steadily with down-strums." (UK NC KS2 play instruments with increasing accuracy; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.4)
- **Hook:** "One finger, one chord — and you can play a song!"
- **Key activity:** Read a chord box; C = ring finger on the 3rd fret of the A string; strum four down-strums per bar; play along with a one-chord song.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me on the chord box where your finger goes for C." → 3rd fret, A string (bottom string).
- **Differentiation:** support: fret stickers. / stretch: add an up-strum (down, down-up).
- **Materials:** ukuleles, chord-box cards.
- **Joy:** Play a whole song on one chord.

### Lesson 9 — A minor and changing chords
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play A minor and change between C and A minor in time." (UK NC KS2 play instruments; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.4)
- **Hook:** "Major sounds bright; minor sounds… how?"
- **Key activity:** A minor = middle finger on the 2nd fret of the G string; practise changes (4 beats each, then 2); listen to the change in mood.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does A minor sound different from C, and what does that do to a song's mood?" → Darker or sadder; it can make a song feel more serious.
- **Differentiation:** support: 8 beats per chord. / stretch: 2 beats per chord.
- **Materials:** ukuleles, chord cards.
- **Joy:** "Chord change challenge".

### Lesson 10 — F major
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play the F chord and change between C, A minor and F." (UK NC KS2 play instruments; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.4)
- **Hook:** "Three chords unlock hundreds of songs."
- **Key activity:** F = index finger on 1st fret E string + middle finger on 2nd fret G string; practise the pattern C–Am–F–C; notice the common finger between Am and F.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which finger stays in the same place when you change from A minor to F?" → The middle finger on the 2nd fret of the G string.
- **Differentiation:** support: C and F only. / stretch: all three with 2 beats each.
- **Materials:** ukuleles.
- **Joy:** Play a four-chord loop along to a backing track.

### Lesson 11 — Reading chord charts and strum patterns
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can follow a chord chart and play a strumming pattern." (UK NC KS2 use notation; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.4)
- **Hook:** "Chord charts are the secret language of bands."
- **Key activity:** Read chord charts with bar lines; strum pattern "down, down-up, down-up, down"; count aloud.
- **Check for understanding:** "How many beats does each chord last in this chart, and how do you know?" → Four; each bar in 4/4 has four beats and there is one chord per bar.
- **Differentiation:** support: all down-strums. / stretch: invent a strumming pattern.
- **Materials:** chord charts, ukuleles.
- **Joy:** "Be the band" with a chart.

### Lesson 12 — A song with three chords
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can accompany a class song with C, A minor and F." (UK NC KS2 perform; NCAS MU:Pr4.3.4)
- **Hook:** "Singers need a band — that's you!"
- **Key activity:** Half the class sings while half plays; swap; aim to keep chords changing in time with the words.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where in the song does the chord change, and how do you know?" → Marked above the word on the chart; they change on that syllable.
- **Differentiation:** support: play only on the first beat of each bar. / stretch: sing and play at the same time.
- **Materials:** song sheets with chords, ukuleles.
- **Joy:** Singing and playing together.

### Lesson 13 — Improvising with the C major scale
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can pick out the C major scale and improvise a short melody over chords." (UK NC KS2 improvise; NCAS MU:Cr1.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Can you make up a tune while your partner plays chords?"
- **Key activity:** Play C–D–E–F–G–A–B–C across the C, E and A strings (C and D on the C string, E, F and G on the E string, A, B and C on the A string) or on chime bars; partner strums C–Am–F; improvise 4-bar answers.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which note sounds 'finished' when the chord is C?" → C (the home note).
- **Differentiation:** support: use only C, E and G. / stretch: use rhythm variety in improvisations.
- **Materials:** ukuleles or chime bars, a scale chart.
- **Joy:** Question-and-answer improvisations.

### Lesson 14 — Unit 2 performance and assessment
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform a song with chord changes and appraise my playing." (UK NC KS2 perform and appraise; NCAS MU:Pr6.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Ukulele open mic."
- **Key activity:** Small groups perform a three-chord song; teacher observes chord accuracy and steady beat; self and peer appraisal.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which chord change is hardest for you, and how will you practise it?" → A specific change and a practice strategy (slow changes, finger 'ghost' moves).
- **Differentiation:** support: two chords only. / stretch: add a strumming pattern and dynamics.
- **Materials:** ukuleles.
- **Joy:** Open mic.

**End-of-unit check:** Play C, A minor and F with clear sound and change between them in time (4 beats each) while the group sings; explain the difference between major and minor in sound.

## Unit 3 — Rhythm and notation: time signatures and new note values

**Essential question:** How does written music tell us how long notes last and how the beats are grouped?

**Key vocabulary:** time signature, bar, bar line, beat, 2/4, 3/4, 4/4, 5/4, semibreve, minim, crotchet, quaver, semiquaver, dotted note, rest, tie, accent, waltz, march

### Lesson 15 — Note values: from semibreve to semiquaver
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read and clap semibreves, minims, crotchets, quavers and semiquavers." (UK NC KS2 staff notation; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.4)
- **Hook:** Beethoven's Symphony No. 5 opening (da-da-da-DUM): "Can you clap it — and write it?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 3's semibreves to quavers in two minutes; the new value is the semiquaver. Complete the note-value "family tree" (1 semibreve = 2 minims = 4 crotchets = 8 quavers = 16 semiquavers); clap and say rhythm names; read rhythm cards with semiquaver groups.
- **Check for understanding:** "How many semiquavers fit in one crotchet?" → Four (links to fractions: each is ¼ of a crotchet).
- **Differentiation:** support: rhythm words (ta, ti-ti, ti-ka-ti-ka). / stretch: write the rhythm of a short phrase from a song.
- **Materials:** rhythm cards, note-value tree poster, Beethoven recording.
- **Joy:** "Rhythm tree" relay.

### Lesson 16 — Rests and dotted notes
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read and perform rests and dotted rhythms." (UK NC KS2 staff notation; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.4)
- **Hook:** "Silence is part of music too!"
- **Key activity:** Rest symbols matched to note values; a dot adds half the note's value (dotted minim = 3 beats; dotted crotchet + quaver = a "skipping" rhythm); perform on percussion.
- **Check for understanding:** "How many beats does a dotted minim last, and why?" → Three: a minim is two beats, and the dot adds half of that (one more).
- **Differentiation:** support: rests only first. / stretch: dotted quaver–semiquaver patterns.
- **Materials:** rhythm cards, untuned percussion.
- **Joy:** "Silent beat" game — clap everything except the rests.

### Lesson 17 — Time signatures: marches and waltzes
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can hear and read the difference between 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4." (UK NC KS2 listen with attention to detail; NCAS MU:Re7.2.4)
- **Hook:** Listen to Johann Strauss II's "The Blue Danube" (a waltz, first performed in 1867) and John Philip Sousa's "The Stars and Stripes Forever" (a march): "Could you dance to both? How?"
- **Key activity:** Count and move: waltz 1-2-3, march with two strong beats (LEFT-right); the top number of a time signature shows beats per bar; the bottom number 4 means crotchet beats.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does 3/4 tell a musician?" → Three crotchet beats in each bar.
- **Differentiation:** support: conduct with the teacher. / stretch: conduct patterns for 2, 3 and 4 alone.
- **Materials:** recordings, space to move.
- **Joy:** Waltz and march dancing.

### Lesson 18 — Five beats in a bar: Holst's "Mars"
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can feel and describe music in an unusual time signature (5/4)." (UK NC KS2 appreciate a range of music; NCAS MU:Re8.1.4)
- **Hook:** Gustav Holst's "Mars, the Bringer of War" from *The Planets* (1914–16; links to Earth and space): "Count the beats — why does it feel uneasy?"
- **Key activity:** Clap the 5/4 ostinato (group as 3 + 2); discuss how the time signature creates tension; compare with another planet ("Jupiter").
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does 5/4 feel less comfortable to march to than 4/4?" → Our steps come in twos; five beats leave an odd one, so it feels off-balance and threatening.
- **Differentiation:** support: count along with the teacher. / stretch: compose a 5/4 ostinato for the class.
- **Materials:** recording of *The Planets*, drums.
- **Joy:** "Planet march" in 5/4.

### Lesson 19 — Writing rhythms: dictation and composition
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write down a rhythm I hear and compose a 4-bar rhythm in a chosen time signature." (UK NC KS2 use and understand notation; NCAS MU:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Rhythm detectives: can you capture a rhythm on paper?"
- **Key activity:** Rhythm dictation (teacher claps, children write); compose a 4-bar rhythm; check each bar adds up to the time signature.
- **Check for understanding:** "How can you check that each bar is correct?" → Add up the note values in each bar; they must match the time signature.
- **Differentiation:** support: rhythm cards to arrange. / stretch: include dotted rhythms and rests.
- **Materials:** manuscript paper, rhythm cards, percussion.
- **Joy:** Swap and perform a partner's rhythm.

### Lesson 20 — Unit 3 assessment: rhythm ensemble
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform my part in a layered rhythm piece from notation." (UK NC KS2 ensemble; notation; NCAS MU:Pr5.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Body-percussion orchestra."
- **Key activity:** Three layered rhythm parts from notation (e.g. crotchets, quavers, dotted rhythms); perform; swap parts.
- **Check for understanding:** "Read and clap your part alone. What makes it different from the others?" → Accurate clapping and a description of note values.
- **Differentiation:** support: simplest part. / stretch: the syncopated or semiquaver part.
- **Materials:** part cards, percussion.
- **Joy:** Body-percussion orchestra performance.

**End-of-unit check:** Clap from notation a 4-bar rhythm including a dotted note, a rest and semiquavers; identify 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 by ear and explain the time signature.

## Unit 4 — Music of the world II: steel pan, flamenco, China, the Arab world and Mongolia

**Essential question:** What can we learn about people and places from their music?

**Key vocabulary:** steel pan, tenor pan, bass pan, calypso, flamenco, palmas, compás, accent, erhu, pipa, guzheng, oud, maqam, quarter-tone, darbuka, khöömei (throat singing), overtone, morin khuur, drone, timbre

### Lesson 21 — Steel pan: music from oil drums
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how the steel pan was invented and play a calypso-style melody and bass pattern in parts." (UK NC KS2 music from different traditions; NCAS MU:Cn11.0.4)
- **Hook:** A video of a steel orchestra at Trinidad and Tobago's Panorama competition: "Every one of these instruments began as an oil drum."
- **Key activity:** The steel pan was developed in Trinidad in the 1930s and 1940s from biscuit tins and then oil drums; it is now Trinidad and Tobago's national instrument. The tenor pan plays the melody, bass pans the low notes. Children play a calypso rhythm on untuned percussion, a bass pattern on low chime bars and a simple melody on glockenspiels, then combine them.
- **Check for understanding:** "One steel pan can play many notes. How does its hammered surface make different pitches?" → the top is hammered into separate note areas; bigger areas vibrate more slowly and give lower notes, smaller areas give higher notes (links to Science: sound)
- **Differentiation:** support: the calypso rhythm or bass part. / stretch: the melody part with a syncopated rhythm.
- **Materials:** video, glockenspiels, chime bars, untuned percussion, photos of pans.
- **Joy:** "Class steel band" performance.

### Lesson 22 — Flamenco: palmas and the 12-beat compás
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can clap a 12-beat flamenco compás with the right accents and describe the parts of a flamenco performance." (UK NC KS2 music from different traditions; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.4)
- **Hook:** Video of flamenco guitar (e.g. Paco de Lucía), singing and dance from Andalusia: "The clapping is part of the music. Can you count it?"
- **Key activity:** Flamenco grew in Andalusia in southern Spain, with deep roots in Gitano (Roma) communities and many other influences. Learn *palmas* (sharp and muffled claps) and count the 12-beat *compás* used in styles such as *alegrías*, accenting beats 3, 6, 8, 10 and 12; layer guitar-style strums on ukulele and a simple dance step (links to Spanish).
- **Check for understanding:** "Clap the flamenco compás. Why does it feel different from counting in 4s?" → the accents fall on 3, 6, 8, 10 and 12 — groups of three and then twos — so the cycle doesn't divide into equal bars of four
- **Differentiation:** support: clap only the accented beats while the teacher counts. / stretch: switch between sharp and muffled palmas and keep the compás while others dance.
- **Materials:** video, ukuleles, accent cards 1–12.
- **Joy:** A flamenco *juerga* (gathering) with palmas and '¡olé!'.

### Lesson 23 — Chinese instruments: erhu, pipa and guzheng
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the timbre of three Chinese instruments and play a pentatonic Chinese folk melody." (UK NC KS2 music from different traditions; NCAS MU:Re7.2.4)
- **Hook:** Listen to "Erquan Yingyue" ("The Moon Reflected in the Second Spring"), played on the erhu by the blind street musician Abing: "How can two strings sound like a voice?"
- **Key activity:** Meet the erhu (two-string bowed fiddle), pipa (plucked lute) and guzheng (plucked zither); compare their timbres; learn "Mo Li Hua" ("Jasmine Flower"), a Chinese folk song, and play its opening on the pentatonic glockenspiel from Years 2–3.
- **Check for understanding:** "How can the erhu slide smoothly between notes in a way a glockenspiel cannot?" → the erhu has no frets or fixed bars — the player presses the strings freely and can glide the pitch; a glockenspiel only has separate fixed notes
- **Differentiation:** support: play the first phrase with note names. / stretch: add a slide on a voice or ukulele string to imitate the erhu and describe the effect.
- **Materials:** recordings, photos of instruments, glockenspiels (pentatonic set), lyric sheet.
- **Joy:** "Jasmine Flower" performance with a paper-fan dance.

### Lesson 24 — Arabic music: the oud, maqam and the darbuka
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the oud and maqam and play a darbuka-style rhythm in a group." (UK NC KS2 music from different traditions; NCAS MU:Re7.2.4)
- **Hook:** Listen to the Iraqi oud master Munir Bashir: "Some of these notes fall between the keys of a piano. How?"
- **Key activity:** The oud (a short-necked lute with no frets, ancestor of the European lute); *maqam*, the system of scales in Arabic music, some of which use 'quarter-tones' between the notes of a piano; the darbuka (goblet drum) with 'dum' (low, centre) and 'tek' (high, edge). Play the *maqsum* rhythm (dum-tek-tek-dum-tek) in parts on hand drums.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can't a piano or glockenspiel play every note of some maqams, but an oud can?" → some maqams use notes between the fixed notes of a piano (quarter-tones); the oud has no frets, so the player can place a finger anywhere on the string
- **Differentiation:** support: play only the 'dum' strokes. / stretch: improvise a short 'tek' fill at the end of every fourth cycle.
- **Materials:** recordings, hand drums or buckets, a photo of an oud.
- **Joy:** Drum circle with the maqsum groove.

### Lesson 25 — Mongolian throat singing and the horse-head fiddle
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a throat singer seems to sing two notes at once and try making overtones." (UK NC KS2 music from different traditions; NCAS MU:Re7.2.4)
- **Hook:** "One person, two notes at the same time — a deep hum and a high whistle. Impossible?"
- **Key activity:** Listen to Mongolian *khöömei* (throat singing, recognised by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage) and the *morin khuur* (horse-head fiddle); link the sounds to the wide grasslands and herding life. Children hum a steady low note and slowly change their mouth shape from 'oo' to 'ee' to hear faint whistling overtones.
- **Check for understanding:** "How can one throat singer seem to sing two notes at once?" → they hold a low, steady drone note and shape their mouth and tongue so that high 'whistling' overtones inside that sound become loud enough to hear as a second melody
- **Differentiation:** support: listen and describe the two layers with a picture prompt. / stretch: explain how the throat singer's drone links to the drones heard in Year 3 (bagpipes, tanpura).
- **Materials:** recordings, map of Mongolia, mirrors.
- **Joy:** 'Overtone choir' — the class hums and listens for the whistles.

### Lesson 26 — Comparing world music traditions
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare two traditions using the elements of music." (UK NC KS2 listen and appraise; NCAS MU:Re8.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Music Top Trumps" (a trumps-style game) with the five traditions.
- **Key activity:** Listening grid (instruments/timbre, texture, tempo, structure, purpose); compare two traditions in writing or speech.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one similarity and one difference between flamenco and Mongolian throat singing." → e.g. both come from particular communities and places and are passed on by ear; flamenco is built on a clapped 12-beat rhythm cycle, while throat singing is built on a held drone with overtones
- **Differentiation:** support: tick-box grid. / stretch: explain how each music's purpose shapes its sound.
- **Materials:** recordings, comparison grids.
- **Joy:** Music trumps game.

### Lesson 27 — World music celebration performance
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform in a world-music showcase and explain the tradition to an audience." (UK NC KS2 perform; NCAS MU:Pr6.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Carnival of the world."
- **Key activity:** Groups perform a steel-band calypso, flamenco palmas, "Jasmine Flower" or the maqsum groove, with a spoken introduction.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did you tell the audience about your tradition?" → Where it's from, instruments and purpose.
- **Differentiation:** support: introduction script. / stretch: lead the group.
- **Materials:** instruments.
- **Joy:** Showcase.

**End-of-unit check:** Perform one part in a steel-band, flamenco, Chinese or Arabic-style piece; describe two traditions using the elements of music (timbre, texture, structure, tempo).

## Unit 5 — Ukulele II: accompanying songs

**Essential question:** How can I use chords and strumming to accompany a song with style?

**Key vocabulary:** G7, chord progression, I–IV–V, strumming pattern, accompaniment, intro, outro, dynamics, tempo

### Lesson 28 — The G7 chord and the I–IV–V progression
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can play G7 and the progression C–F–G7–C." (UK NC KS2 play instruments; NCAS MU:Pr4.2.4)
- **Hook:** "Thousands of songs use the same three chords. Let's unlock them."
- **Key activity:** G7 = fingers on 2nd fret C string, 1st fret E string, 2nd fret A string; practise C–F–G7–C; hear how G7 "wants" to return to C.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does the G7 chord make you feel before C comes back?" → It sounds unfinished; it pulls back to C (home).
- **Differentiation:** support: C–F–C first. / stretch: change every 2 beats.
- **Materials:** ukuleles, chord cards.
- **Joy:** "Guess the song" with three chords.

### Lesson 29 — "La Bamba" (links to Spanish)
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can accompany "La Bamba" with C, F and G7 at a steady tempo." (UK NC KS2 perform; NCAS MU:Pr4.3.4)
- **Hook:** Revisit "La Bamba" from Spanish lessons — "Now we'll be the band!"
- **Key activity:** The repeating C–F–G7 pattern; practise at slow then faster tempo; sing and play.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is "La Bamba" a good song to play with only three chords?" → It repeats the same short chord pattern all the way through.
- **Differentiation:** support: play on the first beat only. / stretch: add a strumming pattern and a percussion part.
- **Materials:** ukuleles, lyrics with chords, percussion.
- **Joy:** Singing in Spanish while playing.

### Lesson 30 — Adding style: dynamics, intros and outros
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add an intro, outro and dynamic changes to a song accompaniment." (UK NC KS2 perform with control and expression; NCAS MU:Pr5.1.4)
- **Hook:** Listen to two versions of the same song: "What makes one more exciting?"
- **Key activity:** Plan an intro (e.g. 4 bars of chords), dynamics (quiet verse, loud chorus) and an ending; mark on the chart; rehearse.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where did you plan to change dynamics, and why there?" → A reasoned choice linked to the words or structure.
- **Differentiation:** support: teacher-planned arrangement. / stretch: groups arrange their own version.
- **Materials:** ukuleles, charts, pencils.
- **Joy:** Band arrangements.

### Lesson 31 — Unit 5 performance and assessment
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform an arranged song on ukulele with chord changes, dynamics and an ending." (UK NC KS2 perform; NCAS MU:Pr6.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Ukulele band concert."
- **Key activity:** Group performances; peer feedback; teacher observes chord accuracy, steady beat, expression.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did your group do to make the performance expressive?" → Dynamics, intro/outro, tempo choices.
- **Differentiation:** support: two-chord version. / stretch: solo verse.
- **Materials:** ukuleles, recording device.
- **Joy:** Concert.

**End-of-unit check:** Perform a song using C, F and G7 (and A minor where used) in time with an intro, dynamics and ending; explain why G7 leads back to C.

## Unit 6 — Compose, rehearse and perform

**Essential question:** How do composers turn ideas into a finished piece that others can play?

**Key vocabulary:** compose, motif, melody, chords, ostinato, structure (ABA, verse–chorus), texture, dynamics, notation, rehearse, score

### Lesson 32 — Planning a class composition
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a composition's structure, theme and layers." (UK NC KS2 compose; NCAS MU:Cr1.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Our piece: 'Journey Across the World' — a musical postcard from the places we've studied."
- **Key activity:** Decide structure (e.g. A: ukulele chords and melody; B: a flamenco-palmas or steel-band section; A again); plan layers (chords, melody, rhythm, ostinato).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the structure of our piece and why did we choose it?" → e.g. ABA — the return of A makes it feel complete.
- **Differentiation:** support: choose a role. / stretch: plan a section.
- **Materials:** planning sheets.
- **Joy:** Choosing the piece's theme and title.

### Lesson 33 — Composing melodies and layers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compose and notate a melody or rhythm layer for our piece." (UK NC KS2 compose; notation; NCAS MU:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Every layer needs a composer — that's you."
- **Key activity:** Groups compose layers (melody on chime bars over ukulele chords, rhythm ostinato, a palmas or maqsum break); notate with staff or graphic notation.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show your notation and explain how another group could play it." → Clear notation with note values/pitch.
- **Differentiation:** support: graphic notation and a pentatonic set. / stretch: staff notation with dynamics marks.
- **Materials:** instruments, manuscript paper.
- **Joy:** Hearing layers come together.

### Lesson 34 — Rehearsal
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can rehearse my part and respond to feedback to improve the ensemble." (UK NC KS2 ensemble; NCAS MU:Pr5.1.4)
- **Hook:** "What do professional musicians do in rehearsal?"
- **Key activity:** Section rehearsals then full rehearsal; record and appraise; fix one issue at a time (balance, tempo, entries).
- **Check for understanding:** "What did we fix in rehearsal and how?" → A specific problem and solution (e.g. the drums were too loud, so they played softer in verse A).
- **Differentiation:** support: assigned role and cue. / stretch: act as conductor.
- **Materials:** instruments, recording device.
- **Joy:** Conductor role.

### Lesson 35 — End-of-year concert and appraisal
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can perform our composition and part-songs for an audience and appraise our year of music." (UK NC KS2 perform and appraise; NCAS MU:Pr6.1.4)
- **Hook:** Families and other classes arrive.
- **Key activity:** Concert (a round, a world-music piece, a ukulele song, the class composition); written or spoken reflection.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which musical skill have you improved most this year, and how do you know?" → A specific, evidenced reflection.
- **Differentiation:** support: reflection frame. / stretch: host the concert.
- **Materials:** instruments, programme.
- **Joy:** The concert.

**End-of-unit check:** Perform a part in the class composition and explain its structure and one compositional choice; reflect on progress.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Singing in parts** — holds a part in a round, partner song or two-part harmony with good tuning. *See it:* four-part "Kookaburra" in a small group.
2. **Ukulele** — plays C, A minor, F and G7 cleanly and changes chords in time while others sing. *See it:* "La Bamba" performance.
3. **Notation** — reads and writes rhythms with semibreves to semiquavers, dotted notes and rests; understands 2/4, 3/4, 4/4. *See it:* rhythm dictation and composition.
4. **Listening and appraising** — describes named pieces and traditions using the elements of music. *See it:* the flamenco vs throat-singing comparison.
5. **World music** — performs a part in a steel-band, flamenco, Chinese or Arabic-style piece and explains the tradition. *See it:* world-music showcase.
6. **Improvising and composing** — improvises over chords or a drone and contributes a notated layer to a class composition. *See it:* composition score and performance.
7. **Performing** — performs with control, expression and awareness of the ensemble. *See it:* end-of-year concert.

## Teacher guidance

**Common misconceptions (and fixes).**
- *"Louder means faster."* — separate dynamics and tempo with games (play loud and slow, quiet and fast).
- *"A round is just singing badly out of time."* — show on a score how the parts fit.
- *"Pressing harder makes a better chord."* — press just behind the fret with fingertips; check each string rings.
- *"The top string must be the lowest note."* — on a standard ukulele the G string is tuned *higher* than C (re-entrant tuning); demonstrate.
- *"Music from other cultures is 'simpler'."* — flamenco compás, maqam and throat singing involve great skill and complex structures; name the traditions, places and musicians.
- *"Notes only come in piano steps."* — fretless instruments (erhu, oud) and voices can play pitches between the piano keys.
- *Time signatures as fractions* — the top number counts beats; the bottom number names the note value (4 = crotchet), not a fraction of a whole.

**Practice routine.** Five minutes of chord changes at the start of every ukulele lesson; slow changes first ("ghost" the shape before strumming); tune every instrument before playing.

**Cultural respect.** Introduce each tradition with its place, people and purpose (e.g. the steel pan's origins in Trinidad's working-class Afro-Trinidadian communities; flamenco's roots in Andalusia's Gitano communities; "Siyahamba" in South African churches and the anti-apartheid struggle).

**International notes.** Swap in songs from your children's home cultures; many rounds and partner songs exist in other languages. Hebrew-, French- or Spanish-speaking schools can link songs to the language programme.

**Vertical links.** Years 2–3: stave reading to low C–high D with F sharp, recorder, texture, structure, world traditions (gamelan, samba, djembe, raga, taiko, Andean, Aka), music history. Year 4: part-singing, ukulele chords, time signatures and semiquavers, new world traditions, composing in a structure. Year 5: keyboard basics and composing a film soundtrack; it should treat rounds, ukulele chords and the world traditions of Years 2–4 as retrieval.

**Cross-curricular links.** Science (*The Planets*; sound and vibration), languages ("La Bamba"; flamenco and Spanish), English (*Journey to Jo'burg* and "Siyahamba"; poetry and rhythm), maths (note values as fractions; counting in time signatures), geography (Trinidad and Tobago, Andalusia, China, the Arab world, Mongolia), PE (dance and movement to waltz, march and samba).
