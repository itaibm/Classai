# Year 4 Life Skills — Tutor Scripts (Lessons 1–21)

Complete tutor scripts for [Year 4 Life Skills](../life-skills-year-4.md), built to the [scripting guide](../../../SCRIPTING-GUIDE.md) and grounded in `knowledge-base/subjects/computing-life-skills/life-skills.md`. The tone is **warm and never shaming** — worry, pressure, mistakes and money muddles are all normal, and the message is always *you can think for yourself, stand firm kindly, and ask for help.* Lessons are talk + story + role-play. Every beat ⏱ sums to the duration, with verbatim Say / Ask, the response to listen for, the mandatory "if the child is stuck" support (kept emotionally safe — simplify, re-ask warmly, never shame), differentiation and assessment evidence. The "check" is usually a reflective question, not a single right answer.

Most lessons run 30 minutes; longer ones (35–40) are projects. Twenty-one lessons across seven units.

# Unit 1 — Worry, stress & resilience (emotional wellbeing)

---

## Lesson 1 — What worry feels like and does
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Emotional wellbeing)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe what worry feels like and that everyone gets it sometimes."
**You will need:** body-map ("gingerbread") sheets, crayons, a picture of a smoke alarm, a slice of (pretend) burnt toast.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 — feelings are normal and they pass; naming a feeling.
**Key vocabulary:** **worry**, **stress**, **body signs**, **mind signs**, **protective**, **over-fires**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** worry is **normal and protective** — it's the brain trying to keep you safe. It has **body signs** and **mind signs**. Sometimes it **over-fires** when there's no real danger. Nobody is "weird" for worrying. The repeatable image: *worry is a smoke alarm.*
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "A smoke alarm is brilliant when there's a real fire — but really annoying when it screams just because you burnt the toast! Worry is a bit like that smoke alarm in your brain. Useful for real danger… loud over little things."
**Show:** the smoke-alarm picture and the burnt toast.  **Do:** mime the alarm shrieking, then waving smoke away from toast.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Last year we said all feelings are okay and they pass. Is worry a feeling?" → *listen for:* "yes — it's a feeling everyone gets."
*(If shaky: "Happy, sad, scared… worry is one too — and it comes and goes like the others.")*
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Worry has **body signs** and **mind signs**. In my **body** I might feel a tight chest, a wobbly tummy, a fast heart, or trouble sleeping. In my **mind** I get racing thoughts that go round and round — 'what if, what if.' Here's the important bit: worry isn't a fault. It's your brain trying to **keep you safe** — like the smoke alarm. The trouble is it sometimes **over-fires** when there's no real danger, like the alarm going off over toast."
**Show / Do:** on the body map, point to and label chest, tummy, heart, head as you name each sign.
**Emphasize:** "Worry is **normal and protective** — everyone gets it. It just sometimes goes off over toast."
### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's map it together. Think of a time you felt a bit worried — a test, a new place, the dark. Where did you feel it in your body? Let's colour those spots on the map."
**Do:** child colours one or two body signs; tutor adds a "racing thoughts" cloud over the head.
**Ask:** "Name one body sign and one mind sign of worry." → *listen for:* body (tight chest / wobbly tummy / fast heart) + mind (racing thoughts / can't stop thinking about it).
### ⏱ 17–23 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "Is it normal to feel worried sometimes — and why does worry even happen?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "Yes — everyone worries; it's the brain trying to keep me safe." — then probe: *"When is worry helpful, and when is it just going off over toast?"*
- ❌ **If "only scared/weak people worry":** that's the shame myth → **fix:** "Even the bravest people worry — astronauts, footballers, your grown-ups. It's a built-in safety system, not a weakness."
- ❌ **If "worry is bad / I should never feel it":** → **fix:** "Worry isn't bad — it's a warning. We just learn to check: real fire, or burnt toast?"
### ⏱ 23–29 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child finishes their worry body-map and names one common worry *without shame* ("Lots of children worry about ___"). Once secure, interleave: decide if that worry is a "real fire" or "burnt toast."
**Watch for:** a child who thinks their worry is silly or unique — normalise it.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't push, keep it warm; 2) name *why* — they may not have words for body signs; 3) shrink it — "where did you feel it last time you were nervous, your tummy or your chest?"; 4) offer a sentence starter: "When I worry, my body…"; 5) re-ask the smaller version; 6) keep it safe — "There's no wrong answer here. Everyone's worry feels a little different."
### ⏱ 29–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me in your own words — why is worry like a smoke alarm?" → *listen for:* "it's there to keep me safe but sometimes goes off when there's no real danger."
**Process praise:** "You described exactly what worry feels like *and* worked out it's normal — that takes real honesty."
**Revisit later:** tomorrow we sort worries into ones we *can* and *can't* do something about.
---
**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** point to worry signs on a ready-made body map and name them.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain when worry is *helpful* (real danger) versus *over-firing* (no real danger), with an example of each.

**Extension (if time allows):** draw their own "worry smoke alarm" and label one "real fire" and one "burnt toast" worry.
**Assessment evidence:** child describes a body sign and a mind sign of worry and states that worry is normal and protective.

## Lesson 2 — What I can and can't control
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Emotional wellbeing)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can sort worries into what I can and can't control."
**You will need:** a large "circle of control" sheet (a ring drawn on paper), blank worry slips, a pen, a coat or umbrella prop.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L1 — worry is normal and has body/mind signs.
**Key vocabulary:** **control circle**, **inside the circle**, **outside the circle**, **action plan**, **let go**, **share**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** some worries are **inside my circle of control** (I can *do* something — make a plan) and some are **outside** (I can't change them — let them go or share them). Spending energy on what you *can* control is the calm move. Image: *you can't control the weather, but you can grab a coat.*
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "You can't control the weather — no matter how hard you wish, you can't stop the rain. But you *can* grab a coat or an umbrella. So we don't waste energy crying at the clouds — we do the bit that's *ours* to do."
**Show:** the coat/umbrella.  **Do:** mime worrying at the sky, then shrug and put the coat on.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What did we learn worry is for?" → *listen for:* "keeping us safe / it's normal."
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Here's a **circle of control**. **Inside** the circle go the worries I can actually *do* something about — like 'I'm worried about my spellings,' because I can *practise* them. **Outside** the circle go worries I can't change — like the weather, or whether it rains on sports day. For outside ones, I **let them go** or I **share** them with someone I trust. For inside ones, I make a tiny **action plan**."
**Show / Do:** write "spellings test" and place it *inside* the ring; write "will it rain?" and place it *outside*.
**Emphasize:** "Put your energy **inside the circle** — the part that's yours to do."
### ⏱ 11–18 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's sort together. I'll read a worry; you tell me — inside or outside the circle? 'I'm worried I'll forget my lines in the play.' … 'I'm worried it'll snow on my birthday.'"
**Do:** child places each slip inside or outside the ring and says why.
**Ask:** "What can you *do* with a worry that's *outside* your control?" → *listen for:* "let it go, or share it with a trusted person, and focus on what I *can* control."
### ⏱ 18–24 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "Here's a worry: 'I'm scared the big dog next door might bark at me on the way to the park.' Which part is inside your circle, and which is outside?"
- ✅ **Expected:** outside = whether the dog barks; inside = I can walk on the other side / tell a grown-up / take a different route. — probe: *"So what would you actually do?"*
- ❌ **If "nothing I can do":** worry feels all-powerful → **fix:** "Almost always there's a *tiny* bit that's yours. Is there a step you could take, even a small one?"
- ❌ **If tries to control the uncontrollable** ("I'll make the dog be quiet"): → **fix:** "Can you really change the dog? What *can* you change — your route, telling someone?"
### ⏱ 24–29 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child writes (or says) two of their own worries, sorts them inside/outside, and turns ONE inside-the-circle worry into a tiny action plan ("First I'll… then I'll…"). Once secure, interleave: for an outside worry, name who they'd share it with.
**Watch for:** putting everything "outside" to avoid having to act — gently find the controllable sliver.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it safe; 2) name *why* — sorting is the tricky bit; 3) give two clear worries to sort first (weather vs spellings); 4) sentence starter: "I can't change ___, but I *can* ___"; 5) re-ask with the simpler pair; 6) reassure — "Even grown-ups have to practise letting the outside ones go."
### ⏱ 29–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Say it back to me — what do you do with the worries you *can* control, and the ones you *can't*?" → *listen for:* "make a plan for the ones I can; let go or share the ones I can't."
**Process praise:** "You sorted those worries like a pro and made a real plan — that's putting your energy where it counts."
**Revisit later:** next, we build a toolkit for bouncing back from a hard day.
---
**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** sort just two given worries (weather vs spellings) into inside/outside.
- **Stretch (challenge):** take an inside-the-circle worry and build a clear step-by-step action plan.

**Extension (if time allows):** design a poster: "Inside my circle / Outside my circle" with three examples each.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly sorts worries by control and makes one small action plan for a controllable worry.

## Lesson 3 — My resilience toolkit
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Emotional wellbeing)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name strategies that help me bounce back from a hard day."
**You will need:** a bouncy ball, resilience-strategy picture cards (talk to someone, move your body, do something you enjoy, helpful self-talk, rest, ask for help), a "bounce-back card" template, crayons.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L2 — focus energy on what you can control.
**Key vocabulary:** **resilience**, **bounce back**, **strategy**, **helpful self-talk**, **support**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **resilience** is bouncing back after a hard day — and it's a *skill you can build*, not luck. You have a **toolkit** of strategies; pick the ones that work for you. Image: *the bouncy ball that hits the floor and springs back.*
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Watch this." *(bounce the ball)* "It hits the floor… and springs straight back up. People can do that too after a hard day — it's called **bouncing back**. The big question is: what *helps* a person bounce back?"
**Do:** bounce the ball a few times, then catch it and hold it low. "What helps *this* bounce? And what helps *you*?"
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If a worry is outside your control, what can you do?" → *listen for:* "let it go or share it."
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Bouncing back is called **resilience** — and it's a skill you *build*, like a muscle. Here's my **resilience toolkit**: **talk to someone**, **move my body**, **do something I enjoy**, **helpful self-talk** ('this is hard, but I can get through it'), **rest**, and **ask for help**. On a hard day I reach into the toolkit and pick a tool — different days, different tools."
**Show / Do:** lay out the six strategy cards; demonstrate "helpful self-talk" by saying a kind sentence to yourself out loud.
**Emphasize:** "Resilience is a **skill you can build** — you've got a whole toolkit."
### ⏱ 11–18 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's test the toolkit. Pretend you've had a rotten day — you fell out with a friend and got your spellings wrong. Which two tools from these cards might help you bounce back?"
**Do:** child picks two cards and says how each would help.
**Ask:** "Name two things that help you bounce back after a hard day." → *listen for:* any two of: talk to someone, move, do something you enjoy, helpful self-talk, rest, ask for help.
### ⏱ 18–24 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "Imagine you tried really hard at something and it still went wrong. Pick a tool from your toolkit — and tell me *why* it would help."
- ✅ **Expected:** names a strategy + a reason ("I'd talk to my mum because saying it out loud makes it smaller"). — probe: *"Which tool is your number one?"*
- ❌ **If "just feel bad / nothing helps":** → **fix:** "On the hardest days even *one* tiny tool helps a little. Which feels easiest — a hug, a walk, or telling someone?"
- ❌ **If picks 'pretend it didn't happen':** that's avoiding, not bouncing → **fix:** "Hiding it usually keeps it stuck. What could you *do* that helps it shrink — move, talk, rest?"
### ⏱ 24–29 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child chooses their **top three** strategies and makes a personal "bounce-back card" they can keep. Once secure, interleave: match a specific tough situation to the best tool for it.
**Watch for:** choosing tools they'd never actually use — nudge toward realistic favourites.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it warm; 2) name *why* — too many choices; 3) narrow to three picture cards; 4) prompt: "On a bad day, do you like to be busy, quiet, or with someone?"; 5) pick one to start; 6) reassure — "Your toolkit, your choices. You can add more any time."
### ⏱ 29–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Show me your bounce-back card and tell me your number-one tool." → *listen for:* a named strategy from their card.
**Process praise:** "You built a toolkit that's truly *yours* — now you'll always have it on a hard day."
**Revisit later:** end-of-unit check; these tools come back when we talk about setbacks in Unit 3.
---
**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** pick three strategies from picture cards and copy them onto the card.
- **Stretch (challenge):** match each strategy to a specific tough situation where it fits best.

**Extension (if time allows):** add a seventh tool of their own invention to the toolkit and explain it.
**Assessment evidence:** child names at least two resilience strategies and selects a top three for their bounce-back card.

> **End-of-unit check (Unit 1):** Child describes worry and that it's normal, sorts worries by control, and names resilience strategies to bounce back.

## Lesson 4 — When friends pressure you
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Friendship & peer pressure)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise peer pressure and name a way to say no kindly but firmly."
**You will need:** peer-pressure scenario cards (small, safe-in-the-room dares), "say no" strategy cards (say no clearly / give a reason / suggest something else / walk away / find a different friend).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 — real friends are kind; calming down before reacting.
**Key vocabulary:** **peer pressure**, **dare**, **stand firm**, **say no kindly**, **suggest something else**, **walk away**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **peer pressure** is when others push you to do something you're not sure about — and **real friends respect a "no."** You can say no *kindly but firmly* using strategies. Saying no doesn't make you unkind or boring. Repeatable line: *"No thanks — let's do something else."*
---
### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "'Everyone's doing it — go on!' Have you ever felt that little push to do something when part of you wasn't sure? That push has a name: **peer pressure**. And today we learn how to stand firm — *kindly*."
**Do:** lean in with a friendly-but-pushy "go ooon, everyone's doing it!" then step back.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does a *real* friend do — push you, or respect you?" → *listen for:* "respect you / be kind."
### ⏱ 5–13 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "**Peer pressure** is when friends — or a group — push you to do something. Sometimes it's fine ('come and join in!'). But sometimes it's something you know is wrong or feels bad. Here's the truth: a **real friend respects your no.** And here are my **ways to say no kindly but firmly**: **say no clearly** ('No thanks'), **give a reason** ('I don't want to get in trouble'), **suggest something else** ('Let's do this instead'), **walk away**, or **find a different friend.** Watch me." *(model:)* "'No thanks — I don't want to. Let's go play football instead.'"
**Show / Do:** lay out the five "say no" cards; demonstrate two of them on a pretend dare.
**Emphasize:** "Real friends **respect a no** — and you can say it **kindly *and* firmly.**"
### ⏱ 13–21 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's practise together. I'll be the friend pushing you: 'Go on, take that last biscuit, no one will know!' Pick a 'say no' card and try it on me."
**Do:** child chooses a strategy and says no; tutor responds warmly.
**Ask:** "A friend dares you to do something you know is wrong. What can you do?" → *listen for:* "say no clearly, give a reason, suggest something else, or walk away."
### ⏱ 21–28 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "Your friend says 'If you were a *real* friend, you'd do it.' Is that true — and what could you say back?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "That's not true — a real friend wouldn't make me do something wrong; I can still say no kindly." — probe: *"Which strategy would you use?"*
- ❌ **If "I'd have to do it or lose the friend":** the fear of exclusion → **fix:** "A friend who only likes you when you obey isn't being a true friend. What would a *kind, firm* answer sound like?"
- ❌ **If "I'd be mean back / shout":** → **fix:** "We can be **firm without being unkind**. How could you say no *and* stay kind?"
### ⏱ 28–34 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child role-plays 2–3 scenario cards (small, safe dares), choosing a different "say no" strategy each time. Once secure, interleave: handle a friend who **keeps pushing** ("say it again, then walk away / find someone else").
**Watch for:** mumbling or giving in when pushed twice — coach the firm-but-kind tone.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it safe, never shaming; 2) name *why* — saying no out loud is hard; 3) pick ONE phrase to master ("No thanks, let's do something else"); 4) say it together first; 5) re-run the easiest scenario; 6) reassure — "It takes courage to say no. You're practising a real superpower."
### ⏱ 34–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me one way to say no kindly but firmly." → *listen for:* a named strategy.
**Process praise:** "You stood firm *and* stayed kind — that's exactly the brave-but-warm thing real friends do."
**Revisit later:** next, what to do when you *see* someone else being treated unkindly.
---
**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** practise one "say no" phrase really well.
- **Stretch (challenge):** handle a friend who keeps pushing after the first no.

**Extension (if time allows):** write a short comic strip where a character uses two different "say no" strategies.
**Assessment evidence:** child recognises peer pressure and demonstrates at least one kind-but-firm refusal.

## Lesson 5 — Upstander, not bystander
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Friendship & peer pressure)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose a safe way to help when I see someone being treated unkindly."
**You will need:** bystander/upstander scenario cards, two simple labels: "BYSTANDER" and "UPSTANDER."
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L4 — standing firm kindly; empathy (caring how others feel).
**Key vocabulary:** **bystander**, **upstander**, **exclude**, **include**, **speak up**, **get help**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** a **bystander** just watches; an **upstander** does something **safe** to help. You don't have to be a hero or put yourself in danger — small safe actions count: include them, speak up kindly, get help, comfort them after. Repeatable: *"Don't just watch — do one safe thing."*
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Picture this: someone in the playground is being left out and teased, and a crowd of children just… stand and watch. Does watching help that person? What could someone do instead?"
**Do:** mime standing frozen, watching, hands in pockets.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If a friend pushed you to be mean to someone, what would you do?" → *listen for:* "say no / not join in."
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "There are two roles here. A **bystander** sees something unkind happening and just watches — maybe they feel bad, but they don't act. An **upstander** does **one safe thing** to help. Safe upstander moves: **include** the person ('Come play with us'), **speak up kindly** ('Hey, that's not nice — leave them alone'), **get a trusted adult**, or **comfort them afterwards** ('Are you okay? I saw what happened'). You don't have to be a hero — you just do one *safe* thing."
**Show / Do:** hold up the BYSTANDER label, then swap it for UPSTANDER as you describe the switch.
**Emphasize:** "Be an **upstander** — do one **safe** thing, not nothing."
### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Here's a scene: a new child at the park is being left out of a game. You're a bystander watching. Let's turn you into an upstander — what's one *safe* thing you could do?"
**Do:** child chooses and acts a safe upstander action; tutor plays the left-out child.
**Ask:** "What's one safe thing an upstander can do when someone's being treated badly?" → *listen for:* include them, ask the person to stop kindly, get a trusted adult, or comfort them after.
### ⏱ 17–23 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "Some people being unkind are bigger or in a group, and stepping right in wouldn't be safe. What's an upstander move that *keeps you safe* too?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "Get a trusted adult" or "include/comfort the person afterwards." — probe: *"Why is getting an adult an upstander move, not telling tales?"*
- ❌ **If "fight them / shout back":** unsafe → **fix:** "That could put *you* in danger. What's a safe way to help — who could you get?"
- ❌ **If "do nothing, it's not my problem":** → **fix:** "Remember the person being left out — if it were you, what would you wish someone did? Even one small safe thing helps."
### ⏱ 23–29 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child role-plays turning a bystander moment into an upstander one, on 2 scenario cards. Once secure, interleave: pick the **safest** action for a trickier situation and explain *why* it's the safest.
**Watch for:** confusing "getting help" with "telling tales" — clarify it's about keeping someone safe.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it warm; 2) name *why* — it's about choosing *safe* help; 3) offer two clear options (include them / get an adult); 4) sentence starter: "I could safely…"; 5) re-ask with the simpler scene; 6) reassure — "Even noticing and caring is the first step. You don't have to fix everything alone."
### ⏱ 29–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What's the difference between a bystander and an upstander?" → *listen for:* "a bystander just watches; an upstander does one safe thing to help."
**Process praise:** "You found safe ways to stand up for someone — that's real courage *and* kindness together."
**Revisit later:** next, being kind in a place where no one can see your face — online.
---
**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** choose an upstander action from picture cards.
- **Stretch (challenge):** pick the *safest* action for a tricky situation and explain why.

**Extension (if time allows):** design an "Upstander, not bystander" poster with three safe actions.
**Assessment evidence:** child names and demonstrates at least one safe upstander action.

## Lesson 6 — Being kind online
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Friendship & peer pressure)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how to be kind and think before posting online."
**You will need:** sample messages (some neutral, some grumpy/ambiguous), a "Think before you post: Is it Kind? True? Necessary?" poster, a "kindness translator" worksheet.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L5 — kindness and empathy; how words affect people.
**Key vocabulary:** **online**, **post**, **tone**, **kind / true / necessary**, **taken the wrong way**, **lasts**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** online words **feel real and last** — and screens **hide your tone** (no smile, no voice), so a joke can be read the wrong way. Before sending, ask: **Is it kind? Is it true? Is it necessary?** Repeatable: *"Words on a screen can't smile."*
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "When you say something cheeky to a friend's *face*, they see your smile and your eyes — they know you're joking. But **words on a screen can't smile or hug.** So how might a little joke get read the wrong way online?"
**Do:** say "you're SO weird" once with a big grin, then again flat and cold. "Same words — very different feeling."
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "How do we tell how someone feels in real life?" → *listen for:* "their face, voice, body."
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Online, your **tone** is hidden — no face, no voice. So a message can hurt even when you didn't mean it to. And online words **last** — they can be saved, shared, screenshotted. So before I post or send, I run a quick check: **Is it Kind? Is it True? Is it Necessary?** If a message fails one of those — I change it or don't send it."
**Show / Do:** point to each word on the "Kind / True / Necessary" poster.
**Emphasize:** "Before you post: **Kind? True? Necessary?** — and remember, words on a screen can't smile."
### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Here are two messages a child might send. Let's run them through our three questions — which one passes, and how could we fix the other?"
**Do:** read a kind message and a grumpy/ambiguous one; child checks each against Kind/True/Necessary.
**Ask:** "Before you send a message online, what three things should you ask yourself?" → *listen for:* "Is it kind, true and necessary? (and: could it be taken the wrong way?)"
### ⏱ 17–23 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "A friend posts a drawing they're proud of. You think it's a bit messy. What could you write — and what should you NOT write?"
- ✅ **Expected:** something kind and true ("I love the colours!"); not "that's messy." — probe: *"Which of our three questions stopped the unkind one?"*
- ❌ **If "just say it's messy — it's true!":** true but not kind/necessary → **fix:** "It might be true, but is it *kind* and *necessary*? Could you find something true *and* kind to say?"
- ❌ **If "say nothing ever to be safe":** → **fix:** "Kind words online are great! The check is for the *unkind* ones — what kind thing could you post?"
### ⏱ 23–29 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** "Kindness translator" — child rewrites an unkind or ambiguous message into a kind, clear one. Once secure, interleave: add *why* the original might have been "taken the wrong way."
**Watch for:** rewriting that's sarcastic or back-handed — keep it genuinely kind.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it warm; 2) name *why* — rewriting kindly is a skill; 3) shrink it — "just change the *one* unkind word"; 4) sentence starter: "Instead of that, I could say…"; 5) re-ask on the easier message; 6) reassure — "Even grown-ups have to think twice before sending. That pause is the smart bit."
### ⏱ 29–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Say our three-question check before posting." → *listen for:* "Is it kind, true, necessary?"

## Lesson 7 — Failure is feedback
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Growth mindset & resilience)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can find the lesson hidden inside a mistake or failure."
**You will need:** famous-failure cards (e.g. the light-bulb's many tries, a footballer who missed shots, an author rejected many times), a "this taught me… / next I'll try…" reflection sheet.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 — mistakes help the brain grow; the word "yet."
**Key vocabulary:** **failure**, **feedback**, **learn from**, **mistake**, **next step**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **failure is feedback** — it shows you what to try next, *not* proof you can't do it. The most successful people failed many times first. Reframe the question from "Am I bad at this?" to **"What can this mistake teach me?"** Never shaming.
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Did you know the light bulb took *hundreds* of tries before it worked? That top footballers have *missed* thousands of shots? That famous authors were told 'no' over and over before a single book got printed? What do all those amazing things have in common?"
**Do:** flick through the famous-failure cards quickly.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Last year we learned a magic word for 'I can't do it' — what was it?" → *listen for:* "yet — 'I can't do it *yet*.'"
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Here's the big idea: **failure is feedback.** When something goes wrong, it isn't a stamp saying 'you're rubbish.' It's *information* — it shows you what to fix and what to try next. The inventor of the light bulb said each failed try just told him one more way that *didn't* work — getting him closer. So instead of asking 'Am I bad at this?' I ask: **'What can this mistake teach me?'**"
**Show / Do:** on the board, write "FAILURE → feedback → next try" as an arrow.
**Emphasize:** "Failure isn't the end — it's **feedback** that points to the next step."
### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's try it on a real one. Say you got lots of spellings wrong on a test. The *fixed* thought is 'I'm bad at spelling.' Let's flip it — what *feedback* does that test give you?"
**Do:** child names the feedback ("I muddle these particular words / I need to practise the 'tion' ones").
**Ask:** "What's a growth-mindset way to think about a failure?" → *listen for:* "as feedback that shows what to try next — not proof I can't do it."
### ⏱ 17–23 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "A child says 'I lost the race, I'm just slow, I give up.' What feedback could that race actually be giving them?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "It shows what to practise — maybe pace, or training more; one race isn't proof." — probe: *"So what would they try next?"*
- ❌ **If "it just means they're slow":** fixed mindset → **fix:** "Is one race forever proof? What did the footballer who missed thousands of shots do next?"
- ❌ **If "they should feel bad":** shame → **fix:** "Feeling a bit sad is okay — but the question is, what does this teach them for *next* time?"
### ⏱ 23–29 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child picks a **recent setback of their own** and completes "This taught me… / Next I'll try…" on the sheet. Once secure, interleave: turn that into a concrete next-step plan.
**Watch for:** self-blame ("I'm just dumb") — gently redirect to the *lesson* in it.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it safe — setbacks can sting; 2) name *why* — it's hard to look back at a flop; 3) shrink it to a tiny example (a wobbly bike, a tricky level); 4) sentence starter: "That showed me I need to…"; 5) re-ask with the small example; 6) reassure — "Everyone — *everyone* — fails first. It's how learning works."
### ⏱ 29–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Finish this: 'Failure isn't proof I can't — it's…'" → *listen for:* "feedback / a lesson / what to try next."
**Process praise:** "You found the lesson hidden inside a mistake — that's exactly how the light-bulb inventor thought."
**Revisit later:** next, what keeps you going through the hard *middle* of a tough task.

## Lesson 8 — Perseverance through the hard middle
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Growth mindset & resilience)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name what keeps me going when I want to quit."

**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** the **middle** of any hard task is the part where you most want to quit — and that's *normal*. **Perseverance** means having "keep-going fuel" ready. ---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Every hard thing has a **messy middle** — the bit where it's too far in to be new and exciting, but not yet finished. **Show / Do:** lay out the "keep-going fuel" cards.
**Emphasize:** "The hard **middle** is normal — that's when you reach for your **keep-going fuel.**"
### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)

### ⏱ 17–23 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "You're halfway through a hard piece of homework and you really want to stop. What's your keep-going fuel — and why does *the middle* make you want to quit?"

- ❌ **If "just stop, it's too hard":** → **fix:** "Quitting in the middle means all your effort so far goes to waste. What's *one* small chunk you could finish first?"

### ⏱ 23–29 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child names a **current challenge** of their own, picks their keep-going fuel, and designs a "don't quit" mini-poster. Once secure, interleave: make a plan to push through that real challenge ("when I want to quit, I'll…").

### ⏱ 29–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Why is the *middle* the part where people quit — and what gets you through it?" → *listen for:* "it's the hardest bit; my keep-going fuel."

**Revisit later:** next, looking back to spot what to improve — like a coach reviewing the match.

## Lesson 9 — Reflecting to improve
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Growth mindset & resilience)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can reflect on what went well and what to change next time."
**You will need:** a reflection template (What went well? / What was tricky? / What will I do differently?), a recent piece of the child's work or a challenge to review.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L7 failure-as-feedback; L8 perseverance.
**Key vocabulary:** **reflect**, **review**, **what went well**, **what was tricky**, **improve**, **next time**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **reflecting** = "watching back" your own work like a sports team reviews a match, to notice what worked and what to improve. It's a friendly look, **not** a telling-off. Three questions: *What went well? What was tricky? What will I do differently?*
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "After a big match, sports teams sit down and *watch it back* — not to feel bad, but to spot what worked and what to tweak so they play better next time. Here's a question: could *you* 'watch back' your own work the same way?"

**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What did we say a mistake really is?" → *listen for:* "feedback / a lesson."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "**Reflecting** means looking back kindly to learn. I use three questions: **What went well?** (always start here — find the wins). **What was tricky?** **What will I do differently next time?** Watch me reflect on a drawing I made: 'What went well — the colours are bright. What was tricky — I rushed the edges. Next time — I'll go slower at the end.' Notice I didn't call myself names; I just spotted one thing to change."
**Show / Do:** model the three answers aloud on a sample piece.
**Emphasize:** "Reflecting is a **kind look back** — wins first, then one thing to improve."
### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Now let's reflect together on this piece of your work. Start with the good bit — what went *well*?"
**Do:** child answers all three questions with prompts; tutor records them.

### ⏱ 14–19 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "Some people skip the reflecting and just rush to the next thing. What might they *miss* by not looking back?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "They'd miss what they could improve / they'd make the same slip again." — probe: *"So what's the *one* thing you'd change for next time?"*

### ⏱ 19–25 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child reflects on a recent piece of work or challenge using the template and sets **one** improvement for next time. Once secure, interleave: turn that improvement into a specific goal ("Next time I will…").
**Watch for:** vague answers ("it was fine") — prompt for one concrete win and one concrete tweak.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it warm; 2) name *why* — looking back at own work feels exposing; 3) start only with "what went well"; 4) sentence starters for all three; 5) re-ask the easiest question first; 6) reassure — "Finding *one* thing to improve isn't a fault — it's a superpower the best people use."
### ⏱ 25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Say my three reflection questions." → *listen for:* "What went well? What was tricky? What will I do differently?"
**Process praise:** "You coached yourself like a pro — found a win *and* one thing to improve. That's how growth happens."
**Revisit later:** end-of-unit check; use these three questions after any big task all year.
---
**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** answer the three questions using sentence-starter prompts.
- **Stretch (challenge):** turn the reflection into a specific, measurable next-time goal.

**Extension (if time allows):** make a "My coach's three questions" bookmark to keep in their workbook.
**Assessment evidence:** child reflects with the three questions and sets one concrete improvement.

## Lesson 17 — Don't get tricked into buying

## Lesson 19 — Can I trust this source?
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 7 (Critical thinking & citizenship)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can ask whether a piece of information can be trusted."
**You will need:** sample claim cards (a silly claim like "chocolate makes you fly!", a sensible one), "source" cards (a known science website, a random anonymous post, a playground rumour), a "trust check" card.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 — facts vs opinions; asking good questions.
**Key vocabulary:** **source**, **trustworthy**, **evidence**, **check**, **who said it**, **how do they know**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** before believing something, **check the source**: *Who said it? How would they know? Can I check it somewhere reliable?* Not everything online or from a friend is true. Image: *"I read online that chocolate makes you fly!"*
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "A friend rushes up and says, 'I read online that chocolate makes you FLY!' Should you believe it? And — more interestingly — how could you *check*?"
**Show:** the "chocolate makes you fly" claim card.  **Do:** mime flapping arms, then stop and look doubtful.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's the difference between a fact and an opinion?" → *listen for:* "a fact can be checked; an opinion is what someone thinks."
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Before I believe something, I do a **trust check** on the **source** — that means *where the information came from.* Three questions: **Who said it?** (a scientist, or a random person?) **How would they know?** (did they test it, or just guess?) **Can I check it somewhere reliable?** (a trusted website, a book, an expert). 'Chocolate makes you fly' fails all three — no real source, no way they'd know, can't check it anywhere sensible. So I don't believe it."
**Show / Do:** hold up the "trust check" card with the three questions; lay out the three "source" cards.
**Emphasize:** "**Who said it? How would they know? Can I check it?** — that's the trust check."
### ⏱ 11–18 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's judge a claim together: 'Spiders have eight legs.' Now — which source would you *trust* most to check it: a science website, a random post with no name, or a playground rumour? Why?"
**Do:** child ranks the sources and explains, then runs the trust check.
**Ask:** "Name one question to ask before believing something you read online." → *listen for:* "Who said it? How do they know? Can I check it somewhere reliable?"
### ⏱ 18–24 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "Lots of people online are sharing a 'fact' that turns out to be made up. Does *lots of people sharing it* make it true?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "No — it could still be wrong; I'd check who said it and whether a reliable source backs it up." — probe: *"How exactly would you check it?"*
- ❌ **If "yes, if everyone says it, it's true":** popularity ≠ proof → **fix:** "Lots of people once believed the Earth was flat! Does many people saying it make it a *fact*? How would you *check*?"
- ❌ **If "believe it because it's online":** → **fix:** "Anyone can post anything online. *Who* posted it, and how would they *know*?"
### ⏱ 24–29 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child judges a set of claims with matched sources and decides what to trust and how to verify. Once secure, interleave: explain *how* they'd check a doubtful claim.
**Watch for:** believing the silliest claim because it's exciting — bring it back to the source.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it warm; 2) name *why* — judging sources is new; 3) compare just two sources (science website vs no-name post); 4) prompt: "Which one would actually *know*?"; 5) re-ask the simpler pair; 6) reassure — "Even grown-ups get fooled online. Asking 'who says?' makes you a sharp thinker."
### ⏱ 29–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Say one trust-check question." → *listen for:* "Who said it? / How do they know? / Can I check it?"
**Process praise:** "You myth-busted that silly claim by checking the source — that's exactly how a smart thinker decides what to believe."
**Revisit later:** next, the rights you have and the responsibilities that come with them.
---
**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** pick the more trustworthy of two sources.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain how they'd verify a doubtful claim.

**Extension (if time allows):** make a "Trust Check" bookmark with the three questions.
**Assessment evidence:** child asks at least one trust-check question and judges a source's reliability.

## Lesson 20 — My rights and responsibilities
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 7 (Critical thinking & citizenship)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name a right children have and a responsibility that goes with it."
**You will need:** rights cards (to be safe, to learn, to play, to be heard) and matching responsibility cards (help others be safe, let others learn, share and take turns, listen so others are heard).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 — fair rules; being heard / listening.
**Key vocabulary:** **right**, **responsibility**, **fair**, **everyone**, **goes together**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** a **right** is something *every* child should have (to be safe, to learn, to play, to be heard). Each right comes with a **responsibility** — to help *others* have the same right. Rights and responsibilities **go together.** Repeatable: *"My right is your right too."*
---
### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "You have a **right** to learn and to be safe — every child does. But here's a thinking puzzle: what **responsibility** comes with that — for *you*, and for *everyone else*?"
**Show:** a "right to learn" card.  **Do:** hold it up, then add a "?" card beside it.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What makes a rule *fair*?" → *listen for:* "it's the same for everyone / it's kind / it keeps people safe."
### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **right** is something *every* child should have, just for being a child: to be **safe**, to **learn**, to **play**, and to be **heard**. But rights only work if we *all* respect them. So every right has a matching **responsibility** — my job to help *others* have it too. I have a right to be heard — so my responsibility is to **listen** so others can be heard. They **go together**, like a pair."
**Show / Do:** put a right card and its responsibility card side by side as a matched pair.
**Emphasize:** "Rights and responsibilities **go together** — my right is your right too."
### ⏱ 11–18 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's match a pair. Here's the right 'to play.' Which responsibility goes with it — and why?"
**Do:** child matches "right to play" with "share and take turns so everyone can play," and explains.
**Ask:** "You have a right to be heard. What's a matching responsibility?" → *listen for:* "to listen to others so they can be heard too."
### ⏱ 18–24 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "Imagine someone says, 'I have a right to play, so I'll grab all the toys for myself!' What have they forgotten?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "The *responsibility* — others have the same right, so they need to share so everyone can play." — probe: *"Why don't rights work if we only take and never give?"*
- ❌ **If "they're right, it's their right!":** rights-without-responsibility → **fix:** "If *everyone* grabbed all the toys, could *anyone* play? What's the responsibility that makes the right work for all?"
- ❌ **If "they should have no rights then":** → **fix:** "Everyone keeps their rights — we just each have a *responsibility* too. What's theirs here?"
### ⏱ 24–29 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child matches rights to responsibilities across the card set. Once secure, interleave: explain *why* rights and responsibilities go together.
**Watch for:** seeing rights as "things I get" with no give-back — surface the matching responsibility.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it warm; 2) name *why* — pairing them is abstract; 3) match just one clear pair (be heard / listen); 4) prompt: "If you want others to do this for *you*, what should you do for *them*?"; 5) re-ask the simple pair; 6) reassure — "This is big citizenship thinking — you're getting it."
### ⏱ 29–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Name a right and the responsibility that goes with it." → *listen for:* a matched pair (e.g. "to learn → let others learn too").
**Process praise:** "You matched rights to responsibilities and saw they go together — that's how a fair community works."
**Revisit later:** next, putting citizenship into action with a community project.
---
**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match one right to its responsibility.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain why rights and responsibilities go together.

**Extension (if time allows):** invent a new right-and-responsibility pair for their home.
**Assessment evidence:** child names a right and a matching responsibility and explains they go together.

## Lesson 21 — Taking community action
**Life Skills · Year 4 · Unit 7 (Critical thinking & citizenship)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a small action that makes my community or world better."
**You will need:** community-action idea cards (litter pick, kindness campaign, save energy at home, food-bank collection, welcome someone new to the street or club), a simple planning sheet (What? / Who helps? / How we get others to join).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** L20 — rights and responsibilities; upstander actions (L5).
**Key vocabulary:** **community**, **action**, **contribute**, **add up**, **recruit**, **together**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **small group actions add up** — many people each doing a little makes a big difference and *encourages others*. You can make your **community** (family, street, neighbourhood, clubs) better. The plan: *What action? Who helps? How do we get others to join?* Image: *one person picking up litter… now imagine a whole team.*
---
### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "If *one* person picks up litter in the park, it helps a little. But what if that person got ten friends to join in? Or their whole club? Suddenly the whole park is clean! Small actions **add up.**"
**Show:** the community-action idea cards.  **Do:** mime picking up one piece of litter, then gesture to a whole "team" doing it.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Last lesson — what's a responsibility that comes with living together?" → *listen for:* "help others / keep things fair / look after each other."
### ⏱ 5–13 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Your **community** is the people around you — your family, your street, your neighbourhood, your clubs. You don't have to be a grown-up to make it better. The secret is that **small actions add up**, and they **encourage others** to join in too. A litter-pick with family and friends, a kindness campaign, saving energy by switching off lights at home, collecting tins for a food bank. To plan one, I ask three things: **What's the action? Who can help? How do we get others to join?**"
**Show / Do:** lay out the idea cards; show the planning sheet with its three boxes.
**Emphasize:** "**Small actions add up** — especially when you **get others to join in.**"
### ⏱ 13–21 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's plan one together. Say we choose a 'kindness campaign.' What exactly would we *do*? Who could help? And how would we get others to join in?"
**Do:** child fills in the three boxes for a chosen action with the tutor.
**Ask:** "Why can a *group* action help the community more than one person alone?" → *listen for:* "more people doing a small thing adds up to a bigger difference and encourages others to join."
### ⏱ 21–28 min · CHECK (reflective)
**Ask:** "Someone says 'I'm just one kid — I can't change anything.' Using what we learned, what would you say back?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "One person *can* start something, and if others join, small actions add up to a big difference." — probe: *"So how would you get others to join you?"*
- ❌ **If "yeah, one kid can't do anything":** → **fix:** "Remember the litter — one person *started* it, then a team made it big. What could *you* start that others would join?"
- ❌ **If picks something impossible** (e.g. "fix the whole world today"): → **fix:** "Let's make it *small and real* first. What's one little action you could actually do this week?"
### ⏱ 28–34 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child plans **one realistic community action** on the planning sheet — what it is, who helps, how to recruit others. Once secure, interleave: add the *steps in order* and a way to invite more helpers.
**Watch for:** plans that are too big or vague — anchor to one small, doable action.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) keep it warm; 2) name *why* — planning a whole project is a lot; 3) pick an action from the idea cards; 4) prompt: "What's one small thing that would make your street or neighbourhood nicer?"; 5) fill just the 'What?' box first; 6) reassure — "Every big change started with one small action by someone like you."
### ⏱ 34–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Why does a *team* action beat one person alone?" → *listen for:* "small actions add up; more people make a bigger difference and encourage others."
**Process praise:** "You planned a real action *and* a way to get others on board — that's how communities get better. You're a true upstander for your whole community."
**Revisit later:** end-of-unit and end-of-year — actually *do* the action, then reflect on it with the three questions from Lesson 9.
---
**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** choose an action from the idea cards.
- **Stretch (challenge):** plan the steps in order and how to recruit helpers.

**Extension (if time allows):** design a poster or short "join us!" invitation to recruit others to the action.
**Assessment evidence:** child plans a small, realistic community action and explains why group action helps more.

> **End-of-unit check (Unit 7):** Child questions whether a source is trustworthy, matches a right to its responsibility, and plans a small community action.

## End-of-year outcomes

By the end of Year 4, a child should reliably show:

1. **Emotional resilience** — recognises worry as normal, sorts what they can and can't control, and uses bounce-back strategies.
2. **Social courage** — recognises and resists peer pressure kindly, acts as an upstander, and is thoughtful and kind online.
3. **Growth from setbacks** — treats failure as feedback, perseveres through the hard middle, and reflects to improve.
4. **Balanced wellbeing** — links food and screens to mood and energy, balances screen time, and uses a mindful reset.
5. **Online safety** — protects private information, knows online people may not be who they claim and never to meet, and tells a trusted adult about anything upsetting.
6. **Money smarts** — judges best value, resists sales tricks and impulse buys, and explains where household money goes with needs first.
7. **Trustworthy thinking & citizenship** — questions whether a source is reliable, links rights to responsibilities, and plans a community action.
8. **Independent, kind character** — thinks for themselves, stands firm with kindness, and helps others and the wider world.

## Closing note

Year 4 Life Skills is where the child steps out into a wider social world — and these scripts hold one steady promise underneath every lesson: *you are not bad for worrying, for failing, for feeling pushed, or for not knowing.* Worry is a smoke alarm. Failure is feedback. A "no" can be kind. A trusted adult is always there, and it's never your fault. Teach these warmly, lean on the role-play and the props, and let the reflective checks be conversations, not tests. The aim isn't a child who never struggles — it's a child who, when life gets harder, knows they can think for themselves, stand firm with kindness, bounce back, and ask for help. Best, not fast.
