## Lesson 11 — Simile and metaphor (comparing for effect)
**English · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Reading: summarising & writer's effect)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify a simile and a metaphor and explain their effect."

**You will need:** figurative phrase cards (*as quiet as a mouse / as cold as ice / her smile was as bright as the sun* [similes]; *the kitchen was a zoo / the sky was a black blanket / he was a lion in the match* [metaphors]); two columns labelled SIMILE (like/as) and METAPHOR (it IS); an effect sentence stem card ("…which makes me picture/feel…").
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 4 inference; adjectives; the idea that writers choose words on purpose.
**Key vocabulary:** *simile* (compares using *like* or *as*), *metaphor* (says one thing IS another), *effect* (the feeling or picture it creates), *signal words* (*like, as*).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Both compare — but a **simile** uses *like/as*, a **metaphor** says it *is*. The KB demand: don't just LABEL the technique — say the EFFECT ("…which makes me picture/feel…"). Pre-empt the classic mix-up by hunting for the signal words *like/as*.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Two ways to describe a noisy kitchen at dinnertime. One: *as loud as a zoo.* Two: *the kitchen was a zoo.* Both paint a picture in your head — but they're built differently. Writers use these comparison tools to make you SEE and FEEL things. Today you'll spot both and say WHY they work."

**Show:** the two phrases on the board.  **Do:** read both, miming a chaotic zoo.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Why do writers choose powerful words instead of plain ones?" → *listen for:* to make you picture/feel something / on purpose for effect.
*(If shaky: contrast "a big dog" with "a towering, snarling beast" — same dog, different feeling.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Tool one: a **simile** compares two things using the signal words *like* or *as.* *Her smile was as bright as the sun.* I look for *like* or *as* — there it is, *as… as* — so it's a simile. The effect: it makes me picture a huge, warm, glowing smile. Tool two: a **metaphor** doesn't compare — it says one thing IS another. *The kitchen was a zoo.* It doesn't say *like* a zoo, it says it WAS a zoo. The effect: I picture total wild chaos. My rule: signal words *like/as* → simile; says it IS something → metaphor. And I never stop at the label — I always add the effect."

**Show / Do:** circle *as… as* in the simile; underline *was* in the metaphor; write the effect under each using the stem "…which makes me picture/feel…".
**Emphasize:** "*Like/as* = simile. Says it IS = metaphor. Always add the effect."

### ⏱ 9–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Sort two with me. *As cold as ice* — any signal words? (*as… as* → simile) What's the effect?" (→ makes me feel freezing/unfriendly) "*The sky was a black blanket* — does it say *like* or does it say it IS? (says it IS → metaphor) Effect?" (→ makes me picture a heavy, dark, smothering sky)

**Do:** child places each card in the SIMILE or METAPHOR column, pointing to the evidence (signal word or "is"), then says the effect with the stem.
**Ask:** "How do you KNOW *as cold as ice* is a simile?" → *listen for:* it uses the signal word *as*.

### ⏱ 15–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Is *as cold as ice* a simile or a metaphor — and how do you know?"
- ✅ **Expected:** Simile, because it uses *as*. Then probe: *"Now give me the effect — what does it make you feel?"*
- ❌ **If "metaphor":** confused the two because both compare. → **fix:** "look for the signal words. Do you see *like* or *as*? Yes — *as*. Signal words mean simile."
- ❌ **If they name it but can't give the effect:** stopped at the label. → **fix:** "use the stem — *…which makes me picture/feel…*. So *as cold as ice* makes you feel what?"

### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child sorts six phrases into simile/metaphor (pointing to the evidence each time) and writes the effect of ONE using the stem "…which makes the reader picture/feel…".
**Watch for:** sorting by guess instead of signal words; labelling without any effect; calling *like a…* a metaphor.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't tell them; 2) name *why* — "let's hunt for the signal words first"; 3) step down — cover everything but check for *like/as*; if present, simile; 4) ONE hint — "does it use *like/as*, or does it say it IS?"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just find the signal word in this one"; 6) keep it safe — "Both compare, so they're easy to mix — the signal words are your clue."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the difference between a simile and a metaphor — and the one thing you must always add after naming it." → *listen for:* simile uses *like/as*, metaphor says it IS; always add the effect.
**Process praise:** "You used the signal words as evidence AND explained the effect every time — that's reading like a literary critic."
**Revisit later:** Lesson 12 adds personification and word choice; figurative language returns in Unit 4 writing (Lessons 13–15) and the Unit 3 check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** a signal-word reminder card (*like/as = simile*); sort the clear similes first.
- **Stretch (challenge):** write one original simile and one original metaphor about the same thing (e.g. the sea), and explain each effect.

**Extension (if time allows):** find a simile or metaphor in a poem or song lyric and explain its effect.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly sorts at least four phrases into simile/metaphor (citing the evidence) and explains the effect of one in a full sentence.

## Lesson 12 — Personification & word choice (why that word?)
**English · Year 4 · Unit 3** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can spot personification and explain why a writer chose a powerful word."

**You will need:** descriptive passages with personification (*the old house glared at us / the wind whispered / the thunder grumbled / the leaves danced / the moon smiled down*); word-choice pairs (*glared vs looked; stomped vs walked; whispered vs said*); technique cards (simile / metaphor / personification); the effect stem card.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Lesson 11 — simile and metaphor; always add the effect.
**Key vocabulary:** *personification* (giving non-living things human actions/feelings), *word choice* (the writer's deliberate pick of a word), *effect*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **Personification** gives a non-living thing a human action or feeling (*the house glared*, *the leaves danced*). And every powerful word is a CHOICE — *stomped* vs *walked* changes how we feel. The KB demand again: name the technique AND explain the effect with "…which makes the reader feel/picture…". Pre-empt stopping at the label.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Listen: *The old house glared at us with its broken windows.* A house can't really glare — only a person can. But doesn't that one word make the house feel alive… and creepy? Writers do this on purpose. Today you'll spot it and explain WHY a writer picks one word over another."

**Show:** the house sentence, *glared* underlined.  **Do:** make a glaring face at the "house".
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge — Lesson 11):** "Name the two comparison tools from yesterday and what makes each one." → *listen for:* simile (*like/as*), metaphor (says it IS).
*(If shaky: one quick example of each before adding the new technique.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "New tool: **personification.** It gives a non-living thing a HUMAN action or feeling. *The house glared* — glaring is something a person does. *The wind whispered. The leaves danced. The thunder grumbled.* All things that can't really do those — but the writer makes them seem alive. Now, word choice. Compare *the man walked in* with *the man stomped in.* *Stomped* tells me he's heavy and angry — that's a powerful CHOICE. So I ask two questions: is a non-living thing doing something human? (personification) And why THAT word instead of a plain one? (the effect)."

**Show / Do:** underline the human verb in each personification example; put *walked* and *stomped* side by side and write the effect of each.
**Emphasize:** "Personification = a human action for a non-living thing. Then always ask: why THAT word — what's the effect?"

### ⏱ 9–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together. *The moon smiled down.* Can the moon smile? (no — that's a human action → personification). Effect?" (→ makes the night feel gentle/friendly) "Now word choice: would you write *the dog walked over* or *the dog crept over* for a sneaky dog? Why?" (→ *crept* shows it's sneaky/quiet)

**Do:** child names the technique for two examples and chooses the more powerful word in a pair, justifying with the effect stem.
**Ask:** "How do you know *the moon smiled* is personification?" → *listen for:* smiling is a human action given to the moon.

### ⏱ 15–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name the technique: *the moon smiled down.* And why might a writer choose *stomped* instead of *walked*?"
- ✅ **Expected:** Personification; *stomped* shows anger/heaviness. Then probe: *"…which makes the reader feel what?"*
- ❌ **If they call *the moon smiled* a metaphor/simile:** confused with the comparison tools. → **fix:** "is it comparing two things with *like/as* or *is*? No — it's giving the moon a HUMAN action. That's personification."
- ❌ **If "stomped just means walked":** missed the word-choice effect. → **fix:** "act it out — walk, then stomp. How does the stomper FEEL? *Stomped* shows anger; that's why the writer chose it."

### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child reads three short descriptive lines, names the technique (focusing on personification, with simile/metaphor mixed in), and explains the effect of one powerful word choice using "…which makes the reader feel/picture…".
**Watch for:** labelling without effect; confusing personification with metaphor; missing the human verb.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "let's check: is a non-living thing doing something a person does?"; 3) step down — underline the verb and ask "can only a person do that?"; 4) ONE hint — "use the effect stem to finish"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just tell me what the house is DOING"; 6) keep it safe — "Spotting WHY a word works takes practice — let's read it again together."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What is personification, and what must you always add after naming any technique?" → *listen for:* a human action/feeling given to a non-living thing; the effect.
**Process praise:** "You spotted the human actions AND explained why each word was chosen — you're reading like a writer now, noticing the choices behind the words."
**Revisit later:** feeds directly into describing settings (Lesson 13); appears in the Unit 3 check and the Lesson 19 word-choice question.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the effect sentence stem provided; a list of "human action" verbs to spot (*glared, whispered, danced*).
- **Stretch (challenge):** rewrite a flat sentence (*the door opened*) using personification, then explain the effect.

**Extension (if time allows):** hunt a spooky story for the words that make it scary and explain one writer's choice.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly names personification at least once and explains the effect of a deliberate word choice in a full sentence.

---

> **End-of-Unit 3 check (tutor-led, ~10 min):** Child writes a one-sentence main idea for a paragraph and an overall summary, and identifies a simile, metaphor and personification, explaining one effect. **Mastery =** summary keeps main points only; figurative techniques named with an effect.

## Lesson 13 — Describing a setting with the senses
**English · Year 4 · Unit 4 (Writing fiction: settings & characters)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe a setting using two senses and a simile."

**You will need:** a setting image prompt (Pobble 365 style — e.g. a misty forest or an abandoned house); a senses word-bank (SEE / HEAR / SMELL / FEEL columns); a sentence frame ("As I stepped into the…, I could ___ and ___."); the simile reminder (*like/as*).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Lessons 11–12 — simile and personification for effect; fronted adverbials (Lesson 1).
**Key vocabulary:** *setting* (where and when a story happens), *the senses* (see/hear/smell/feel/taste), *atmosphere* (the feeling of a place), *adjective-stacking* (piling up adjectives without imagery).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Don't TELL the reader "a scary forest" — SHOW it through the **senses** plus a **simile** to build atmosphere. The KB misconception to pre-empt: *adjective-stacking* ("a big, dark, cold, wet forest") — a list, not a picture. The move: pick two senses, add a simile.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Watch the difference. Flat: *It was a scary forest.* Now alive: *Twisted branches clawed at the grey sky, and somewhere an owl shrieked.* The second one puts you THERE — you can see it and hear it. Today you'll build a setting the reader can step right into, using your senses and a simile."

**Show:** the setting image prompt.  **Do:** read both versions; let the second hang in the air.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does a simile use to compare?" → *listen for:* *like* or *as*.
*(If shaky: one quick simile — *the fog was as thick as soup* — before continuing.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Lazy writing just TELLS: *a scary forest.* Good writing SHOWS through the senses — what would I SEE, HEAR, SMELL, FEEL there? Watch me build one line from this image. SEE: twisted branches against a grey sky. HEAR: an owl shrieking. I'll pick two senses and add a simile: *Twisted branches clawed at the grey sky like bony fingers, and somewhere an owl shrieked.* Notice I did NOT just stack adjectives — *a big, dark, cold, wet forest* is a boring LIST. The senses plus a simile build a PICTURE."

**Show / Do:** fill the SEE and HEAR columns of the word-bank from the image, then craft one sense-rich line with a simile; cross out an adjective-stacked version beside it.
**Emphasize:** "Show, don't tell. Pick two senses, add a simile — not a pile of adjectives."

### ⏱ 9–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's build one line together from this image. What would we SEE here? What would we HEAR? Now let's add a simile to one of them. How about… *the floorboards groaned like an old man's bones*?"

**Do:** child contributes a sight and a sound; together craft one line using two senses and a simile; tutor writes it.
**Ask:** "Why is *the floorboards groaned like an old man's bones* better than *the old floor*?" → *listen for:* it gives a sound and a picture / it shows the atmosphere, not just tells.

### ⏱ 15–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name two senses you'd use when describing a setting."
- ✅ **Expected:** Any two of sight, sound, smell, touch (taste). Then probe: *"And what could you add to make a picture, not a list?"* (a simile).
- ❌ **If they list adjectives instead of senses ("dark, cold, big"):** adjective-stacking. → **fix:** "those are adjectives, not senses. What would you SEE? What would you HEAR? Use the senses to build a picture."
- ❌ **If only one sense / no simile:** half the recipe. → **fix:** "great start — now add a second sense, and a *like/as* simile to bring it alive."

### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child describes their chosen setting (from an image) in 2–3 sentences using at least TWO senses and ONE simile.
**Watch for:** adjective-stacking; only one sense; a simile that doesn't fit; telling ("it was scary") instead of showing.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it for them; 2) name *why* — "I think that's a list of adjectives, not the senses"; 3) step down — give the sentence frame and word-bank; fill SEE and HEAR together; 4) ONE hint — "what's one sound you'd hear here?"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just give me one thing you'd SEE, then one you'd HEAR"; 6) keep it safe — "Authors build settings piece by piece — one sense at a time."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What's the recipe for a setting that pulls the reader in?" → *listen for:* show don't tell; two senses + a simile (not adjective-stacking).
**Process praise:** "You showed the place through what you'd see and hear instead of just telling us it was scary — and your simile painted a real picture."
**Revisit later:** Lesson 15 combines setting + character; this skill anchors the Unit 4 check and the end-of-year mastery checks.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the senses word-bank and sentence frame; choose two senses to focus on before writing.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add a fronted adverbial opener (Lesson 1) to vary the rhythm, and a touch of personification.

**Extension (if time allows):** describe a familiar place (their kitchen, the park) at night to make it mysterious, using two senses and a simile.
**Assessment evidence:** child writes a setting description using at least two senses and one simile, avoiding a flat adjective list.

## Lesson 14 — Show, don't tell (character feelings)
**English · Year 4 · Unit 4** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can show a character's emotion through actions instead of stating it."

**You will need:** "telling" sentence cards (*Sam was nervous. / Mia was scared. / He was angry. / She was happy. / He was excited.*); a body-clue bank (shaking hands, racing heart, clenched fists, wide grin, darting eyes, trembling voice); a "show vs tell" two-column chart.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Lesson 13 — show, don't tell (for settings); the idea that details reveal more than labels.
**Key vocabulary:** *show, don't tell* (reveal feelings through actions/details, not by stating them), *body clue* (a physical sign of an emotion), *emotion*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Stating "Sam was nervous" is flat — SHOW the feeling through actions and body clues: "Sam's hands trembled as he reached for the door." The KB worked example: *Mia was scared* → *Mia's heart hammered; she backed away, eyes darting.* Pre-empt flat "telling" by reaching for a body clue or action.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Two sentences. *Sam was nervous.* …Boring, right? Now: *Sam's hands trembled as he reached for the door.* THAT one — we FEEL it, we don't just get told. Today you'll do the 'show, don't tell' upgrade that makes characters come alive."

**Show:** the two Sam sentences stacked.  **Do:** mime trembling hands reaching for a door.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Yesterday we showed a SETTING instead of telling. What does 'show, don't tell' mean?" → *listen for:* reveal it through details/actions, not by stating it.
*(If shaky: contrast "it was scary" with "an owl shrieked" from Lesson 13.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "When I want a reader to feel a character's emotion, I don't NAME it — I show it through what their BODY does. Watch. Telling: *Mia was scared.* Now I think — what does a scared body do? Heart races, you back away, your eyes dart, your voice shakes. Showing: *Mia's heart hammered. She backed away, eyes darting, and her voice came out as a whisper.* I never wrote the word 'scared' — but you FELT it. My move: name the emotion in my head, then pick a body clue or action to show it."

**Show / Do:** in the chart, write *Mia was scared* on the TELL side; on the SHOW side build it from body clues (hammering heart, backing away, darting eyes).
**Emphasize:** "Don't name the feeling — show it with a body clue or an action."

### ⏱ 9–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's upgrade one together. *He was angry.* What does an angry body do? Give me a body clue." (child: clenched fists / red face / slammed door) "Brilliant — let's build it: *He slammed the door and his fists clenched at his sides.*"

**Do:** child supplies body clues from the bank; together craft a "show" sentence; tutor writes it on the SHOW side.
**Ask:** "How do we know he's angry WITHOUT the word 'angry'?" → *listen for:* the slammed door and clenched fists show it.

### ⏱ 15–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Turn *He was angry* into a 'show' sentence."
- ✅ **Expected:** e.g. *He slammed the door and clenched his fists.* Then probe: *"Which body clue shows the anger?"*
- ❌ **If they just swap synonyms ("He was furious"):** still telling, just a bigger label. → **fix:** "that still NAMES the feeling. What does his BODY do when he's angry? Show that."
- ❌ **If they add an action but still state the emotion ("He was angry and slammed the door"):** belt-and-braces. → **fix:** "you've shown it beautifully with the slam — now DELETE 'he was angry'. Trust the reader to feel it."

### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child rewrites two "telling" sentences as "showing" sentences using body clues/actions, deleting the named emotion.
**Watch for:** swapping one label for another; keeping the named emotion AND the action; body clues that don't match the emotion.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "that still NAMES the feeling"; 3) step down — point to the body-clue bank and act the emotion out together; 4) ONE hint — "what does the body DO when you feel this?"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just give me one body clue"; 6) keep it safe — "Showing feelings is a writer's secret weapon — one body clue at a time."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What's the 'show, don't tell' move for a character's feelings?" → *listen for:* show it through a body clue or action, not by naming the emotion.
**Process praise:** "You upgraded flat 'telling' into 'showing' and trusted the reader to feel it — that's exactly how real authors make us care about a character."
**Revisit later:** combined with setting in Lesson 15; anchors the Unit 4 check and the Lesson 19 retrieval round.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the body-clue bank on the desk; choose a clue, then build the sentence around it.
- **Stretch (challenge):** show TWO contrasting emotions in two characters in the same moment (one nervous, one excited).

**Extension (if time allows):** rewrite a "telling" sentence from a younger reader's book as a "show, don't tell" sentence.
**Assessment evidence:** child rewrites at least one "telling" sentence as a "showing" sentence that reveals the emotion through action, with the label removed.

## Lesson 15 — Bringing it together: a setting + character scene
**English · Year 4 · Unit 4** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a short scene with a vivid setting and a character shown through action."

**You will need:** an image prompt (a character about to enter a place); a planning frame (SETTING: 2 senses + simile → CHARACTER ENTERS: emotion shown through action → OPENER: a fronted adverbial); senses and body-clue word-banks.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Lesson 13 (setting through senses + simile), Lesson 14 (show don't tell), Lesson 1 (fronted adverbials).
**Key vocabulary:** *scene* (a moment in a story), *expanded noun phrase* (a noun with extra describing detail), all prior unit vocabulary.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** This is the unit's apply-it-all lesson. Plan, then write a short scene that (1) shows the setting through two senses + a simile and (2) shows a character's emotion through action, with a varied opener (fronted adverbial). Rehearse aloud BEFORE writing. Pre-empt flat telling and adjective-stacking by leaning on the plan and word-banks.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Today, you're the author. You'll drop a character into a setting and let us SEE how they feel — no telling allowed. Everything you've learned this unit, in one short scene. Let's make a reader hold their breath."

**Show:** the image prompt.  **Do:** read a strong one-line model scene aloud for atmosphere.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Two tools from this unit — how do we show a setting, and how do we show a feeling?" → *listen for:* setting → two senses + a simile; feeling → a body clue/action, not the name.
*(If shaky: one quick example of each before planning.)*

### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN / model the plan (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch me plan before I write — authors always plan. First, the SETTING: two senses plus a simile. SEE: the cracked door; HEAR: wind howling — *The cracked door creaked, and the wind howled like a wounded animal.* Next, the CHARACTER ENTERS, shown through action: *Her hands trembled as she stepped inside.* (Not 'she was scared'!) And I'll vary my opener with a fronted adverbial: *Slowly, she pushed the door open.* I rehearse it aloud first to hear if it flows, THEN write."

**Show / Do:** fill the planning frame on the board — setting line, character-entering line, fronted-adverbial opener — then say the whole mini-scene aloud.
**Emphasize:** "Plan first: setting (2 senses + simile), then character shown through action, with a fronted-adverbial opener. Rehearse aloud, then write."

### ⏱ 10–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do — plan together)
> **Say:** "Let's plan ONE together from this image. Setting — give me a sight and a sound, and a simile for one. Now the character — they walk in feeling… let's SHOW it with a body clue. And an opener — a fronted adverbial to start with a pause."

**Do:** child fills each box of the planning frame with the tutor; together rehearse the planned scene aloud once.
**Ask:** "How will the reader know how your character feels?" → *listen for:* from their actions/body clues, not from being told.

### ⏱ 15–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What does 'show, don't tell' mean?"
- ✅ **Expected:** Reveal feelings through actions/details, not by stating them. Then probe: *"And what shows a setting instead of telling it?"* (the senses + a simile).
- ❌ **If "use bigger words/adjectives":** confused show with describe-more. → **fix:** "it's not MORE words — it's showing the FEELING through what the body or the place DOES. What does a scared body do?"
- ❌ **If they restate it as 'don't be boring':** too vague. → **fix:** "be exact — instead of *she was scared*, what could her hands or her heart DO?"

### ⏱ 18–26 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child writes a short paragraph (about 4–6 sentences) combining a vivid setting (2 senses + simile) and a character whose emotion is SHOWN through action, opening with a fronted adverbial. They rehearse it aloud first.
**Watch for:** telling the emotion; adjective-stacking; missing the simile or a sense; no comma after the fronted adverbial.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "I think a feeling got told, or a sense is missing"; 3) step down — return to the planning frame and word-banks; fill one box at a time; 4) ONE hint — "rehearse just the setting line aloud, then add the character"; 5) re-ask smaller — "write just the setting sentence first"; 6) keep it safe — "Whole scenes are big — build it one sentence at a time, like the plan."

### ⏱ 26–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Read me your scene 'in character'. Then tell me: where did you SHOW a feeling instead of telling it?" → *listen for:* the child reads and points to a shown emotion.
**Process praise:** "You planned it, rehearsed it, and showed both the place and the feeling — that's a real author's process, not just writing whatever comes."
**Revisit later:** anchors the Unit 4 check; the show-don't-tell move returns in the Lesson 19 retrieval round.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the planning frame and word-banks completed in the "we do"; write one sentence per box.
- **Stretch (challenge):** include an expanded noun phrase and a simile, and a second varied opener.

**Extension (if time allows):** add a second sentence of dialogue that reveals the character's personality, then perform the whole scene for the tutor or family.
**Assessment evidence:** child writes a short scene with a sensory setting (2 senses + simile) and an emotion shown through action; at least one figurative touch present.

> **End-of-Unit 4 check (tutor-led, ~10 min):** Child describes a setting using two senses and a simile, and shows a character's emotion through action (not telling). **Mastery =** setting is sensory not a list; emotion shown, with at least one figurative touch.

## Lesson 16 — What an explanation text does
**English · Year 4 · Unit 5 (Writing non-fiction: explanation texts)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify the features of an explanation text and tell it apart from instructions."

**You will need:** a model explanation text ("Why do we have day and night?" or "How does a volcano erupt?") with features visible; a feature checklist (present tense / third person / cause-effect connectives / ordered paragraphs / title often a question / headings); mixed sentence cards to sort into EXPLANATION vs INSTRUCTION.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 paragraphs; cause conjunctions; present tense.
**Key vocabulary:** *explanation* (tells how/why something happens), *instructions* (tells the reader how to DO something), *present tense, third person, cause-and-effect connective, heading*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** An explanation answers "**How?**" or "**Why?**" something happens — by itself, in the **present tense**. The KB misconception to pre-empt: writing INSTRUCTIONS ("First, get a volcano…") instead of explaining the process. Key contrast: an explanation describes how something happens on its own; instructions tell the reader steps to do.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "How does a volcano erupt? Why do we have day and night? Why do leaves fall? An explanation text answers 'How?' and 'Why?' — it explains how the world WORKS. Today you'll learn exactly what makes an explanation an explanation, and how it's different from a set of instructions."

**Show:** the title "Why do we have day and night?" on the model.  **Do:** read the model's first paragraph aloud.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What tense is this in — has it happened, is it happening now, or will it happen?" → *listen for:* present tense / happening now.
*(If shaky: contrast *the Earth spins* (present) with *the Earth spun* (past) on the board.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "An explanation has a set of features — let me show you in this model. One: **present tense** — *the Earth spins*, not *spun*. Two: **third person** — it talks about *the Earth*, not *you* or *I*. Three: **cause-and-effect connectives** — *because, so, as a result.* Four: **ordered paragraphs**, often with **headings**, and a **title that's often a question.** Now the big difference: an explanation tells how something happens BY ITSELF. INSTRUCTIONS are different — they tell YOU steps to do: *First, get a bowl…* That's a how-to FOR the reader, not an explanation of a process."

**Show / Do:** tick each feature on the checklist as you find it in the model; then write one instruction sentence (*First, mix the flour*) beside one explanation sentence (*The mixture rises because the air expands*) and label which is which.
**Emphasize:** "An explanation tells how/why it happens by itself, in present tense. Instructions tell the reader steps to DO."

### ⏱ 9–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's label features together in this paragraph. Find me a present-tense verb… a cause-effect connective… Now sort these two sentences: *The Sun heats the water, so it rises* — explanation or instruction? *Next, pour the water into the cup* — which one?"

**Do:** child labels two features in the model and sorts two sentences into EXPLANATION vs INSTRUCTION, justifying each.
**Ask:** "How do you know *Next, pour the water* is an instruction, not an explanation?" → *listen for:* it tells the reader to DO something / it's a step for you, not how it happens by itself.

### ⏱ 15–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What question does an explanation text answer?"
- ✅ **Expected:** How or why something happens. Then probe: *"And how is that different from instructions?"* (instructions tell the reader steps to do).
- ❌ **If "it tells you how to make something":** confused with instructions. → **fix:** "that's INSTRUCTIONS — steps for YOU. An explanation tells how/why something happens on its own, like why we have day and night."
- ❌ **If "it tells a story":** confused with narrative. → **fix:** "a story has characters and events; an explanation is FACTUAL and answers how/why, in the present tense."

### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child sorts six mixed sentences into "explanation" vs "instruction" and ticks which explanation features each one shows.
**Watch for:** sorting by topic instead of by purpose; missing the present-tense or cause-effect markers.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't tell them; 2) name *why* — "let's ask: is this telling YOU to do something, or how it happens by itself?"; 3) step down — read the sentence and ask "who is doing the action — the reader, or the thing itself?"; 4) ONE hint — "instructions boss the reader; explanations describe a process"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just decide: does this tell ME to do something?"; 6) keep it safe — "These two are easy to mix — the purpose is the clue."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Name two features of an explanation text, and tell me how it's different from instructions." → *listen for:* present tense / cause-effect connectives / etc.; instructions tell the reader steps to do.
**Process praise:** "You sorted by the PURPOSE of each sentence, not just the topic — that's how you tell genres apart like an expert reader."
**Revisit later:** Lesson 17 builds the cause-effect language; the features checklist guides the Lesson 18 write and the Unit 5 check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the feature checklist with one example each; sort the clearest instruction/explanation pair first.
- **Stretch (challenge):** turn an instruction sentence into an explanation sentence (e.g. *Add yeast* → *The yeast makes the dough rise because it releases gas*).

**Extension (if time allows):** find an explanation text in a non-fiction book and tick which features it has.
**Assessment evidence:** child sorts at least four sentences correctly into explanation/instruction and names two explanation features.

## Lesson 17 — Cause-and-effect connectives
**English · Year 4 · Unit 5** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can link ideas with *because, so, as a result, this means* to show cause and effect."

**You will need:** a connective bank (*because, so, as a result, this means, which causes*); cause-effect sentence pairs (cause | effect) to join; the day/night model paragraph; "domino" cards to build a cause-effect chain.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Lesson 16 — explanation features; cause-effect connectives are a key one.
**Key vocabulary:** *cause* (what makes it happen), *effect* (what happens as a result), *connective* (a linking word/phrase).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Explanations need cause → effect links: *because, so, as a result, this means.* The KB model: *The Earth spins, so the side facing the Sun has day; as a result, the other side has night.* Pre-empt the "list of facts with no links" problem — join ideas to show WHY.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Watch a domino chain: one falls, *so* the next falls, *as a result* the last one topples. Explanations work the same way — one thing causes the next. *The Earth spins, SO the side facing the Sun has day, AS A RESULT the other side has night.* Cause to effect, like dominoes. Today you'll build your own chains."

**Show:** the day/night model with connectives highlighted.  **Do:** tip a row of dominoes (or mime it) saying "cause… so… as a result…".
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From yesterday — name one feature of an explanation text." → *listen for:* present tense / cause-effect connectives / headings / etc.
*(If shaky: re-read one model sentence and spot a connective.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A cause is what MAKES something happen; the effect is what happens as a result. I link them with connectives: *because, so, as a result, this means, which causes.* Watch the day/night paragraph. *The Earth spins on its axis.* That's my cause. *Because the Earth turns, the side facing the Sun has daytime.* See *because* linking cause to effect? *As a result, the side facing away is in darkness, so we have night. This means day and night keep swapping.* Every link shows WHY — that's what turns a list of facts into a real explanation."

**Show / Do:** build the day/night chain on the board, underlining each connective and drawing an arrow from cause to effect.
**Emphasize:** "Join cause to effect with *because, so, as a result, this means* — show WHY, don't just list facts."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Join two with me. Cause: *the Sun heats the sea.* Effect: *the water turns into vapour.* Which connective links them best?" (→ *so* / *as a result* / *this causes*) "Now: *plants need sunlight* … *they grow towards the window.*" (→ *so* / *as a result*)

**Do:** child chooses the best connective for each pair and reads the joined sentence aloud.
**Ask:** "How do you know *so* fits here?" → *listen for:* the second part is the RESULT of the first / it shows the effect.

### ⏱ 12–15 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Give me two cause-and-effect connectives."
- ✅ **Expected:** Any two of *because, so, as a result, this means, which causes.* Then probe: *"Use one to link 'it rained' and 'the ground got wet'."*
- ❌ **If they give *and/then* only:** sequence words, not cause-effect. → **fix:** "*and* and *then* just add or order — they don't show WHY. Which word shows one thing CAUSED the other? Try *so* or *because*."
- ❌ **If they can name them but not use one:** label without application. → **fix:** "put *because* between them: *the ground got wet because it rained.* Now you've shown the cause."

### ⏱ 15–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child completes five sentences with the best cause-effect connective, then writes one cause-effect sentence of their own.
**Watch for:** *and/then* used instead of cause-effect connectives; the connective placed so cause and effect are the wrong way round.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't tell them; 2) name *why* — "that word adds or orders, but doesn't show WHY"; 3) step down — point to the connective bank and ask "which part is the cause? which is the effect?"; 4) ONE hint — "*because* goes before the cause; *so* goes before the effect"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just tell me the cause and the effect, then I'll show you where the word goes"; 6) keep it safe — "Linking ideas takes practice — let's find the cause first."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What do cause-and-effect connectives do, and name two." → *listen for:* show why/that one thing causes another; *because, so, as a result…*
**Process praise:** "You linked cause to effect instead of just listing facts — that's what makes an explanation actually EXPLAIN."
**Revisit later:** essential for the Lesson 18 write and the Unit 5 check; returns in the Lesson 19 explanation-sentence challenge.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the connective bank on the desk; the cause and effect halves already separated.
- **Stretch (challenge):** write a 3-link cause-effect chain (cause → effect/cause → effect) like a domino run.

**Extension (if time allows):** explain a real everyday process (why you wear a coat in winter) in two linked cause-effect sentences.
**Assessment evidence:** child completes at least four sentences with the correct cause-effect connective and writes one original cause-effect sentence.

## Lesson 18 — Write an explanation text
**English · Year 4 · Unit 5** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a short explanation of a process in the present tense with cause-effect language and headings."

**You will need:** an explanation writing frame (TITLE question / INTRODUCTION / paragraph 1 with HEADING / paragraph 2 with HEADING); a connective bank; process images (water cycle, why leaves fall, how brakes stop a bike); the feature checklist from Lesson 16.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Lesson 16 (explanation features), Lesson 17 (cause-effect connectives), present tense and third person.
**Key vocabulary:** *process* (a series of stages), *title, introduction, heading*, plus all unit vocabulary.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Apply it all — plan and write a short explanation with a title (often a question), an intro, and two explaining paragraphs with headings, in the **present tense**, using **cause-effect connectives**. Pre-empt slipping into instructions or past tense, and listing facts without links.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Today you explain a real wonder of the world like a true non-fiction author — the water cycle, why leaves fall, how a bike's brakes stop it. Pick something you're genuinely curious about, and by the end you'll have written a real explanation text."

**Show:** the process images.  **Do:** read a strong model title and intro aloud.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What tense are explanations in, and name one cause-effect connective." → *listen for:* present tense; *because/so/as a result.*
*(If shaky: recap the feature checklist in one line.)*

### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN / model the plan (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch me plan before writing — authors always plan. My TITLE is a question: *Why do we have day and night?* My INTRODUCTION sets it up: *Every day the sky changes from light to dark. But why?* Then ordered paragraphs, each with a HEADING. Heading one — *The spinning Earth*: *The Earth spins on its axis once every 24 hours.* Heading two — *Day and night*: *Because the side facing the Sun gets light, it is daytime; as a result, the other side is in darkness, so we have night.* Present tense throughout — *spins*, not *spun*. Cause-effect connectives joining ideas."

**Show / Do:** fill the writing frame on the board — title question, intro, two headed paragraphs — ticking the feature checklist as you go.
**Emphasize:** "Title (a question), intro, then headed paragraphs in the present tense, linked with cause-effect connectives."

### ⏱ 10–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do — plan together)
> **Say:** "Let's plan ONE process together. Pick one — the water cycle. What's our title question? Our intro? Heading one… heading two… Now give me one sentence for heading one in the present tense, with a connective."

**Do:** child helps fill each box of the frame for a chosen process and drafts one present-tense, connective-linked sentence aloud.
**Ask:** "How will you make sure you're EXPLAINING, not giving instructions?" → *listen for:* describe how it happens by itself, in present tense — not steps for the reader.

### ⏱ 15–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What tense are explanations usually in?"
- ✅ **Expected:** Present tense. Then probe: *"Give me a present-tense verb for your process."* (e.g. *rises, spins, falls*).
- ❌ **If "past tense":** confused with recount/story. → **fix:** "explanations describe what happens GENERALLY, every time — so present tense: *the water rises*, not *rose*."
- ❌ **If they start giving instructions ("first you boil the water"):** drifted into how-to. → **fix:** "you're telling the reader to DO it — instead describe how it happens by itself: *the water heats and rises.*"

### ⏱ 18–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child plans (using the frame) and writes a short explanation with a title question, a one-sentence introduction, and TWO explaining paragraphs (each with a heading), in the present tense, using at least two cause-effect connectives.
**Watch for:** past tense creeping in; instructions instead of explanation; facts listed without connectives; missing headings or title.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "I think a step/instruction or past tense slipped in"; 3) step down — return to the frame and write one box at a time; supply connective starters; 4) ONE hint — "what happens FIRST in the process, and what does it cause?"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just write the title question and one sentence under heading one"; 6) keep it safe — "Real authors draft a bit at a time — one paragraph, then the next."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Read me your title and one cause-effect sentence. What three features make this an explanation text?" → *listen for:* present tense, cause-effect connectives, headings/title question.
**Process praise:** "You planned it, kept it in the present tense, and linked your ideas with cause and effect — that's a genuine non-fiction explanation, not a list of facts."
**Revisit later:** the central evidence for the Unit 5 check; the explanation skill is rehearsed once more in Lesson 19.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the writing frame with headings and connective starters (*Because…, As a result…*); plan completed in the "we do".
- **Stretch (challenge):** add a labelled diagram and a third subheading, and a closing sentence that summarises the process.

**Extension (if time allows):** explain a second, shorter process (why we yawn) in two linked sentences.
**Assessment evidence:** child writes a short explanation with a title, intro and two headed paragraphs, in the present tense, using cause-effect connectives correctly.

## Lesson 19 — End-of-year review: summarise, craft, explain (retrieval)
**English · Year 4 · Unit 5** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can summarise a text, show a feeling, and explain with cause-effect language."

**You will need:** a short non-fiction text (one paragraph) for summarising; a "telling" sentence card (*He was nervous.*); a process prompt (e.g. why ice melts) for the explanation sentence; mixed task cards (1 summary / 2 show-don't-tell / 3 cause-effect explanation); word banks and frames for support.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the whole year — summarising (L9–10), show-don't-tell (L14), word choice (L12), cause-effect explanation (L17–18).
**Key vocabulary:** all year-4 vocabulary, revisited: *summary, show don't tell, cause-effect connective, present tense, word choice.*
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A celebratory mixed-retrieval round — three quick challenges proving the year's biggest skills. No new content; the point is independent recall across strands. Keep it safe and triumphant ("beat your personal best").

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Show-off day! Everything Year 4 taught you, in three quick challenges. Challenge one: summarise. Challenge two: show, don't tell. Challenge three: explain with cause and effect. Ready to beat your personal best?"

**Show:** the three mixed task cards laid out.  **Do:** read the three challenges aloud like a game-show host.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Lightning recall — what's the magic question for finding a main idea?" → *listen for:* what is it MOSTLY about?
*(If shaky: one quick reminder per skill before the round.)*

### ⏱ 3–7 min · EXPLAIN / quick model (I do)
> **Say:** "A lightning reminder of each. ONE — summary: read, ask 'what's it MOSTLY about?', say it in one sentence, your own words. TWO — show, don't tell: instead of naming a feeling, show it with a body clue. THREE — explain: link cause to effect with *because/so/as a result*, in the present tense. That's the whole year in three moves."

**Show / Do:** on the board, write a tiny model of each: a one-line summary; *He was nervous* → *His hands trembled*; *The Sun heats the ice, so it melts.*
**Emphasize:** "Three moves: summarise the main idea; show the feeling; link cause to effect."

### ⏱ 7–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "One warm-up of each together. Quick — what's the main idea of this short text? Now upgrade *She was happy* into a 'show' sentence. Now link these: *the temperature drops … the water freezes* — with a cause-effect connective."

**Do:** child gives a one-line summary, a show-don't-tell upgrade, and a cause-effect link, with the tutor confirming each.
**Ask:** "How do you know your summary keeps only the main points?" → *listen for:* I left out the small details/examples.

### ⏱ 11–14 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Why might a writer choose *stomped* instead of *walked*?"
- ✅ **Expected:** To show anger/heaviness. Then probe: *"…which makes the reader feel what?"*
- ❌ **If "they mean the same":** missed the word-choice effect. → **fix:** "act both out — *walked*, then *stomped*. How does the stomper feel? *Stomped* shows anger; that's the choice."
- ❌ **If they name a feeling but no reason:** stopped short. → **fix:** "finish with the effect — *stomped* makes the reader feel/picture…?"

### ⏱ 14–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child independently completes all three challenges: (1) writes a one-sentence summary of the short text; (2) rewrites *He was nervous* as a 'show' sentence; (3) writes one cause-effect explanation sentence in the present tense.
**Watch for:** summary copying or detail-grabbing; telling instead of showing; *and/then* instead of a cause-effect connective; past tense in the explanation.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "which skill is this — summarise, show, or explain?"; 3) step down — give the matching frame/word-bank for that challenge; 4) ONE hint — the magic question / body clue / connective for that skill; 5) re-ask smaller — break the challenge to its first step; 6) keep it safe — "This is a victory lap, not a test — take each challenge at your own pace."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Three challenges, three skills — tell me one thing you got better at this year." → *listen for:* a specific skill named (summarising / showing feelings / explaining).
**Process praise:** "You pulled three different skills out of your toolkit and used each one independently — that's a whole year of growth, right there. Brilliant work this year."
**Revisit later:** feeds the end-of-year mastery checks below; flag any wobbly strand for a spaced-review warm-up before Year 5.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** frames and word-banks given for all three challenges; do them one at a time with a short break.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add a fronted adverbial (with a comma) and a possessive apostrophe somewhere in the three answers.

**Extension (if time allows):** combine all three skills into one short paragraph: summarise a topic, show a feeling about it, and explain one cause-effect fact.
**Assessment evidence:** child independently produces a correct one-sentence summary, a show-don't-tell rewrite, and a present-tense cause-effect sentence.

## End-of-year mastery checks

A Year 4 child has mastered English when they can:

1. **Open and punctuate with control** — use fronted adverbials followed by a comma, and place possessive apostrophes correctly for singular, plural and irregular owners (*Suddenly, …*; *the boys' toys*; *the children's coats*). *(Lessons 1–3)*
2. **Write Standard English** — use correct subject–verb agreement (*we were, she doesn't*) and irregular past tenses (*went, saw, caught*), fixing non-Standard forms. *(Lessons 4–5)*
3. **Spell the `/shun/` families** — choose *-tion, -sion, -ssion* or *-cian* by root clue (*action, division, discussion, musician*) and spell remaining Y3/4 list words. *(Lessons 6–8)*
4. **Summarise** — give the main idea of a paragraph in one sentence and combine paragraph ideas into an overall summary, keeping only the main points. *(Lessons 9–10)*
5. **Analyse writer's effect** — identify simile, metaphor and personification, and explain how a word choice makes the reader feel or picture something. *(Lessons 11–12)*
6. **Describe settings** — build a setting through at least two senses and a simile, avoiding flat adjective-lists. *(Lesson 13)*
7. **Create character** — show emotion through action ("show, don't tell") rather than stating it. *(Lessons 14–15)*
8. **Explain in non-fiction** — write a clear explanation of a process in the present tense, using cause-and-effect connectives and headings. *(Lessons 16–18)*

**Suggested mastery task (one sitting, ~30 min):** Give the child a short non-fiction paragraph and an image prompt. Ask them to: (a) write a one-sentence summary of the paragraph; (b) write two sentences of setting description (two senses + a simile) and one "show, don't tell" character sentence from the image; (c) write a two-sentence present-tense explanation of a simple process using a cause-effect connective; (d) on a quick grammar strip, front an adverbial with a comma, punctuate *the children's coats* and *the boys' toys*, and fix *We was happy and I done my best.* Mastery = success across at least three of the four parts, with the grammar strip correct.

### Closing note

These nineteen scripts take a Year 4 child from organising writing to **crafting it for effect**: opening sentences with style, showing belonging precisely, writing correct Standard English, spelling the whole `/shun/` family by root clue, summarising the gist, noticing how writers make us feel, building vivid settings and characters, and explaining how the world works. Every lesson is explicit-first then gradually released to independence, with retrieval and spaced spelling woven through so the year is a **gap-free climb**, not a set of isolated tasks. Keep the wins specific and process-focused, revisit wobbly strands as 30-second warm-ups, and carry into Year 5 any support scaffolds the child still needs on apostrophes, `/shun/` spellings, or show-don't-tell. *Best, not fast.*
