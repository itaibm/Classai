## Lesson 1 — Fronted adverbials and the comma
**English · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Adverbials, apostrophes, Standard English)** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can move an adverbial to the front of a sentence and follow it with a comma."

**You will need:** sentence cards (*We set off later that day. / Something stirred in the deep, dark cave. / She crept forward without a sound. / The rooster crowed every morning. / We ate breakfast quickly before school.*); an "adverbial bank" card sorted into Time / Place / Manner; a large comma cut-out or a red pen.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 adverbs and prepositions — words/phrases that tell *how, when, where*.
**Key vocabulary:** *adverbial* (a word or phrase telling how/when/where), *fronted adverbial* (an adverbial moved to the start), *comma* (the pause mark after it).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Not every sentence should start with "The…". The move that must land: **find the when/where/how phrase → move it to the front → add a comma.** Pre-empt the two KB misconceptions — *forgetting the comma* (read aloud and hear the pause) and *thinking any opening word is a fronted adverbial* (the subject "The dog" is NOT an adverbial). Repeated phrase: *"Front it, then comma."*

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Listen to two story openers. Number one: *A noise echoed.* Number two: *Suddenly, a noise echoed.* Which one makes you lean in? Now this: *Something stirred. Deep in the cave, something stirred.* Hear how the second one sets the scene before anything happens? Today you learn the trick great writers use so not every sentence starts with 'The…'."

**Show:** the two openers written on the board, the fronted version underlined.  **Do:** read each pair aloud, pausing clearly at the comma.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "An adverbial tells us *when, where* or *how*. In *We set off later that day*, which words tell us WHEN?" → *listen for:* "later that day".
*(If shaky: ask three quick questions — "when did it happen? where? how?" — and circle the answering words to rebuild the idea of an adverbial.)*

### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch me front an adverbial. My sentence: *We ate breakfast quickly before school.* Step one — I find the adverbial. *Before school* tells me WHEN — that's my adverbial. Step two — I move it to the very front: *Before school… we ate breakfast quickly.* Step three, the most important — I add a comma straight after it: *Before school, we ate breakfast quickly.* The comma tells the reader to take a tiny pause after the scene-setting phrase. My rule every single time: **front it, then comma.**"

**Show / Do:** physically move the *before school* card to the front of the sentence, then slot the comma cut-out in. Read it aloud and nod your head at the comma to mark the pause.
**Emphasize:** "Front it, then comma. The fronted adverbial still tells when, where or how — it has just moved to the start."

### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn to help. Sentence one: *Something stirred in the deep, dark cave.* Find the adverbial that tells us WHERE." (child: *in the deep, dark cave*) "Move it to the front and tell me what goes next." 

**Do:** child moves the phrase; tutor writes *In the deep, dark cave* and pauses — child must supply the comma. Then do *She crept forward without a sound* together (manner → *Without a sound, she crept forward*).
**Ask:** "How do you KNOW a comma goes there?" → *listen for:* because it's a fronted adverbial / because you pause after the scene-setting bit.

### ⏱ 14–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What punctuation follows a fronted adverbial? And is *The dog* in *The dog barked loudly* a fronted adverbial?"
- ✅ **Expected:** a comma; and no — *The dog* is the subject, not an adverbial. Then probe: *"How do you know The dog isn't an adverbial?"* (it doesn't tell when/where/how).
- ❌ **If "no comma needed" / forgets the comma:** they don't hear the pause. → **fix:** read the sentence aloud together and freeze where the voice naturally pauses — "that pause is the comma's home. Front it, then comma."
- ❌ **If "The dog is a fronted adverbial":** confusing the subject with an adverbial. → **fix:** ask "does *The dog* tell us *when, where* or *how*? No — it tells us *who*. So it's the subject, not an adverbial."

### ⏱ 17–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child rewrites five sentences, moving the adverbial to the front and adding a comma: *We left later that day. / A figure waited at the gate. / The team scored in the final minute. / He spoke without any fear. / The rooster crowed every morning.* Then sort three of the fronted adverbials by Time / Place / Manner.
**Watch for:** the missing comma (most common slip); the child fronting the *subject* by mistake; a comma placed mid-phrase rather than after the whole adverbial.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it for them; 2) name *why* — "I think the comma slipped, or you moved the wrong chunk"; 3) step down — read the sentence aloud and find the when/where/how phrase together; 4) ONE hint — "underline the whole phrase before you move it"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just tell me where the pause is, then put the comma there"; 6) keep it safe — "Editors fix commas all day. Let's take one sentence at a time."

### ⏱ 22–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Teach me the trick. If I don't want every sentence to start with 'The', what three steps do I take?" → *listen for:* find the when/where/how phrase, move it to the front, add a comma.
**Process praise:** "You didn't just move the words — you remembered the comma every time and checked it with the pause. That's careful, writer-like editing."
**Revisit later:** open Lesson 2 with a 30-second warm-up — front one adverbial. Bring fronted adverbials back in Lesson 15 (varying story openers) and the Unit 1 check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the adverbial is already underlined on each card so the child only moves it and adds the comma; start with short time phrases (*Suddenly, … / Later, …*).
- **Stretch (challenge):** sort a set of fronted adverbials into Time / Place / Manner and write one original sentence for each type.

**Extension (if time allows):** open a favourite book, hunt for a fronted adverbial, and copy it out — proving real authors use this.
**Assessment evidence:** child independently fronts at least four adverbials, each followed correctly by a comma, and explains the comma marks the pause.

## Lesson 2 — Singular possessive apostrophe (recap & secure)
**English · Year 4 · Unit 1** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can place the apostrophe before the *s* for one owner."

**You will need:** belonging phrase cards (*the dog's lead, the girl's bag, the tutor's desk, Sam's coat*); a plain-plural card (*the dogs are barking*); an "apostrophe judge" gavel or sticker.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 2 — the apostrophe shows belonging.
**Key vocabulary:** *possession* (owning/belonging), *apostrophe* (the floating mark that shows who owns something), *plain plural* (more than one, no owning).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** For ONE owner, the apostrophe goes **before the *s***: *the girl's bag*. The big trap to pre-empt (from the KB): putting an apostrophe in a *plain plural* (*the dog's are barking*). The judge's question: *"Is something belonging here? If not — no apostrophe."*

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Whose bag is this? *The girl's bag* — one girl, and the little apostrophe before the *s* shows she OWNS it. Today you're the apostrophe judge: you decide who owns what — and you catch the apostrophes that have snuck in where they don't belong!"

**Show:** *the girl's bag* on the board, apostrophe circled.  **Do:** point at the apostrophe and say "this little mark means *belongs to*."
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does an apostrophe show in *the dog's lead*?" → *listen for:* that the lead belongs to the dog / belonging.
*(If shaky: hold up the *dog's lead* card and ask "who does the lead belong to?" — link "belongs to" → apostrophe.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch me. *The dog's lead* — one dog. I find the owner: *dog*. There's just one, so I add apostrophe then *s*: *dog's*. The apostrophe goes BEFORE the *s* for one owner. Now watch the trap. *The dogs are barking.* Is anything belonging here? No — there are just lots of dogs. *Dogs* is a plain plural, so it gets NO apostrophe. The judge's question every time: **is something belonging here?** If yes, apostrophe. If it just means *more than one*, leave it alone."

**Show / Do:** write *the dog's lead* (circle the apostrophe, label "one owner") beside *the dogs are barking* (cross out any imagined apostrophe, label "plain plural — no owner").
**Emphasize:** "One owner → apostrophe before the *s*. Plain plural → no apostrophe."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Judge with me. *The bag belonging to the girl.* Who's the owner? How many? So where does the apostrophe go?" (→ *the girl's bag*) "Now this one: *the desk belonging to the tutor*." (→ *the tutor's desk*)

**Do:** child punctuates each phrase on a mini-whiteboard; tutor confirms the apostrophe sits before the *s*.
**Ask:** "How do you know it's *girl's* and not just *girls*?" → *listen for:* because the bag belongs to her / one owner, so apostrophe.

### ⏱ 12–15 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Is *cats* in *the cats are asleep* possessive — does it need an apostrophe?"
- ✅ **Expected:** No — it's a plain plural, no apostrophe. Then probe: *"How do you know?"* (nothing is belonging; it just means more than one cat).
- ❌ **If "yes, it needs an apostrophe":** confusing plural with possessive. → **fix:** ask "is something belonging to the cats? No. So it's just *more than one* — plain plural, no apostrophe."
- ❌ **If they add it after the *s* (*cats'*):** still treating it as possessive. → **fix:** "ask the judge's question first — is anything owned? Nothing is. So no apostrophe at all."

### ⏱ 15–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child writes singular possessives for four phrases — *the lead belonging to the dog / the coat belonging to Sam / the desk belonging to the tutor / the tail belonging to the cat* — and then judges *the boys are running* (correctly rejecting an apostrophe — plain plural).
**Watch for:** apostrophe placed after the *s* on a singular; an apostrophe wrongly inserted into the plain plural.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't correct it silently; 2) name *why* — "let's check: is this one owner, or just more than one?"; 3) step down — sort the card into the "belonging" pile or the "just more than one" pile first; 4) ONE hint — "for one owner, the apostrophe hugs in *before* the *s*"; 5) re-ask smaller — "punctuate just *dog's*"; 6) keep it safe — "Even shop signs get this wrong. Let's judge one at a time."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Apostrophe judge — what's the very first question you ask before adding an apostrophe?" → *listen for:* is something belonging here?
**Process praise:** "You didn't just sprinkle apostrophes — you asked 'is something belonging?' every time and threw out the ones that didn't belong. That's a sharp-eyed editor."
**Revisit later:** warm up Lesson 3 with one singular possessive before meeting plurals. Revisit in the Unit 1 check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** a yes/no "is something belonging?" gate card to tick before each item; start with names (*Sam's coat*).
- **Stretch (challenge):** write one sentence that uses a possessive AND a plain plural correctly (e.g. *The dogs barked at the cat's bowl.*).

**Extension (if time allows):** find a real sign or label and judge whether its apostrophe is right or wrong, explaining why.
**Assessment evidence:** child writes four correct singular possessives and correctly rejects an apostrophe for a plain plural, explaining the difference.

## Lesson 3 — Plural & irregular possessive apostrophe
**English · Year 4 · Unit 1** | **Duration:** 25 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can place the apostrophe for many owners and for irregular plurals."

**You will need:** owner cards split into three piles — singular (*girl, dog*), regular plural (*girls, dogs, babies*), irregular plural (*children, men, women*); a "does the plural end in *s*?" decision card; mini-whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Lesson 2 — one owner, apostrophe before the *s*.
**Key vocabulary:** *plural* (more than one), *regular plural* (ends in *s*), *irregular plural* (doesn't end in *s* — *children, men*), *owner word*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Where the apostrophe goes tells you HOW MANY owners. Two new moves: regular plural already ending in *s* → apostrophe **after** the *s* (*the dogs' leads*); irregular plural (no *s*) → treat like singular, add **'s** (*the children's toys*). The KB trick: **find the owner word first; if it already ends in *s*, just add an apostrophe after it.** Pre-empt *the dogs's leads* and *the childrens' toys*.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Yesterday: one girl owns a bag — *the girl's bag*. But what if MANY girls each own a bag? *The girls' bags* — and look, the apostrophe jumps to AFTER the *s*! Even trickier: *children* don't end in *s* at all… so where does their apostrophe go? Detectives, let's find out."

**Show:** *the girl's bag* and *the girls' bags* side by side, apostrophes circled.  **Do:** point out the apostrophe moving from before to after the *s*.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "For ONE owner, where does the apostrophe go in *the dog's lead*?" → *listen for:* before the *s*.
*(If shaky: re-state "one owner → apostrophe before the *s*" with the *dog* card before introducing plurals.)*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "My golden trick: find the owner word FIRST, then look at how it ends. Watch. *Leads belonging to the dogs.* Owner word = *dogs*. It already ends in *s* because it's a plural — so I just add an apostrophe AFTER the *s*: *the dogs' leads.* I do NOT add another *s* — *dogs's* would be wrong. Now an irregular one. *Toys belonging to the children.* Owner word = *children*. Does it end in *s*? No! So I treat it like one owner and add apostrophe-*s*: *the children's toys.* So: ends in *s* → apostrophe after; doesn't end in *s* → add *'s*."

**Show / Do:** write *dogs* → add only an apostrophe (no extra s) → *dogs'*. Then *children* → add *'s* → *children's*. Use the decision card "does the plural end in *s*?" with arrows to each rule.
**Emphasize:** "Find the owner word first. Ends in *s* → apostrophe after. No *s* → add *'s*."

### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together now. *Toys belonging to the babies.* Owner word? (*babies*) Does it end in *s*? (yes) So…?" (→ *the babies' toys*) "Now *the room belonging to the men*. Owner word? (*men*) Ends in *s*? (no) So…?" (→ *the men's room*)

**Do:** child runs the decision card for each — owner word, does-it-end-in-*s*, place the apostrophe. Write both on the board.
**Ask:** "How do you know *babies'* has the apostrophe after but *men's* has it before the *s*?" → *listen for:* babies already ends in *s* (plural); men doesn't.

### ⏱ 16–19 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Coats belonging to the children — write it. And toys belonging to many boys?"
- ✅ **Expected:** *the children's coats* (irregular → *'s*); *the boys' toys* (regular plural → apostrophe after). Then probe: *"Why does children get apostrophe-s but boys gets just an apostrophe?"*
- ❌ **If "*the childrens' coats*":** added an *s* to an irregular plural, then apostrophe-after. → **fix:** "*children* is already the plural — it doesn't take an extra *s*. It doesn't end in *s*, so it's like one owner: add *'s* → *children's*."
- ❌ **If "*the boys's toys*" or "*the boy's toys*" for many boys:** added an extra *s*, or made it singular. → **fix:** "*boys* already ends in *s* for many boys — just pop the apostrophe AFTER: *boys'*."

### ⏱ 19–23 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child places the apostrophe for six items mixing all three types — *the lead belonging to the dog (singular) / the leads belonging to the dogs (regular plural) / the toys belonging to the children (irregular) / the bags belonging to the girls / the room belonging to the men / the nest belonging to the bird* — then fixes two wrong ones: *the childrens' toys* and *the dogs's leads*.
**Watch for:** extra *s* on irregulars; apostrophe-before on a regular plural; forgetting the owner-word check.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "let's find the owner word and check its ending"; 3) step down — sort the card into "ends in *s*" or "doesn't" first; 4) ONE hint — "ends in *s* → apostrophe after; no *s* → add *'s*"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just punctuate the owner word *dogs*"; 6) keep it safe — "This one trips up adults. Find the owner word, take it slow."

### ⏱ 23–25 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me your golden trick for choosing where the apostrophe goes." → *listen for:* find the owner word first; ends in *s* → after; no *s* → *'s*.
**Process praise:** "You found the owner word every time before deciding — that's why your apostrophes landed in the right spot. Real detective work."
**Revisit later:** mix one plural possessive into a sentence in Lesson 15; appears in the Unit 1 check and the end-of-year mastery checks.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the "does the plural end in *s*?" decision card on the desk; do all the singular and regular ones before any irregular.
- **Stretch (challenge):** fix and explain why *the childrens' toys* and *the dogs's leads* are wrong, then write a sentence using a plural possessive.

**Extension (if time allows):** photograph or copy three real signs and judge each apostrophe (singular / plural / irregular) as right or wrong.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly punctuates singular, regular-plural and irregular-plural possessives (at least one of each) and fixes a wrong example.

## Lesson 4 — Standard English: subject–verb agreement
**English · Year 4 · Unit 1** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can write in Standard English (we *were*, he *doesn't*) for formal writing."

**You will need:** non-Standard sentence cards (*We was happy. / They was late. / He don't like it. / She don't care. / You was right.*); an agreement table (I/he/she/it **was**; we/you/they **were**; he/she **doesn't**); a "newsreader" microphone prop.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 — writing in past and present tense; the idea of a home way of talking.
**Key vocabulary:** *Standard English* (the agreed "school/news" form for writing and formal speech), *agreement* (the verb matching the subject), *dialect* (a home way of talking).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Everyone's home dialect is lovely — but for writing and formal speech we use Standard English, where the verb **agrees** with the subject. The KB rules: *I/he/she/it was; we/you/they were; she doesn't* (not *don't*). Pre-empt *we was / they was* (dialect agreement). Keep it respectful: home talk isn't "wrong", it's just a different setting.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Everyone has a home way of talking — and that's brilliant, it's part of who you are. But when we WRITE, or read the news, we switch to a special shared form called Standard English — let's call it 'newsreader voice'. Pick up the mic and read this like a newsreader: *We were the champions.* Sounds official, doesn't it?"

**Show:** *We were the champions* on the board.  **Do:** hand over the mic; let the child read in "newsreader voice".
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "When might we need this formal Standard English?" → *listen for:* writing a letter, the news, a report, school writing.
*(If shaky: give the two settings — "playground talk with your friends" vs "writing a formal letter" — and place this lesson in the formal one.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "In Standard English the verb has to MATCH the subject — we call that agreement. Watch. *We was happy* — newsreaders would never say that. *We* takes *were*, not *was*. So it's *We were happy.* My table: *I, he, she, it* → *was*; *we, you, they* → *were*. And one more: *she doesn't* — never *she don't*. *He doesn't like it*, not *he don't like it*. The verb dresses up to match its subject."

**Show / Do:** display the agreement table; fix *We was happy* → *We were happy* live, pointing from *we* to *were*. Then *He don't* → *He doesn't*.
**Emphasize:** "We/you/they → were. He/she/it → was. And it's doesn't, not don't, for he/she/it."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Newsreaders, fix these with me. *They was late.* Look at the subject *they* — which one, *was* or *were*?" (→ *They were late.*) "Now *She don't care.*" (→ *She doesn't care.*)

**Do:** child corrects each on the whiteboard, checking against the agreement table.
**Ask:** "How do you know it's *were* and not *was* for *they*?" → *listen for:* because *they* goes with *were* on the table / the verb matches the subject.

### ⏱ 12–15 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Standard English: *they ___ at the park* — was or were? And fix *He don't like it.*"
- ✅ **Expected:** *were*; and *He doesn't like it.* Then probe: *"How did you decide?"* (matched the verb to the subject).
- ❌ **If "they was":** dialect agreement. → **fix:** point to the table — "*they* lines up with *were*. Read it back: *they were* — that's the newsreader form."
- ❌ **If "he don't":** non-Standard verb for he/she/it. → **fix:** "for *he, she, it* we use *doesn't*. Say it: *he doesn't* — does that sound like the news?"

### ⏱ 15–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child corrects five sentences into Standard English: *We was first. / You was right. / They was tired. / She don't know. / It don't matter.*
**Watch for:** *was/were* slips with *we/you/they*; *don't* left in for *he/she/it*; over-correcting (changing *I was* — which is already correct).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't just give the answer; 2) name *why* — "let's check the subject against the table"; 3) step down — point to the subject, then trace across to its verb; 4) ONE hint — "*we/you/they* always take *were*"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just say *they ___* in newsreader voice"; 6) keep it safe — "Your home voice is great — this is just the formal switch. One at a time."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "In newsreader voice, what verb do *we, you* and *they* take — and what about *he, she, it*?" → *listen for:* *were*; *was* / *doesn't*.
**Process praise:** "You switched into Standard English on purpose and checked every verb against the subject. That's exactly how careful writers edit."
**Revisit later:** warm up Lesson 5 (irregular past) with one agreement fix; central to the Unit 1 check and end-of-year mastery checks.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the agreement table stays on the desk; start with the *was/were* sentences before the *don't/doesn't* ones.
- **Stretch (challenge):** edit a short paragraph so the *narrator* is Standard English, but allow a character to speak naturally in dialogue.

**Extension (if time allows):** "read the news" — write one Standard-English sentence reporting something that happened at home today, then read it in newsreader voice.
**Assessment evidence:** child corrects at least four non-Standard sentences (mixing *was/were* and *don't/doesn't*) into Standard English, explaining the agreement.

## Lesson 5 — Irregular past tenses (went, saw, caught) — retrieval
**English · Year 4 · Unit 1** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can use the correct irregular past tense and avoid *goed/catched*."

**You will need:** irregular verb-pair cards (*go→went, see→saw, catch→caught, bring→brought, do→did, come→came*) face down for matching; a gap-fill sheet; a "rebel verbs" poster.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Lesson 4 — Standard English subject–verb agreement; the idea that past tense usually adds *-ed*.
**Key vocabulary:** *past tense* (already happened), *irregular* (doesn't follow the usual rule), *regularise* (wrongly adding *-ed*).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Most verbs add *-ed* for the past — but the common "rebel verbs" don't. The KB set: *go→went, see→saw, catch→caught, bring→brought, do→did, come→came*. Pre-empt *goed, catched, seed* and *I done* — the brain over-applies the *-ed* rule. These are learned as fixed pairs, not worked out.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Quick warm-up first, then a challenge. *Goed* to the shop? *Catched* the ball? Those sound wrong, don't they — but they follow the normal rule of just adding *-ed*. Some verbs are REBELS: they refuse the *-ed* rule. *Go* becomes *went*. *Catch* becomes *caught*. Today we tame the rebel verbs."

**Show:** the "rebel verbs" poster with *go → went* and *catch → caught*.  **Do:** say each pair with a rebellious shrug for the changed word.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge — Lesson 4):** "Fix this into Standard English: *We was late.*" → *listen for:* *We were late.*
*(If shaky: point to the agreement idea — *we/you/they → were* — then move on.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Most verbs add *-ed* for the past: *jump → jumped, play → played.* Easy. But the rebels change completely — you can't just add *-ed*, you have to KNOW them. Watch my rebel list: *go → went* (not goed). *See → saw* (not seed). *catch → caught* (not catched). *bring → brought.* *do → did* (not done on its own). *come → came.* I don't work these out — I just know the pairs, like best friends who always go together."

**Show / Do:** reveal the six pairs one at a time, crossing out the wrong *-ed* version (*goed*, *catched*) beside each correct one.
**Emphasize:** "Rebel verbs don't add *-ed* — learn the pair: *go→went, catch→caught*."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Match the rebels with me. I say the present, you give me the past. *See*…?" (→ *saw*) "*Bring*…?" (→ *brought*) "*Come*…?" (→ *came*) "Now use one in a sentence: *Yesterday I ___ home early.* (came)"

**Do:** child turns over and matches three present→past pairs, then drops one into a spoken past-tense sentence.
**Ask:** "How do you know it's *saw* and not *seed*?" → *listen for:* because *see* is a rebel verb / you just have to know it / *seed* isn't a word for it.

### ⏱ 12–15 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What's the past tense of *catch*? And fix this: *I done my best.*"

> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't supply the word; 2) name *why* — "this is a rebel verb, so *-ed* won't work"; 3) step down — show the matching pair card; 4) ONE hint — "say the pair out loud: *go… went*"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just tell me the past of *go*"; 6) keep it safe — "Even fluent speakers have to learn these by heart. **Process praise:** "You spotted every rebel and used the right pair instead of guessing *-ed*. That's exactly how strong writers handle tricky verbs."

## Lesson 6 — -tion and -cian (the default and the jobs)
**English · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Spelling: the /shun/ families & Y3/4 list)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can spell *-tion* words and the *-cian* job words."

**You will need:** root cards (*act, complete, attend, music, magic, electric*); a spelling sheet; a "four disguises" poster (-tion / -sion / -ssion / -cian) with the first two revealed.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 suffix families; the idea that a suffix is an ending added to a root.
**Key vocabulary:** `/shun/` (the ending sound), *suffix* (an ending added to a root), *root* (the base word), *default* (the most common choice).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** The `/shun/` sound has FOUR spellings; today we unmask two. **-tion** is the default (often from *-t/-te* roots: *act→action*); **-cian** is for a person/job (from *-c/-cs* roots: *music→musician*). The KB clue: **look at the root.** Pre-empt over-using *-tion* — jobs end in *-cian*.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Say these words: *acTION… musiCIAN.* Hear that same ending — `/shun/`? Here's the puzzle: that one sound wears FOUR different disguises in spelling. Today we unmask two of them — the most common one, and a special one just for jobs."

**Show:** *action* and *musician* on the board, the `/shun/` endings underlined.  **Do:** say each, exaggerating the matching end-sound.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's a suffix? Give me the ending we add to *jump* to make it past." → *listen for:* *-ed* / an ending added to a root word.
*(If shaky: define quickly — "a suffix is an ending we bolt onto a base word, like *-ed*, *-ing*" — and show *jump→jumping*.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Disguise one: **-tion** — this is the DEFAULT, the one to try first. It often comes from roots ending in *-t* or *-te*. Watch: *act* → *action.* *complete* → *completion* (drop the *e*, add *-tion*). Disguise two: **-cian** — this one is special, it's for a PERSON or a JOB, and it comes from roots ending in *-c* or *-cs*. *music* → *musician.* *magic* → *magician.* So my clue is the root and the meaning: is it a job? Then *-cian*. If not, try *-tion* first."

**Show / Do:** build *act → action* and *music → musician* on the board, pointing to the root each time; label *-tion* "default" and *-cian* "jobs/people".
**Emphasize:** "Look at the root. Job or person → *-cian*. Otherwise, *-tion* is the default."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Spell with me. The action of *attend* — *attend → atten…*?" (→ *attention*) "A person who does *magic*?" (→ *magician*) "A person who fixes *electric* things?" (→ *electrician*)

**Do:** child builds each from the root card, deciding *-tion* or *-cian* by asking "is it a job?".
**Ask:** "How did you know *magician* uses *-cian* and *attention* uses *-tion*?" → *listen for:* a magician is a job/person; attention isn't a job.

### ⏱ 12–15 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Spell the word for a person who plays music. And the noun from *act*."
- ✅ **Expected:** *musician*; *action*. Then probe: *"Why -cian for one and -tion for the other?"*
- ❌ **If "musition":** over-used the default *-tion*. → **fix:** "is a musician a job? Yes — jobs and people take *-cian*. The root *music* ends in *-c*, which helps too."
- ❌ **If "actcian" / wrong root handling:** misread the clue. → **fix:** "is *action* a job? No. So use the default *-tion*: *act* + *ion* → *action.*"

### ⏱ 15–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child spells five words choosing *-tion* or *-cian* from the root: *act, complete, attend (→ -tion); music, magic, electric (→ -cian)* — pick five across both.
**Watch for:** everything spelled *-tion*; dropping or keeping the wrong final letter of the root (*complete* → drop the *e*).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't spell it for them; 2) name *why* — "let's check: is it a job, and how does the root end?"; 3) step down — sort the root card into "job" or "not a job" first; 4) ONE hint — "jobs/people → *-cian*"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just add the ending to *magic*"; 6) keep it safe — "Spelling these is detective work, not magic. Use the root clue."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Two disguises unmasked! When do we use *-cian* instead of the default *-tion*?" → *listen for:* for a person or a job (root ends in *-c/-cs*).
**Process praise:** "You used the root and the meaning to choose the spelling instead of guessing. That's how good spellers crack new words."
**Revisit later:** add *action* and *musician* to the words-I-own wall; both return in the Lesson 8 sort and the Unit 2 check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** an "is it a job?" prompt card; provide the root with the change already made (e.g. *complet-*).
- **Stretch (challenge):** spell *politician* from *politics* and *optician* from *optics*, explaining the *-cs → -cian* pattern.

**Extension (if time allows):** find three more `/shun/` job words (*beautician, technician, mathematician*) and add them to the wall.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly spells at least two *-tion* and two *-cian* words and explains the job clue.

## Lesson 7 — -ssion and -sion (double-s roots & /zhun/)
**English · Year 4 · Unit 2** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose *-ssion* or *-sion* using the root."

**You will need:** root cards (*discuss, permit, divide, confuse, explode*); a root-clue chart (*-ss/-mit → -ssion*; *-d/-de/-se → -sion, often /zhun/*); spelling sheet.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Lesson 6 — `/shun/` has four spellings; use the root clue; *-tion* default, *-cian* jobs.
**Key vocabulary:** `/zhun/` (the softer, buzzing version of the ending), *root clue*, *double s*.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** The last two disguises. **-ssion** keeps the root's double *s* (*discuss→discussion*) or comes from *-mit* roots (*permit→permission*). **-sion** comes from *-d/-de/-se* roots and often sounds like `/zhun/` (*divide→division, confuse→confusion, explode→explosion*). KB trick: **keep the root's double *s*.** Pre-empt *discusion* (one *s*) and over-using *-tion*.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Last two disguises! Say *discuss*… now *discussion* — hear how the double *s* from *discuss* stays in? And listen to this one: *division* — it doesn't even sound like `/shun/`, it's softer, almost `/zhun/`. The root tells us which to use. Let's finish unmasking the `/shun/` family."

**Show:** *discuss → discussion* with both *s*s circled; *divide → division* beside it.  **Do:** say *discussion* and *division*, exaggerating the double *s* vs the soft `/zhun/`.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From yesterday — what spelling do we use for a job, like a person who plays music?" → *listen for:* *-cian* (musician).
*(If shaky: recap "default *-tion*, jobs *-cian*" in one line before adding the new two.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Disguise three: **-ssion.** When the root already has a double *s* — like *discuss* — we KEEP it: *discuss → discussion.* Roots ending in *-mit* swap to *-ssion* too: *permit → permission.* Disguise four: **-sion.** This comes from roots ending in *-d, -de* or *-se*, and it often sounds like `/zhun/`: *divide → division*, *confuse → confusion*, *explode → explosion.* My trick: look at how the root ends. Double *s* or *-mit* → *-ssion.* *-d/-de/-se* → *-sion.*"

**Show / Do:** build *discuss → discussion* (keep the *ss*), then *divide → division* (change *-de* to *-sion*); display the root-clue chart with arrows.
**Emphasize:** "Keep the root's double *s* → *-ssion*. Roots ending *-d/-de/-se* → *-sion*."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Choose with me. *Permit* — does it end in double *s* or *-mit*? So…?" (→ *permission*) "*Confuse* — ends in *-se*, so…?" (→ *confusion*) "*Explode* — ends in *-de*, so…?" (→ *explosion*)

**Do:** child uses the root-clue chart to choose and spell each.
**Ask:** "How do you know *confusion* is *-sion* and not *-ssion*?" → *listen for:* the root *confuse* ends in *-se*, and it sounds like `/zhun/`.

### ⏱ 12–15 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Spell the maths word for sharing into groups. And spell the noun from *discuss*."
- ✅ **Expected:** *division*; *discussion*. Then probe: *"Why one s in division but two in discussion?"*
- ❌ **If "discusion" (one s):** missed the root's double *s*. → **fix:** "spell the root: *d-i-s-c-u-s-s*. Keep both *s*s when you add *-ion* → *discussion.*"
- ❌ **If "divition":** over-used *-tion*. → **fix:** "the root *divide* ends in *-de*, and it sounds soft like `/zhun/` — that's a *-sion* word: *division.*"

### ⏱ 15–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child spells five using the root clue: *discuss→discussion, permit→permission (-ssion); divide→division, confuse→confusion, explode→explosion (-sion).*
**Watch for:** single *s* in *discussion*; *-tion* used for `/zhun/` words; not changing *-de/-se* correctly.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't spell it; 2) name *why* — "let's check how the root ends"; 3) step down — spell the root first, then look at its ending on the chart; 4) ONE hint — "double *s* in the root stays"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just spell the root *discuss*"; 6) keep it safe — "Roots are the key — sound out the root first, then add the ending."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "When do we keep a double *s*, and when do we use *-sion*?" → *listen for:* keep *ss* when the root has it (*discuss→discussion*); *-sion* for *-d/-de/-se* roots, often `/zhun/`.
**Process praise:** "You let the root decide every time instead of guessing — that double-*s* trick will save you a hundred spellings."
**Revisit later:** add *division* and *discussion* to the words-I-own wall; both anchor the Lesson 8 sort and the Unit 2 check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** the root-clue chart on the desk; spell the root first as a separate step.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain *why* *discussion* has double *s* but *division* has one, and spell *admission* (from *admit*) and *decision* (from *decide*).

**Extension (if time allows):** sort a mixed pile of `/shun/` words into all four spellings as a sneak preview of Lesson 8.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly spells at least one *-ssion* (keeping the double *s*) and two *-sion* words, explaining the root clue.

## Lesson 9 — Summarising one paragraph (the main idea)
**English · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Reading: summarising & writer's effect)** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can say the main idea of a paragraph in one sentence."

**You will need:** the hedgehog paragraph printed big (*"Hedgehogs are small, spiny mammals. They sleep all day and hunt insects at night. In winter they hibernate, curling up in leaves to stay warm."*); two more short non-fiction paragraphs; a "headline" strip for writing one-sentence summaries; for support, three candidate main-idea sentences.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 — each paragraph holds one idea; retrieval (finding facts in a text).
**Key vocabulary:** *summary* (a short version saying what a text is mostly about), *main idea* (what the paragraph is mostly about), *detail/example* (a small supporting point).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A summary is the **headline**, not the whole story. The move: read → ask "what is this MOSTLY about?" → say it in ONE sentence in your own words, dropping small examples. Pre-empt the two KB misconceptions — *retelling everything* (summary ≠ copying) and *picking a tiny detail* as the main idea.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Imagine telling a friend about a whole film in ONE sentence — not every scene, just the big point. That's a summary, and it's a superpower. Today you'll read a paragraph and write its 'headline' — what it's MOSTLY about — in a single sentence."

**Show:** the hedgehog paragraph.  **Do:** read it aloud once at normal pace.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does each paragraph usually hold?" → *listen for:* one main idea / one topic.
*(If shaky: remind — "a paragraph is a chunk about one idea; we're hunting for that one idea.")*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch me find the main idea. Lots of facts — but mostly it's about how hedgehogs live: hunting at night and hibernating. So my one-sentence headline is: *Hedgehogs are night-time insect hunters that hibernate in winter.* Notice I did NOT copy every line — I kept the big points and left out the tiny ones, and I used my OWN words."

**Show / Do:** underline the two big ideas (hunt at night / hibernate), cross out the small detail "curling up in leaves"; write the headline sentence underneath.
**Emphasize:** "Ask 'what is it MOSTLY about?' Keep the big points, drop the small examples, say it in one sentence."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's summarise one together. Read this paragraph with me." (read second paragraph) "Now the magic question — what's it MOSTLY about? Tell me the big point… now let's squeeze it into one sentence in our own words."

**Do:** child names the main idea; tutor and child draft a one-sentence headline together, deleting any small example that creeps in.
**Ask:** "How do you know that's the main idea and not just one fact from it?" → *listen for:* it covers what the whole paragraph is about, not just one line.

### ⏱ 12–15 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Should a summary include every example from the paragraph?"
- ✅ **Expected:** No — keep only the main points. Then probe: *"So what do we leave out?"* (the small details/examples).
- ❌ **If "yes, include everything":** thinks summarising = copying. → **fix:** "if we keep everything it's as long as the original — a summary is the BIG point only, one sentence. What could we cut?"
- ❌ **If they pick a tiny detail as the main idea (e.g. 'they curl up in leaves'):** grabbed the first/most interesting fact. → **fix:** "is the WHOLE paragraph about curling up in leaves? No — that's one small detail. What is it mostly about?"

### ⏱ 15–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child writes a one-sentence main-idea "headline" for two fresh paragraphs, in their own words, leaving out small examples.
**Watch for:** copying a whole sentence from the text; choosing a vivid detail over the main idea; a summary that's two or three sentences long.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "I think a small detail snuck in / this is a copy"; 3) step down — for support, offer three candidate headlines and have the child choose the best; 4) ONE hint — "ask: what is the WHOLE paragraph mostly about?"; 5) re-ask smaller — "tell me the big point in just a few words first"; 6) keep it safe — "Summarising is a skill we build — let's find the headline together."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What's the magic question you ask to find the main idea?" → *listen for:* what is this MOSTLY about?
**Process praise:** "You squeezed a whole paragraph into one clear headline and used your own words — that's real summarising, not copying."
**Revisit later:** Lesson 10 builds straight on this — combining paragraph headlines into a whole-text summary; revisited in the Unit 3 check and Lesson 19.

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** choose the best main-idea sentence from three options; mark the main idea with a highlighter before writing.
- **Stretch (challenge):** summarise a paragraph dropping ALL examples, in the fewest words possible.

**Extension (if time allows):** turn a paragraph from a favourite non-fiction book into a one-sentence headline.
**Assessment evidence:** child writes a one-sentence main idea (own words, no copying) for at least one paragraph, keeping the big point and dropping examples.

## Lesson 10 — Summarising across a text
**English · Year 4 · Unit 3** | **Duration:** 20 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can combine paragraph main ideas into one overall summary."

**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Zoom out — first a one-sentence headline per paragraph, then JOIN those headlines into one short overall summary. The "one-breath recap" of the whole text. Pre-empt re-telling everything; keep only the big points from each paragraph.

---

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Yesterday you wrote the headline for ONE paragraph. Today, the big challenge: recap a whole text to me in one breath. That's a summary of the WHOLE thing — and it's exactly what you do when you tell someone what a book is about."

*(If shaky: model one quick headline on paragraph 1 before moving on.)*

### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Two steps. Step one — I write a one-sentence headline for EACH paragraph, like yesterday. Paragraph one is mostly about where bees live — *Bees live together in a hive.* Paragraph two is mostly about food — *Bees collect nectar to make honey.* Step two — I JOIN those headlines into one short overall summary, smoothing them together: *Bees live together in a hive and collect nectar to make honey, which helps plants grow.* I keep only the big points from each paragraph — no tiny details."

**Emphasize:** "Headline each paragraph first, then join the headlines into one short overall summary."

### ⏱ 8–12 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's combine two together. Here are two headlines we made. How could we join them into ONE smooth sentence or two? Watch out — we keep the big points only."

**Do:** child joins two paragraph headlines into a combined summary, with the tutor prompting to drop any small detail.
**Ask:** "How do you know your summary covers the WHOLE text?" → *listen for:* it includes the main idea from each paragraph.

### ⏱ 12–15 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What is a summary?"
- ✅ **Expected:** A short version saying what a text is mostly about. Then probe: *"And how do we make one for a whole text?"* (headline each paragraph, then join).
- ❌ **If "it's when you write the whole thing again":** thinks summary = copying. → **fix:** "if it's as long as the text, it's not a summary. It's the BIG points only — one headline per paragraph, joined."
- ❌ **If the summary misses a paragraph entirely:** lost a main idea. → **fix:** "let's check — did we catch the big point of EVERY paragraph? Which one did we miss?"

### ⏱ 15–18 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** child summarises a 2–3 paragraph text: writes one headline sentence per paragraph, then combines them into one short overall summary.
**Watch for:** a summary built from small details; one paragraph's idea missing; the overall summary as long as the original.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't write it; 2) name *why* — "I think a paragraph's big point is missing / a detail crept in"; 3) step down — for support, give the paragraph headlines pre-written and just combine them; 4) ONE hint — "one big point per paragraph, then join"; 5) re-ask smaller — "just give me the headline for paragraph two"; 6) keep it safe — "Recapping a whole text is hard — one paragraph at a time."

### ⏱ 18–20 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What are the two steps to summarise a whole text?" → *listen for:* headline each paragraph, then join the headlines.
**Process praise:** "You caught the big point of every paragraph and joined them smoothly — that's a one-breath recap of the whole thing. Brilliant zooming out."
**Revisit later:** central to the Unit 3 check and the Lesson 19 retrieval round; reuse the skill when summarising explanation texts in Unit 5.

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** paragraph main ideas provided on strips — the child only combines them; sentence starter "*This text is about…*".
- **Stretch (challenge):** turn the overall summary into a single newspaper headline of under eight words.

**Extension (if time allows):** summarise a non-fiction page from a favourite book in two sentences for a family member.
**Assessment evidence:** child writes a one-sentence main idea per paragraph and joins them into one short overall summary that covers the whole text without copying.
