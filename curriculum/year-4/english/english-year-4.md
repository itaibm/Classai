## Year overview

Year 4 is the year children become **readers of whole novels who talk like critics and writers who make deliberate choices**. Every unit is built around a real, complete text: children read (and are read) the whole book, discuss it in depth, and write in genres that grow out of it. Grammar, punctuation and spelling are taught *in service of writing* — a relative clause is learned because a character needs describing, a modal verb because an argument needs weighing — never as test drills.

**Class-text spine (whole books, with author and why):**
- **Term 1 — Survival, belonging and justice.** *The Wild Robot* by **Peter Brown** (USA) — short chapters, a gentle non-human narrator and a rich island setting make it ideal for teaching setting, character and point of view; links to science (life cycles, habitats). **"The Highwayman"** by **Alfred Noyes** — the great narrative poem for performance, rhythm and imagery. *Journey to Jo'burg* by **Beverley Naidoo** (South Africa) — a short, powerful novel about two children under apartheid that opens discussion of fairness and courage and teaches diary and letter writing.
- **Term 2 — Stories that explain the world.** *The Orchard Book of Greek Myths* by **Geraldine McCaughrean** plus traditional tales from West Africa (Anansi), Polynesia (Māui) and the Norse world — myth structure and oral storytelling. *Where the Mountain Meets the Moon* by **Grace Lin** (Chinese folklore; Newbery Honor) — a quest woven with stories-within-the-story, perfect for structure and figurative language. *Cosmic* by **Frank Cottrell-Boyce** (UK) — a hilarious first-person voice (a 12-year-old who looks like an adult ends up in space) for voice, dialogue and humour; links to science (Earth and space). A world-poetry unit (Langston Hughes, Grace Nichols, Benjamin Zephaniah, Matsuo Bashō).
- **Term 3 — Voices that change things.** *The Boy Who Harnessed the Wind* (Young Readers' Edition) by **William Kamkwamba and Bryan Mealer** (Malawi) — true story for biography, explanation, persuasion and formal writing. *Esperanza Rising* by **Pam Muñoz Ryan** (Mexico/USA) — a rich character-change novel for inference, theme and response writing; links to Spanish. Poetry of imagery and form ("The Listeners", "Jabberwocky", "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening"), then a **book-club** unit where children choose a whole novel from a curated list and write and present reviews.

**Strands in every unit.** Reading (vocabulary, inference, prediction, summarising, author's craft, comparing texts), writing (plan → draft → revise → edit → publish), grammar for writing, spelling (one explicit spelling/morphology lesson most weeks, with 10-minute daily practice outside the lesson), **handwriting** (a fluent, legible joined hand at speed; choosing print for labels and captions; publishing lessons focus on presentation), and **speaking & listening** (discussion roles, debate, performance poetry, book talks). Children also read independently every day (20+ minutes) from a class library, and hear the class novel read aloud.

**What Year 4 assumes (from Year 3):** fluent decoding and reading at a good pace; fronted adverbials with commas; possessive apostrophes (singular and plural); direct speech punctuation; paragraphs organised around a theme; simple similes and metaphors; the /shun/ spellings (-tion, -sion, -ssion, -cian) and the Years 3–4 statutory word list; summarising a paragraph; writing settings, characters and explanation texts.

**What Year 5 relies on from Year 4:** relative clauses, modal verbs and parenthesis used by choice; cohesion across paragraphs; the -able/-ible, -ant/-ent, -cious/-cial families and most of the Years 5–6 word list; point-evidence-explanation answers about a writer's word choice; confident persuasive and formal letter writing; stamina to read and discuss a whole novel. Year 5 then adds the passive voice, semicolons and colons, the subjunctive and formal register, précis and longer analytical writing.

## Unit 1 — The Wild Robot: setting, character and point of view

**Essential question:** How does a writer make us care about a character who isn't even human?

**Key vocabulary:** narrator, point of view, setting, atmosphere, character, motive, inference, evidence, expanded noun phrase, relative clause, relative pronoun, cohesion, paragraph

### Lesson 1 — Meeting the book: prediction and first impressions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make and justify predictions using the cover, blurb and opening chapters." (UK NC Y5–6 reading — predict; CCSS RL.4.1)
- **Hook:** A mystery crate on the desk labelled "ROZZUM unit 7134 — handle with care". "What might be inside, and where did it wash up?"
- **Key activity:** I model a prediction with "because" using the cover; we read the opening chapters aloud (the storm, the crates on the rocks); you write two predictions with evidence on sticky notes for a "prediction wall" we will revisit.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is one prediction you made, and which clue in the text supports it?" → A prediction linked to a specific detail (e.g. the storm and the ship suggest Roz arrived by accident).
- **Differentiation:** support: prediction sentence frame "I think… because the text says…". / stretch: make a prediction about theme, not just plot (e.g. "This will be about whether machines can belong in nature").
- **Materials:** class set of *The Wild Robot* (Peter Brown), a cardboard crate prop, sticky notes, prediction wall.
- **Joy:** Unboxing the "robot crate" and starting the class prediction wall.

### Lesson 2 — Setting through the senses
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find and explain how the writer builds the island setting through senses and detail." (UK NC Y5–6 reading — language for effect; CCSS RL.4.3)
- **Hook:** Play a recording of waves, wind and gulls with eyes closed: "Where are you? What can you see?"
- **Key activity:** Reread a setting passage; highlight sight, sound, touch and smell words; build a class "senses grid"; explain one choice using "The writer uses… to show…".
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose one phrase that makes the island feel wild. Explain why that word works better than a plainer one." → e.g. a strong verb or precise noun, with its effect on the reader.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-highlighted extract with sense-colour key. / stretch: compare how the setting feels before and after the storm.
- **Materials:** extract photocopies, highlighters, audio of seashore sounds, senses grid.
- **Joy:** Build a "soundscape" of the island with voices and body percussion.

### Lesson 3 — Expanded noun phrases for vivid settings
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can build expanded noun phrases with adjectives and prepositional phrases to describe precisely." (UK NC Y5–6 grammar — noun phrases; CCSS L.4.1d)
- **Hook:** "'A tree' vs 'an ancient pine with twisted roots clinging to the cliff'. Which one do you see?"
- **Key activity:** Noun phrase "building blocks": determiner + adjectives + noun + prepositional phrase. We build five together from photos of the island's landscapes; you write six for your own island.
- **Check for understanding:** "Improve 'the rock' in two ways and explain which details you added." → e.g. "the jagged grey rock beneath the waterfall" — adjectives before, prepositional phrase after.
- **Differentiation:** support: word banks sorted by position (adjective / noun / preposition). / stretch: cut unnecessary adjectives — choose one precise noun instead ("boulder" not "big rock").
- **Materials:** island landscape photos (forest, cliffs, meadow, beach), phrase-building cards.
- **Joy:** "Phrase Jenga" — each correct building block adds a Jenga brick to a tower.

### Lesson 4 — Character: what Roz does, says and thinks
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can infer a character's traits from actions, speech and thoughts and support them with evidence." (UK NC Y5–6 reading — inference; CCSS RL.4.3)
- **Hook:** "Is Roz clever, scary, or kind? You must prove it."
- **Key activity:** Role-on-the-wall: outside the outline, what others see; inside, what Roz thinks. Collect evidence quotes; model point + evidence + explanation.
- **Check for understanding:** "Name one of Roz's traits and prove it with a quotation or event." → e.g. "curious — she watches and copies the animals to learn how to survive".
- **Differentiation:** support: trait word bank with definitions. / stretch: identify a trait that changes across the chapters read so far.
- **Materials:** role-on-the-wall poster, sticky notes, the novel.
- **Joy:** Hot-seat "Roz" — a volunteer answers in a robot voice.

### Lesson 5 — Spelling: -able and -ible
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose -able or -ible using the root-word clue and explain exceptions." (UK NC Y5–6 spelling; CCSS L.4.2d)
- **Hook:** "Adorable robot, terrible storm — why does one end in -able and one in -ible?"
- **Key activity:** Sort words: -able usually follows a complete root word (accept → acceptable, depend → dependable; drop the e in adore → adorable, but keep it after soft c/g: noticeable, changeable); -ible usually follows a root that isn't a word on its own (terrible, visible, possible). Note exceptions (sensible, responsible). Practise in dictated sentences about the book.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does 'noticeable' keep its e when 'adorable' drops it?" → The e keeps the c soft (/s/); without it, it would read 'notic-able' with a hard /k/.
- **Differentiation:** support: 8 core words with root-word boxes. / stretch: -ably/-ibly adverbs (dependably, visibly) and a rule-exception hunt.
- **Materials:** word-sort cards, dictionaries, spelling journals.
- **Joy:** "Robot sorter" — act as a robot sorting word cards into two bins at speed.

### Lesson 6 — Point of view: a robot narrator's world
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the third-person narrator lets us see the world through Roz's eyes." (UK NC Y5–6 reading — narrator; CCSS RL.4.6)
- **Hook:** "How would a robot describe a sunset if it didn't know the word 'beautiful'?"
- **Key activity:** Compare a passage narrated close to Roz with the animals' view; identify first vs third person; discuss what the narrator tells us that Roz can't know.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is The Wild Robot told in first or third person? How can you tell, and what does that let the writer do?" → Third person ("she", "Roz"); the narrator can show things Roz doesn't understand yet and sometimes speaks directly to the reader.
- **Differentiation:** support: pronoun-highlighting in a short extract. / stretch: rewrite a paragraph in Roz's first-person voice and discuss what changes.
- **Materials:** extracts, highlighters, the novel.
- **Joy:** "Robot eyes" — describe a classroom object as a robot would (precise, literal, no feelings words).

### Lesson 7 — Relative clauses to add information
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add relative clauses with who, which, that, whose, where and when." (UK NC Y5–6 grammar — relative clauses; CCSS L.4.1a)
- **Hook:** "Brightbill, who was only a gosling, followed Roz everywhere." What does the middle part add?
- **Key activity:** I do: match relative pronouns to nouns (who — people/characters, which — things/animals in formal writing, whose — belonging, where — place, when — time, that — essential information). We do: extend character and setting sentences. You do: six sentences about the island.
- **Check for understanding:** "Add a relative clause to 'The island had a meadow' using where." → e.g. "The island had a meadow where the geese gathered each spring."
- **Differentiation:** support: pronoun cards and sentence starters. / stretch: use whose correctly in two sentences ("the goose whose nest was destroyed…").
- **Materials:** relative-pronoun cards, sentence strips, the novel.
- **Joy:** "Sentence surgery" — insert relative clauses with scissors and tape.

### Lesson 8 — Commas and relative clauses
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can decide whether a relative clause needs commas using the 'lift-out' test." (UK NC Y5–6 punctuation; CCSS L.4.2)
- **Hook:** "My sister, who lives in Nairobi, is a doctor" vs "My sister who lives in Nairobi is a doctor". Is there a difference?
- **Key activity:** If the clause is extra information and the sentence still makes sense without it, use commas; if it tells us *which one*, no commas. 'That' only introduces essential clauses.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain the difference in meaning between the two sister sentences." → With commas: I have one sister and she happens to live in Nairobi. Without: I have more than one sister and I mean the one in Nairobi.
- **Differentiation:** support: colour-code the clause and physically cover it to test. / stretch: write a pair of sentences where commas change the meaning.
- **Materials:** sentence cards, sticky notes to cover clauses.
- **Joy:** "Lift-out magic" — cover the clause with a sticky note and see if the sentence survives.

### Lesson 9 — Summarising chapters
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can summarise a group of chapters in no more than 40 words, keeping only the key events." (UK NC Y5–6 reading — summarise; CCSS RL.4.2)
- **Hook:** "Tell me the last five chapters in exactly one breath."
- **Key activity:** Model "who, wanted, but, so, then"; cross out details that aren't essential; compare summaries and vote on the clearest.
- **Check for understanding:** "What did you leave out of your summary and why?" → Minor details that don't change the main events or the character's journey.
- **Differentiation:** support: summary frame with sentence starters. / stretch: 20-word summary, then a one-sentence theme statement.
- **Materials:** summary frames, the novel, timers.
- **Joy:** "One-breath summaries" contest.

### Lesson 10 — Planning a setting description
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a setting description for my own wild place, organising ideas into paragraphs." (UK NC Y5–6 writing — plan; CCSS W.4.3)
- **Hook:** Show photos of real wild places — the Galápagos, a Norwegian fjord, a Borneo rainforest: "Your robot has just washed up here."
- **Key activity:** Choose a place; plan in three paragraphs (arrival / exploring / a change in weather or light); gather expanded noun phrases, senses and a relative clause for each.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why have you put your ideas in that paragraph order?" → A logical movement (e.g. from wide view to close detail, or from calm to storm).
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture-based planning frame. / stretch: plan a change in atmosphere across the paragraphs.
- **Materials:** photos of wild places from several continents, planning frames.
- **Joy:** "Choose your island" — pick a real wild place from a world map.

### Lesson 11 — Drafting a setting description
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draft a vivid setting description using noun phrases, senses and relative clauses." (UK NC Y5–6 writing — draft; CCSS W.4.3d)
- **Hook:** Read Peter Brown's island description once more: "Steal the craft, not the words."
- **Key activity:** Shared write of the first sentences; children draft independently for 20 minutes with a success-criteria checklist.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me a sentence where you used a relative clause to add a detail. What does it add?" → A correct relative clause adding precise information about the setting.
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence starters for each paragraph. / stretch: include a sentence that shows atmosphere without naming a feeling.
- **Materials:** plans, checklists, writing journals.
- **Joy:** Writing to calm ambient sounds from their chosen place.

### Lesson 12 — Revising: improving word choice and cohesion
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can revise my writing by improving vocabulary and linking paragraphs." (UK NC Y5–6 writing — evaluate and edit; CCSS W.4.5)
- **Hook:** "Writers never finish on the first draft — even Peter Brown revised."
- **Key activity:** Partner feedback with "two stars and a wish"; use a thesaurus carefully (check the meaning fits); add adverbials of place and time to link paragraphs.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show one change you made and explain why it improves the writing." → A specific revision with reasoning.
- **Differentiation:** support: a short list of revision targets. / stretch: cut 10% of words while keeping meaning.
- **Materials:** drafts, coloured pens, thesauruses.
- **Joy:** "Word upgrade shop" — trade in tired words for precise ones.

### Lesson 13 — Reading: Roz learns to belong
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Roz changes from an outsider to part of the island community, with evidence." (UK NC Y5–6 reading — character change; CCSS RL.4.3)
- **Hook:** "At first the animals called Roz a 'monster'. What changed?"
- **Key activity:** A "character journey" line: plot key moments (adopting Brightbill, learning animal language, helping others) with quotations; discuss causes of change.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the most important moment in Roz's change and why?" → A justified choice (e.g. caring for Brightbill made her a mother figure and others began to trust her).
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-selected moments to order. / stretch: explain how the animals' view of Roz changes too.
- **Materials:** journey-line template, the novel.
- **Joy:** "Freeze-frame" key moments in small groups.

### Lesson 14 — Spelling and vocabulary: prefixes dis-, mis-, re-, de-, over-
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use verb prefixes to change meaning and explain what each prefix does." (UK NC Y5–6 vocabulary — verb prefixes; CCSS L.4.4b)
- **Hook:** "Roz can activate — can she deactivate, reactivate, overactivate?"
- **Key activity:** Prefix meanings: dis- (not/opposite), mis- (wrongly), re- (again), de- (remove/reverse), over- (too much). Build words and use them in sentences about the story.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between 'misplace' and 'replace'?" → Misplace means put in the wrong place (lose); replace means put back again or swap for another.
- **Differentiation:** support: prefix-root flip cards. / stretch: find words where the prefix isn't really a prefix (e.g. 'dish', 'real') and explain.
- **Materials:** prefix cards, root-word cards, dictionaries.
- **Joy:** "Prefix machine" — feed a verb in, pull a lever, see new words come out.

### Lesson 15 — Non-fiction: animals of the island (research)
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can research an animal from the novel, taking notes in my own words." (UK NC Y5–6 writing — note-taking; CCSS W.4.7)
- **Hook:** "Are the real geese, foxes and bears like the ones in the book?"
- **Key activity:** Model skimming and scanning a non-fiction text; notes as key words, not sentences; organise notes under headings (habitat, diet, life cycle, adaptations). Links to science life cycles.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me a note in your own words and the sentence you took it from. Why is your note better?" → A shorter, own-words note that keeps the key fact.
- **Differentiation:** support: a note-taking grid with headings. / stretch: compare two sources and note where they disagree.
- **Materials:** non-fiction books and printed fact pages (e.g. Canada goose, red fox, black bear, beaver), note grids.
- **Joy:** "Fact or fiction?" — spot what the novel invented and what is true to nature.

### Lesson 16 — Non-chronological report: structure and features
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify the structure and features of a non-chronological report." (UK NC Y5–6 writing — non-fiction forms; CCSS W.4.2)
- **Hook:** Two texts about foxes — a story and a report. "How are they built differently?"
- **Key activity:** Features hunt: title, introduction, subheadings, present tense, technical vocabulary, facts not opinions, diagrams and captions. Plan the report from research notes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a report use subheadings but a story doesn't?" → Reports are organised by topic so readers can find information; stories are organised by time/events.
- **Differentiation:** support: labelled model report. / stretch: add a "Did you know?" fact box and a glossary.
- **Materials:** model reports, features checklist, research notes.
- **Joy:** "Report detectives" feature hunt with stickers.

### Lesson 17 — Writing the report
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a clear non-chronological report with paragraphs under subheadings." (UK NC Y5–6 writing — draft; CCSS W.4.2)
- **Hook:** "The island's animals need a field guide for visiting robots."
- **Key activity:** Write the introduction together; children write paragraphs under 3–4 subheadings using notes; include one relative clause and technical vocabulary.
- **Check for understanding:** "Point to a sentence that is a fact. How do you know it isn't an opinion?" → It can be checked/proved (e.g. "Red foxes are mostly active at night").
- **Differentiation:** support: paragraph frames per subheading. / stretch: include a comparison with a similar animal on another continent.
- **Materials:** plans, notes, writing journals.
- **Joy:** Each report becomes a page in the class "Field Guide to the Wild Island".

### Lesson 18 — Grammar: fronted adverbials and cohesion (retrieval)
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use fronted adverbials of time, place and number to link ideas across paragraphs." (UK NC Y5–6 grammar — cohesion; CCSS L.4.3)
- **Hook:** "Without joining words, a story is a pile of bricks with no cement."
- **Key activity:** Retrieve fronted adverbials and commas from Year 3; sort adverbials into time (later that winter), place (beyond the cliffs), number (firstly); add them to link paragraphs in a report and a narrative.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which adverbials suit a report and which suit a story? Why?" → Reports often use number/logic (Firstly, In addition); stories use time/place (As dawn broke, Deep in the forest).
- **Differentiation:** support: adverbial word mat. / stretch: vary adverbials so no two paragraphs start the same way.
- **Materials:** adverbial sorting cards, drafts.
- **Joy:** "Adverbial relay" — teams build a story one fronted adverbial at a time.

### Lesson 19 — Reading: the winter and the ending
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can discuss the novel's ending and give an opinion supported by evidence." (UK NC Y5–6 reading — discuss and debate; CCSS SL.4.1)
- **Hook:** Revisit the prediction wall: "Which predictions came true?"
- **Key activity:** Read the final chapters; structured discussion (agree/build/challenge prompts): was the ending happy, sad, or both?
- **Check for understanding:** "Was the ending happy or sad? Give a reason from the text and respond to someone who disagrees." → A justified opinion that acknowledges another view.
- **Differentiation:** support: discussion sentence stems. / stretch: explain how the ending sets up a sequel and what the author wants us to feel.
- **Materials:** the novel, discussion prompt cards, prediction wall.
- **Joy:** Revealing and scoring the prediction wall.

### Lesson 20 — Writing: a new chapter from an animal's viewpoint (plan and draft)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draft a short narrative scene told from an island animal's point of view." (UK NC Y5–6 writing — narrative; CCSS W.4.3)
- **Hook:** "What did Chitchat the squirrel really think of Roz?"
- **Key activity:** Plan a scene (problem → attempt → change); choose first person for the animal; include setting description and a relative clause; draft.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does your animal's voice show its personality?" → Specific word choices and thoughts that fit the animal.
- **Differentiation:** support: a story mountain template with the scene chosen. / stretch: include a moment where the animal misunderstands Roz, creating dramatic irony.
- **Materials:** story mountain templates, character cards.
- **Joy:** "Animal voices" — read openings aloud in character.

### Lesson 21 — Editing and publishing with fluent handwriting
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can proof-read for spelling and punctuation and publish in a neat, fluent joined hand." (UK NC Y5–6 writing — proof-read; handwriting)
- **Hook:** "Publishers need clean copy — you're the editor."
- **Key activity:** Proof-reading checklist (capital letters, commas, relative clause commas, spelling list words); handwriting warm-up (joins for -able/-ible); publish the final scene.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me an error you found and fixed. How did you spot it?" → A specific correction and the strategy (reading aloud, checking a word list).
- **Differentiation:** support: a 4-item checklist and a partner. / stretch: edit a partner's work with helpful comments.
- **Materials:** checklists, handwriting pens, publishing paper.
- **Joy:** A class "Wild Island Anthology" with illustrations.

### Lesson 22 — Unit 1 review: book talk and assessment
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can talk about the novel, its characters and themes, and show my learning in a short assessment." (UK NC Y5–6 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Would you recommend The Wild Robot to a 7-year-old? A 12-year-old? Why?"
- **Key activity:** Book talk in groups; short assessment (inference, vocabulary, relative clauses, -able/-ible).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the book really about, beneath the plot?" → A theme (belonging, nature vs technology, motherhood/kindness) with evidence.
- **Differentiation:** support: theme word bank. / stretch: compare the theme with another story they know.
- **Materials:** assessment sheet, discussion cards.
- **Joy:** Vote for the "Wild Robot award" for the best character.

**End-of-unit check:** Write one paragraph describing a wild setting that includes an expanded noun phrase, a relative clause with correct commas and a fronted adverbial; answer one inference question about Roz with evidence.

## Unit 2 — Narrative poetry: "The Highwayman"

**Essential question:** How can a poem tell a story that feels more dramatic than prose?

**Key vocabulary:** narrative poem, ballad, stanza, rhyme scheme, rhythm, repetition, refrain, imagery, simile, metaphor, personification, onomatopoeia, performance

### Lesson 23 — First hearing: the story of the poem
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can retell the story of 'The Highwayman' and describe how it made me feel." (UK NC Y5–6 reading — poetry; CCSS RL.4.5)
- **Hook:** Lights down, a candle (LED), hoofbeats tapped on the desk: "The wind was a torrent of darkness among the gusty trees…"
- **Key activity:** Listen to a performance of the poem (teacher or recording); retell in a six-box storyboard (the highwayman, Bess, Tim the ostler, the soldiers, the warning, the ending).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does Bess pull the trigger? What does it tell us about her?" → To warn the highwayman of the trap; she sacrifices herself out of love and courage.
- **Differentiation:** support: storyboard with picture prompts. / stretch: identify the moment of highest tension and explain.
- **Materials:** copies of "The Highwayman" (Alfred Noyes), LED candle, storyboard sheets.
- **Joy:** The atmospheric first reading.

### Lesson 24 — Vocabulary: old and unusual words
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can work out the meaning of unfamiliar words using context and dictionaries." (UK NC Y5–6 reading — vocabulary; CCSS RL.4.4)
- **Hook:** "What is a 'galleon', an 'ostler', a 'casement' — and why does the poet use them?"
- **Key activity:** Context clues first, then dictionaries; create a glossary with pictures; discuss how old words set the time period (18th century).
- **Check for understanding:** "What does 'casement' mean and which clue in the poem helped?" → A window (that opens on hinges); Bess leans from it and looks out.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture glossary cards. / stretch: explain the metaphor "The moon was a ghostly galleon tossed upon cloudy seas".
- **Materials:** dictionaries, glossary template, images.
- **Joy:** "Word wanted" posters for mysterious words.

### Lesson 25 — Imagery and figurative language
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify metaphor, simile and personification and explain their effect." (UK NC Y5–6 reading — figurative language; CCSS L.4.5a)
- **Hook:** "The road was a ribbon of moonlight." Is the road really a ribbon?
- **Key activity:** Find three metaphors in the first stanza; distinguish simile/metaphor/personification; explain the picture each creates with "This makes me imagine… because…".

### Lesson 38 — Spelling: -ant/-ance/-ancy and -ent/-ence/-ency

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Reads and discusses a whole novel** — sustains a novel of 200+ pages and discusses character, theme and author's choices with evidence. *See it:* in a book-club or seminar, the child makes a claim and supports it with a quotation.
2. **Infers and explains with evidence** — writes a point-evidence-explain paragraph about language or structure. *See it:* a PEE paragraph explaining a symbol or technique accurately.
3. **Summarises and compares** — summarises a chapter in 40 words or fewer and compares two texts. *See it:* a comparison paragraph using "whereas"/"similarly".
4. **Writes narrative with craft** — controls setting, character, dialogue and a distinct voice across cohesive paragraphs. *See it:* a published story with purposeful dialogue and a clear narrative voice.
5. **Writes non-fiction for purpose** — a report, explanation, newspaper report, biography and persuasive letter with correct features and formality. *See it:* a formal persuasive letter with reasons, evidence and a counter-argument.
6. **Uses Year 4 grammar by choice** — relative clauses (with correct commas), modal verbs and adverbs of possibility, parenthesis (brackets, dashes, commas), commas for clarity, the perfect form, direct and reported speech. *See it:* the child explains why they chose dashes rather than brackets in their own writing.
7. **Spells accurately** — -able/-ible, -ant/-ent, -cious/-tious, -cial/-tial, -fer words, ei/ie, silent letters, hyphens and most of the Years 5–6 list. *See it:* 90%+ accuracy in unseen dictation, with the rule explained.
8. **Speaks and performs** — recites poems with expression, debates respectfully, gives a two-minute talk. *See it:* a book talk or TED-style talk delivered with eye contact and clear structure.
9. **Handwriting** — legible, fluent joined handwriting at speed for published work. *See it:* a published piece in consistent, well-formed joined script.

## Teacher guidance

**How the lessons work.** Read the class novel aloud as well as having children read — expressive read-aloud models fluency and keeps momentum through whole books. Most weeks follow a rhythm: 2–3 reading/discussion lessons, 1–2 writing lessons (plan → draft → revise/edit → publish), and 1 grammar or spelling lesson tied to the writing. Add 10 minutes a day outside lessons for spelling practice and 20 minutes for independent reading.

**Common misconceptions (and fixes).**
- *Commas around every relative clause* — use the lift-out test: only non-essential clauses take commas; 'that' clauses never do.
- *Parenthesis that can't be lifted out* — check the sentence still makes sense without it.
- *Confusing modal verbs of possibility and obligation* ('might' vs 'must') — use the certainty line.
- *Forgetting to shift tense and pronouns in reported speech* — practise with step-by-step conversion cards.
- *-able/-ible guessed* — ask "is the root a whole word?", then check exceptions (sensible, responsible).
- *'i before e' applied to every ei word* — the guideline is for the /ee/ sound only; learn exceptions (seize, protein).
- *Summaries that retell everything* — cap the word count and use "who/wanted/but/so/then".
- *'I liked it' reviews and answers* — insist on point-evidence-explain.
- *Persuasion = shouting* — teach fair vs manipulative techniques and always include a counter-argument.
- *Formal = long words* — formality is about precision, politeness and no slang or contractions, not about length.

**Differentiation.** Support means more scaffolding with the *same* rich text (audio-book, pre-teaching vocabulary, guided reading groups, sentence frames); stretch means greater depth (subtext, comparing authors, evaluating choices), not simply more writing.

**Sensitive content.** *Journey to Jo'burg* (apartheid), *Esperanza Rising* (a parent's death, deportations, poverty) and *The Boy Who Harnessed the Wind* (famine) include hardship. Pre-read, prepare children, invite family perspectives and emphasise courage, dignity and agency.

**International notes.** British and US spellings both appear in the world (colour/color, apologise/apologize, practise/practice); teach one consistently and recognise the other. Swap local texts and poets in beside the spine where possible, keeping the whole-book principle.

**Vertical links.** Year 3 built fronted adverbials, possessive apostrophes, /shun/ spellings, summarising and similes; retrieve them rather than re-teach. Year 5 builds on this year's relative clauses, parenthesis and persuasive/formal writing with semicolons, colons, the passive voice, formal register and extended analytical writing.

**Cross-curricular links.** Science (life cycles in *The Wild Robot*; forces and rockets in *Cosmic*; energy in *The Boy Who Harnessed the Wind*), history and geography (South Africa, Mexico/California, Malawi, ancient Greece, China), Spanish (*Esperanza Rising*, Neruda), life skills (fairness, rights, resilience), art (illustrating anthologies).
