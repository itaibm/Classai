# Year 4 Geography (ages 9–10) — Scope & Sequence

> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Written to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Standards reference:
> `knowledge-base/subjects/history-geography/geography-ks2.md`, `misconceptions.md` and `_lesson-banks-humanities.md`.
> Codes: **UK NC KS2 Geography** (locational knowledge, place knowledge, human and physical geography, skills and fieldwork) and **C3** (US College, Career and Civic Life Framework, D2.Geo, Grades 3–5).

## Year overview

Year 4 geography is about **the edges and extremes of the Earth — and how people live with them**. Children study the places where land meets sea (**coasts**), a region of **North America** shaped by one great river (the **Colorado River and the desert Southwest**), the one continent with no permanent population (**Antarctica**), **mountains and life at altitude** in the Andes and the Himalaya, and the **natural resources and energy** that power daily life. The year ends with a **microclimate fieldwork investigation** in the school grounds using real instruments and maps.

Every unit uses **maps** (atlases, globes, digital maps, topographic maps), includes **fieldwork, models or real data**, connects **physical and human geography**, and asks about **sustainability** — how people can live well with the planet.

**Why these topics.** Year 2 already taught latitude and longitude, time zones, the eight compass points, four-figure grid references, rivers, and mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes; Year 3 taught climate zones and biomes, the Amazon, the Mediterranean and trade. Year 4 *uses* those skills and ideas (short retrieval starters) but spends its lessons on places and processes no other year teaches in depth: coasts, a North American region, Antarctica and resources and energy. Tectonic hazards and rivers return in depth in Years 5–6.

**Continents across the spine:** Year 4 covers **North America** (region study), **Antarctica** (continent study) and **South America and Asia** (mountain regions: the Andes of Bolivia and Peru, the Himalaya of Nepal), with coastal case studies from **Africa** (Kenya, Senegal), **Europe** (the Netherlands, Portugal), **Asia** (Bangladesh), **Oceania** (Australia, New Zealand, Tuvalu) and the child's own country.

**What Year 4 assumes (from Years 2–3):** continents and oceans; latitude, longitude, hemispheres and time zones; eight compass points; symbols, keys, scale and four-figure grid references; rivers from source to sea; mountains and plates (introduced in Year 2); weather vs climate; climate zones and biomes; the Amazon and Mediterranean region studies; the fieldwork cycle (question → collect → present → conclude).
**What Year 5 relies on:** confident four-figure grid references and scale on real maps (Year 5 adds six-figure references, contours and GIS); comparing regions fairly with data; the idea that physical processes (waves, rivers, ice) shape land; renewable and non-renewable resources (for trade and globalisation); measuring and mapping fieldwork data.

## Unit 1 — Coasts: where land meets sea

**Essential question:** How do waves and people shape the coast — and how can coastal communities live with a rising sea?

**Key vocabulary:** coast, coastline, wave, tide, erosion, deposition, headland, bay, cliff, cave, arch, stack, stump, beach, spit, longshore drift, mangrove, sea wall, groyne, port, sea level, latitude, longitude

### Lesson 1 — Coasts of the world
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can locate contrasting coasts using latitude and longitude and describe how they differ." (UK NC KS2 locational knowledge — coasts, latitude and longitude; C3 D2.Geo.2.3-5)
- **Hook:** Six photos — Norwegian fjords, white-sand coral coast in the Maldives, the chalk cliffs of Étretat in France, Bangladesh's mangrove coast, the Namib Desert meeting the Atlantic, and a busy port: "Are these all 'the seaside'?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieval starter: latitude and longitude (Year 2). Pairs receive coordinates for each photo and find the place in an atlas and on a digital map; then sort the coasts by type (rocky cliffs, sandy beaches, mangroves, fjords, coral, ports) and suggest what might shape each. Mark them on a class world map that stays up all unit.
- **Check for understanding:** "The mangrove coast of Bangladesh is about 22° N and the fjords of Norway about 62° N. How might latitude help explain why one has mangroves and the other has glacier-carved fjords?" → Bangladesh is much nearer the Equator, so it is warm enough for mangroves; Norway is far north, where ice sheets once covered the land and carved deep valleys that the sea later filled
- **Differentiation:** support: coordinates given in whole degrees with the atlas page named. / stretch: find the coordinates of the nearest coast to your school and describe its type.
- **Materials:** atlases, tablets with a digital map, photo cards, coordinate cards, large world map.
- **Joy:** "Coastline passport" — a stamp for each coast found by coordinates.

### Lesson 2 — Waves and tides: the energy that shapes coasts
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how waves form and why tides rise and fall." (UK NC KS2 physical geography — coasts; C3 D2.Geo.7.3-5)
- **Hook:** Time-lapse of the Bay of Fundy in Canada, where the sea can rise and fall by more than 15 m in about six hours: "Where does all that water go?"
- **Key activity:** Wave tank (a long clear tray): blow across the water with a straw, then a fan, to show that wind makes waves and stronger, longer-blowing wind makes bigger ones. Compare gentle 'constructive' waves (build beaches) and steep 'destructive' waves (erode). Tides: the Moon's gravity (helped by the Sun's) pulls the oceans, giving most coasts two high tides roughly every day; read a real tide table for a local or famous port.
- **Check for understanding:** "A beach loses sand in winter storms and gains it back in calm summers. Explain why, using the two kinds of wave." → winter storms bring strong, steep destructive waves that drag sand away; calm summer weather brings gentle constructive waves that push sand back up the beach
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture sequence of wind → wave → beach, with a tide-clock diagram. / stretch: use a tide table to plan the safest time for a rock-pooling trip and justify it.
- **Materials:** long clear tray, water, straws, a small fan, sand, tide tables, Moon–Earth diagram. Safety: mop spills at once; no electrical items near the water tray except a fan kept well away.
- **Joy:** Make a storm in a tray and watch the 'beach' disappear.

### Lesson 3 — Coastal erosion: from headland to stack
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how waves erode a headland to form caves, arches, stacks and stumps." (UK NC KS2 physical geography — coasts; C3 D2.Geo.7.3-5)
- **Hook:** Photos of the Twelve Apostles (limestone stacks in Victoria, Australia) — "There were more of them. What happened?"
- **Key activity:** Model a headland from layers of damp sand and clay (or sugar cubes) in a tray; 'waves' from a jug attack a crack; children photograph each stage and sequence crack → cave → arch → stack → stump. Match to real photos (Étretat, France; the Algarve, Portugal; the Twelve Apostles). Discuss why harder rock sticks out as headlands and softer rock wears back into bays.
- **Check for understanding:** "Put these in order and explain the step between arch and stack: stump, cave, crack, stack, arch." → crack → cave → arch → stack → stump; waves keep eroding the arch from below until the roof is too weak and falls, leaving a stack standing alone
- **Differentiation:** support: a five-picture card sequence to order before explaining. / stretch: explain why coastal paths and houses are sometimes moved inland, and who should pay.
- **Materials:** trays, damp sand, modelling clay or sugar cubes, jugs, photo cards, cameras.
- **Joy:** "Arch collapse" — whose model arch lasts longest?

### Lesson 4 — Beaches, spits and longshore drift
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how longshore drift moves sand along a coast and builds spits." (UK NC KS2 physical geography — coasts; C3 D2.Geo.7.3-5)
- **Hook:** "A coin dropped at one end of a beach turned up hundreds of metres along it weeks later. How did it travel?"
- **Key activity:** Playground model: children are 'sand grains' who move up the beach at an angle with each wave (swash) and straight back down (backwash), zig-zagging along the chalk coast. Then a tray model with a wave board angled to the shore. Look at real spits from the air: Farewell Spit (New Zealand) and the Langue de Barbarie at Saint-Louis (Senegal); see how groynes trap sand.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does sand pile up on one side of a groyne and not the other?" → longshore drift carries sand along the coast in one direction; the groyne blocks it, so sand builds up on the side it comes from
- **Differentiation:** support: arrows on a beach diagram to trace with a finger before explaining. / stretch: predict what could happen further along the coast if many groynes are built.
- **Materials:** chalk, trays, sand, a flat board for waves, aerial photos, groyne photos.
- **Joy:** The "human longshore drift" zig-zag race.

### Lesson 5 — People and the coast: ports, fishing and tourism
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why many people live and work at the coast and what problems this can bring." (UK NC KS2 human geography — settlements and economic activity; C3 D2.Geo.5.3-5)
- **Hook:** "About two in every five people on Earth live within 100 km of a coast. Why crowd to the edge of the land?"
- **Key activity:** Case studies on cards: Rotterdam (Europe's largest port), Mombasa (East Africa's main port and a tourist coast), a fishing town in Ghana or Peru, and a coast in your own country. Groups identify the jobs each coast provides, map them, and sort effects into benefits (trade, food, jobs) and problems (pollution, overfishing, crowding, flooding risk).
- **Check for understanding:** "Mombasa is a busy port and also a tourist coast. Explain one way the port helps tourism and one way it could harm it." → e.g. the port brings trade, roads and visitors' goods; but ship pollution and busy waters could spoil beaches and coral that tourists come to see
- **Differentiation:** support: picture-supported case cards and a two-column benefits/problems frame. / stretch: explain why port cities often grow at river mouths or in sheltered bays.
- **Materials:** case-study cards, world map, atlases.
- **Joy:** "Port manager" game: route cargo cards to the right ports on the map.

### Lesson 6 — Protecting coasts and a rising sea
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare hard and soft ways of protecting coasts and explain why rising sea levels threaten low-lying places." (UK NC KS2 human-environment interaction; sustainability; C3 D2.Geo.10.3-5)
- **Hook:** "Most of the island nation of Tuvalu is only a couple of metres above the sea. What happens as the sea rises?"
- **Key activity:** Test defences in the Lesson 3/4 trays: a card 'sea wall', a pebble 'groyne', and a 'mangrove' strip of pipe-cleaner roots in sand. Compare with real examples: the Dutch Delta Works (built after the 1953 flood), mangrove replanting in the Sundarbans of Bangladesh, and moving homes inland. Discuss causes of sea-level rise (land ice melting, warmer water expanding) and who is most affected.
- **Check for understanding:** "A village could build a concrete sea wall or plant mangroves. Give one advantage of each and say which you would choose for a poor fishing village, with a reason." → sea wall: strong, works straight away; mangroves: cheaper, grow stronger over time, give fish nurseries and wood; a reasoned choice (e.g. mangroves, because they cost less and support fishing)
- **Differentiation:** support: hard/soft sorting cards with pictures. / stretch: explain why a sea wall in one place can make erosion worse further along the coast.
- **Materials:** trays from earlier lessons, card, pebbles, pipe-cleaners, photos, a simple sea-level graph.
- **Joy:** "Storm test" — the class storm hits every model defence.

### Lesson 7 — Unit 1 assessment: reading a coast
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a map and photos to explain how a real coast has been shaped and how people use it." (UK NC KS2 — assessment; C3 D2.Geo.3.3-5)
- **Hook:** "You are the coastal expert for a new visitor centre. Explain this coast to the public."
- **Key activity:** Virtual fieldwork: each child receives a map extract with four-figure grid references and three photos of one coast (e.g. the Algarve, or a local coast); they locate features by grid reference, draw an annotated field sketch of one photo labelling erosion or deposition features, and write a short explanation board including one human use and one risk.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give the grid reference of the stack on the map and explain how it formed." → a correct four-figure reference and the sequence crack → cave → arch → stack with the role of waves
- **Differentiation:** support: a sketch frame with label boxes and a word bank. / stretch: recommend whether the coast should be defended, with evidence.
- **Materials:** coastal map extracts with grids, photo sets, sketch frames.
- **Joy:** Visitor-centre display boards.

**End-of-unit check:** Sequence and explain cave → arch → stack → stump; explain longshore drift and why groynes trap sand; compare one hard and one soft coastal defence; locate coastal features by four-figure grid reference.

## Unit 2 — Region study: the Colorado River and the desert Southwest of North America

**Essential question:** How does one river make life possible in a desert — and how should its water be shared?

**Key vocabulary:** North America, Rocky Mountains, drainage basin, watershed, source, mouth, delta, canyon, rock layers, dam, reservoir, drought, irrigation, Indigenous nation, Navajo Nation, heat island, compare, stereotype

### Lesson 8 — North America: countries, features and peoples
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can locate North America's countries, major physical features and cities, and describe how varied the continent is." (UK NC KS2 locational knowledge — North America; C3 D2.Geo.2.3-5)
- **Hook:** An empty outline of North America: "What do you already know — and what might be a stereotype from films?"
- **Key activity:** Atlas work: Canada, the United States, Mexico, the countries of Central America and the Caribbean; the Rocky Mountains, the Great Plains, the Great Lakes, the Mississippi, the deserts of the Southwest; cities (Mexico City, New York, Toronto, Los Angeles). Many languages (English, Spanish, French and hundreds of Indigenous languages such as Navajo, Cree and Nahuatl). Retrieve Year 3's South America map and compare the two continents' shapes and mountain chains.
- **Check for understanding:** "Someone says 'North America is all big cities and skyscrapers.' Use the map to explain why that is not true." → the continent includes huge deserts, mountains, forests, prairies, Arctic tundra and farmland, and many small towns and Indigenous communities as well as cities
- **Differentiation:** support: a labelled map to complete with a word bank. / stretch: explain why the Rockies and the Andes both run down the western side of their continents.
- **Materials:** atlases, blank maps, photo cards of North American landscapes and cities.
- **Joy:** "Myth or fact?" stereotype-busting quiz.

### Lesson 9 — The Colorado River and its drainage basin
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can trace the Colorado River from source to mouth and explain what a drainage basin is." (UK NC KS2 physical geography — rivers; C3 D2.Geo.7.3-5)
- **Hook:** "A raindrop falls in the Rocky Mountains of Colorado. Could it end up in a tap in Mexico?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 2's river vocabulary (source, tributary, confluence, mouth). New: the drainage basin — all the land that drains into one river, with its edge called the watershed. Children crumple paper into a 'mountain' landscape, draw ridges in marker and spray water to see which drops end up in which 'river', then colour the Colorado's basin on a map: the river starts in the Rockies and flows about 2,300 km south-west into Mexico towards the Gulf of California, and its basin covers parts of seven US states and two Mexican states.
- **Check for understanding:** "Two raindrops land a few metres apart on a mountain ridge. Explain how they could end up in completely different rivers." → the ridge is a watershed; rain on one side drains into one basin and rain on the other side drains into another
- **Differentiation:** support: a map with the basin outline dotted to trace. / stretch: find the watershed nearest to your school on a map and name the basin you live in.
- **Materials:** paper, washable markers, spray bottles, trays, atlases, Colorado basin map.
- **Joy:** "Which river will you reach?" — spray-bottle rainstorm on a paper landscape.

### Lesson 10 — The Grand Canyon: a river's long work
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the Colorado River carved the Grand Canyon and what its rock layers show." (UK NC KS2 physical geography — rivers, erosion; C3 D2.Geo.7.3-5)
- **Hook:** "The Grand Canyon is about 1.8 km deep. How could a river dig that?"
- **Key activity:** Photos and a cross-section: the canyon is about 450 km long and was carved over roughly five to six million years as the land slowly rose and the river cut down; the oldest rocks at the bottom are more than 1.7 billion years old. Children build a layered 'canyon' from coloured modelling dough, cut down through it, and read the layers bottom (oldest) to top (youngest). Learn that the canyon is the homeland of Indigenous peoples such as the Havasupai and Hopi.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are the oldest rocks found at the bottom of the canyon and not at the top?" → rock layers were laid down one on top of another, so the lowest formed first; the river has cut down through them all, exposing the oldest at the bottom
- **Differentiation:** support: a colour-coded cross-section with layers numbered. / stretch: explain why the canyon is so much wider at the top than at the river (rain, frost and landslides wear back the sides while the river cuts down).
- **Materials:** photos, cross-section diagram, several colours of modelling dough, plastic knives.
- **Joy:** Build and 'erode' a mini Grand Canyon.

### Lesson 11 — Desert cities: water and heat in Phoenix and Las Vegas
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how large cities grow in a desert and the problems of water and heat they face." (UK NC KS2 human geography — settlements, land use; C3 D2.Geo.5.3-5)
- **Hook:** Climate graphs for Phoenix and your own town: "Summer days in Phoenix are often above 40 °C and it rains very little. So why do millions of people live there?"
- **Key activity:** Map the canals and pipelines that bring Colorado River water to desert cities; read a satellite image of Lake Mead (behind the Hoover Dam, completed 1936), which fell to about a quarter full in 2022 after years of drought. Explore the 'urban heat island': children use thermometers to compare the temperature of a dark tarmac surface, a light pavement and grass in sunshine.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did the water level in Lake Mead fall, and what could cities do about it?" → years of low rain and snow in the mountains meant less water flowed in while cities and farms kept using a lot; cities can save water (less lawn watering, fixing leaks, recycling wastewater)
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture flow diagram from mountain snow to city tap. / stretch: explain, using your thermometer readings, why planting trees and using light-coloured roofs could cool a city.
- **Materials:** climate graphs, satellite images of Lake Mead, maps, thermometers. Safety: sun protection outdoors; don't touch very hot surfaces — rest the thermometer on them.
- **Joy:** "Hot-spot hunt" around the playground with thermometers.

### Lesson 12 — Sharing the river: farms, cities, nations and nature
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why sharing the Colorado's water is difficult and weigh up different groups' needs." (UK NC KS2 human geography — distribution of natural resources; C3 D2.Geo.8.3-5)
- **Hook:** "In most years the Colorado River no longer reaches the sea. Where does the water go?"
- **Key activity:** 'River budget' game: a jug of water is shared out between cups labelled farms (the largest user), cities, Mexico, the Navajo Nation and the river delta's wildlife; each group argues its case. Learn that dams and canals take most of the water before the delta, that a planned 'pulse flow' in 2014 briefly let the river reach the sea again, and that many homes on the Navajo Nation still have no piped water.
- **Check for understanding:** "Choose two groups who need the river's water. Explain why each needs it and suggest one fair way to share it." → two groups' needs explained (e.g. farmers to irrigate crops; the Navajo Nation for drinking water) and a reasoned sharing idea (e.g. drinking water first, farms using more efficient irrigation)
- **Differentiation:** support: role cards with each group's needs written simply. / stretch: explain why countries that share a river (here the USA and Mexico) need treaties.
- **Materials:** a large jug of water, labelled cups, role cards, a map of dams and canals.
- **Joy:** The "river budget" negotiation.

### Lesson 13 — Comparing the Southwest with our region
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare the desert Southwest with my own region using physical and human geography and data." (UK NC KS2 understand similarities and differences; C3 D2.Geo.6.3-5)
- **Hook:** "What would a child in Phoenix or on the Navajo Nation find surprising about where we live — and familiar?"
- **Key activity:** Comparison grid (climate graph, landscape, water source, jobs, homes, transport, languages, one environmental challenge); use real data (climate graphs, population) for both places; teacher models a worked example for the local region first.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one similarity and one difference between the Southwest and our region, and explain the cause of the difference." → a specific, evidenced comparison (e.g. both have cities that need a reliable water supply; the Southwest gets far less rain because it lies in a hot desert climate zone)
- **Differentiation:** support: a partly filled grid with data cards. / stretch: explain which region you think is more sustainable in how it uses water, with evidence.
- **Materials:** comparison grids, local and Phoenix climate data, photos.
- **Joy:** Write a postcard to a child in the Southwest.

### Lesson 14 — Unit 2 assessment: a fair guide to the Southwest
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write a fair, evidence-based description of a region and avoid stereotypes." (UK NC KS2 — assessment; C3 D2.Geo.6.3-5)
- **Hook:** "Travel-guide editors need accurate writers."
- **Key activity:** Write a short guide entry for the Colorado River region: a map, the drainage basin, the Grand Canyon, how people use the water, and one challenge — with data and balanced language.
- **Check for understanding:** "Point to a sentence where you avoided a stereotype or a vague claim. How did you do it?" → a sentence using specific evidence (a figure, a place name, a source) instead of a generalisation
- **Differentiation:** support: a guide frame with headings. / stretch: include a climate graph and interpret it.
- **Materials:** assessment frame, data, maps.
- **Joy:** Class travel-guide booklet.

**End-of-unit check:** Label the Colorado's drainage basin, source and mouth on a map; explain how the Grand Canyon formed; explain two competing needs for the river's water and a fair way to share it.

## Unit 3 — Antarctica: a continent for peace and science

**Essential question:** Why is Antarctica so extreme, and why have the world's countries agreed to share it for science?

**Key vocabulary:** Antarctica, South Pole, ice sheet, ice shelf, glacier, iceberg, blizzard, research station, Antarctic Treaty, krill, food web, ice core, sea level, expedition, scale

### Lesson 15 — The coldest, driest, windiest continent
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can locate Antarctica and explain, using data, why it is the most extreme continent." (UK NC KS2 locational knowledge — polar regions; C3 D2.Geo.2.3-5)
- **Hook:** "The coldest air temperature ever measured by a weather station was −89.2 °C, at Vostok Station in Antarctica in 1983. How cold is that compared with your freezer?"
- **Key activity:** Locate Antarctica on a globe and a polar map (retrieve latitude and the Antarctic Circle); compare its area with other continents; learn that its ice sheet is on average about 2 km thick and holds most of the world's fresh water, that parts receive less snowfall than many hot deserts, and that no one lives there permanently. Read climate data for a coastal research station.
- **Check for understanding:** "Antarctica is covered in ice, yet it is called a desert. Explain why." → a desert is defined by very low precipitation, not heat; much of Antarctica receives very little snow each year — the ice built up over hundreds of thousands of years because it hardly ever melts
- **Differentiation:** support: a data card comparing Antarctica's temperature and precipitation with your town. / stretch: explain why the South Pole is colder than the North Pole (the South Pole is on a high ice plateau on land; the North Pole is on sea ice over ocean).
- **Materials:** globes, polar-projection maps, data cards, atlases.
- **Joy:** "Freezer versus Antarctica" temperature line across the classroom wall.

### Lesson 16 — Reaching the South Pole: maps, routes and scale
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a map scale to measure an expedition route and explain why some polar expeditions succeeded." (UK NC KS2 map skills — scale; C3 D2.Geo.1.3-5)
- **Hook:** "In 1911–12 two teams raced to the South Pole. One returned safely; the other did not. Could geography explain why?"
- **Key activity:** Roald Amundsen's Norwegian team reached the Pole on 14 December 1911, using dog sleds, skis and fur clothing based on what Amundsen had learned from Netsilik Inuit people in the Arctic; Robert Falcon Scott's British team arrived on 17 January 1912 and died on the return journey. Children use the map's scale bar to measure both routes, compare the start points and plan daily distances, then list the geographical challenges (crevasses, blizzards, altitude of the plateau).
- **Check for understanding:** "Using the scale, how far did Amundsen's team travel from their base to the Pole, and roughly how many kilometres a day did they need to cover in about eight weeks?" → an answer measured from the map (roughly 1,300–1,400 km each way) and a sensible daily figure (about 25 km)
- **Differentiation:** support: a route already marked with 100 km intervals to count. / stretch: explain how learning from Inuit people's knowledge of cold places helped Amundsen.
- **Materials:** polar maps with scale bars, string, rulers, route cards, photos of expedition equipment.
- **Joy:** "Plan your own expedition" kit list with a weight limit.

### Lesson 17 — Life in the Southern Ocean
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how Antarctic wildlife depends on the ocean and on krill." (UK NC KS2 physical geography — biomes; C3 D2.Geo.7.3-5)
- **Hook:** "Almost nothing lives on Antarctica's ice — yet the ocean around it is full of whales, seals and penguins. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Build a Southern Ocean food web on the floor with cards (tiny algae → krill → penguins, fish, seals, whales); learn that krill feed on algae that grow under the sea ice; emperor penguins breed through the Antarctic winter, with males keeping the egg warm on their feet. Retrieve adaptation from Year 3 biomes.
- **Check for understanding:** "If warming seas meant there was less sea ice, why could that harm penguins and whales, even though they don't eat ice?" → less sea ice means fewer algae growing under it, so fewer krill, and penguins and whales depend on krill (directly or through fish) for food
- **Differentiation:** support: a three-link food chain with pictures. / stretch: explain why a limit on catching krill for fishing and farm feed matters.
- **Materials:** food-web cards, string, photos, a short approved wildlife clip.
- **Joy:** "Huddle" game — act out emperor penguins taking turns at the cold edge.

### Lesson 18 — The Antarctic Treaty: a continent shared
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what the Antarctic Treaty is and why countries agreed to share Antarctica for peace and science." (UK NC KS2 human geography; global citizenship; C3 D2.Geo.10.3-5)
- **Hook:** "Seven countries once claimed slices of Antarctica, and some claims overlapped. How did they avoid a fight?"
- **Key activity:** Map the old territorial claims and today's research stations run by many countries (e.g. Argentina, Australia, China, India, South Africa, the USA). Learn that 12 countries signed the Antarctic Treaty in 1959, that more than 50 have joined since, and that it keeps Antarctica for peaceful use and science; a later agreement (signed in 1991) bans mining. Children draft 'station rules' for visitors.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might it be better for the world that no country owns Antarctica? Give one reason and one problem that could still arise." → e.g. it prevents wars over land and lets scientists from every country share research; problems could include pressure to fish or mine, or too many tourists
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards for 'allowed' and 'not allowed' activities. / stretch: debate whether tourism should be limited, using evidence.
- **Materials:** polar maps showing claims and stations, treaty summary cards.
- **Joy:** "Treaty summit": each group represents a country and must reach an agreement.

### Lesson 19 — Ice, climate and sea level
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what ice cores tell scientists and why melting land ice raises sea level but melting sea ice does not." (UK NC KS2 physical geography; sustainability; C3 D2.Geo.7.3-5)
- **Hook:** "Scientists have drilled ice so old that bubbles in it hold air from about 800,000 years ago. What can old air tell us?"
- **Key activity:** Model: two identical trays marked with the water level — ice cubes floating in water (sea ice) and ice cubes on a 'land' block of plasticine above water (land ice). Predict and measure the water level after melting. Link to ice cores as a record of past climate and to sea-level rise threatening coasts (Unit 1).
- **Check for understanding:** "In our model, which melting ice raised the water level, and why does that matter for places like Tuvalu or Bangladesh?" → the ice on land, because its meltwater was added to the sea; melting land ice from Antarctica and Greenland raises sea level, which floods low-lying coasts
- **Differentiation:** support: a prediction sheet with pictures of both trays. / stretch: explain why a warmer ocean also raises sea level (water expands as it warms).
- **Materials:** identical clear trays, ice cubes, plasticine or stones, water, rulers, marker pens.
- **Joy:** The "which tray floods?" prediction reveal.

### Lesson 20 — Unit 3 assessment: design an Antarctic research station
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use my knowledge of Antarctica's geography to design and justify a research station." (UK NC KS2 — assessment; C3 D2.Geo.3.3-5)
- **Hook:** "Your country has been given permission to build a new station. Where, and how?"
- **Key activity:** Groups choose a site on a map (coast or plateau) and justify it (access by ship, shelter, research purpose); design the station to survive cold, wind and snow build-up and to follow the Treaty's environmental rules (waste removed, no harm to wildlife); present.
- **Check for understanding:** "Justify one choice in your station design using a fact about Antarctica's climate or rules." → e.g. raised on legs so blowing snow passes underneath; all waste shipped home because the Treaty protects the environment
- **Differentiation:** support: a design template with prompts. / stretch: add a research question your station would study and explain why Antarctica is the best place to study it.
- **Materials:** polar maps, design sheets, photos of real stations (e.g. Halley VI, Princess Elisabeth).
- **Joy:** Station pitches to a "Treaty panel".

**End-of-unit check:** Explain why Antarctica is a desert; describe the Southern Ocean food web and how sea ice supports it; explain what the Antarctic Treaty does; explain why melting land ice raises sea level.

## Unit 4 — Mountains: life at altitude in the Andes and the Himalaya

**Essential question:** How does height change the climate, and how do people live, farm and work in the world's great mountain ranges?

**Key vocabulary:** mountain range, fold mountain, plate, altitude, sea level, temperature, climate graph, terrace, glacier, meltwater, tourism, porter, Sherpa, Quechua, Aymara, sustainability, compare

### Lesson 21 — How fold mountains grow
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how the Andes and the Himalaya formed where plates meet." (UK NC KS2 physical geography — mountains; C3 D2.Geo.7.3-5)
- **Hook:** Push layers of coloured modelling dough or towels together: "Watch mountains grow!"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 2's mountain ranges and tectonic plates. New depth: the Himalaya formed as the Indian Plate pushed into the Eurasian Plate and are still rising a few millimetres a year; the Andes are forming where the Nazca Plate sinks beneath the South American Plate. Locate both ranges and read their highest peaks from an atlas (Everest about 8,849 m; Aconcagua about 6,961 m).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are fossils of sea creatures found near the top of Mount Everest?" → the rock formed under an ancient sea and was pushed up when the plates collided
- **Differentiation:** support: modelling with guided commentary and a labelled diagram. / stretch: explain why earthquakes are common in Nepal, using the plate map.
- **Materials:** layered modelling dough or towels, plate maps, photos of the Himalaya and Andes, atlases.
- **Joy:** Make mountain ranges from dough layers.

### Lesson 22 — Altitude and climate
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use climate data to explain how temperature changes with altitude." (UK NC KS2 physical geography — climate zones; C3 D2.Geo.4.3-5)
- **Hook:** "La Paz in Bolivia and Santa Cruz in Bolivia are in the same country, not far apart in latitude. Why do people in one wear warm jackets while the other is hot all year?"
- **Key activity:** Compare climate graphs for La Paz (about 3,600 m; average temperature under 10 °C) and Santa Cruz (about 400 m; average about 24 °C); learn that air usually gets about 6–7 °C cooler for every 1,000 m you climb. Children draw a mountain profile and label the temperatures and vegetation belts at different heights (forest, grassland, bare rock, snow).
- **Check for understanding:** "A town at sea level has an average of 25 °C. Estimate the average at a village 2,000 m higher up the same mountain, and explain." → about 12 °C (roughly 6–7 °C cooler per 1,000 m), because air cools as it rises and thins
- **Differentiation:** support: a mountain diagram with 1,000 m steps marked. / stretch: explain why snow can lie on mountains close to the Equator, such as Kilimanjaro.
- **Materials:** climate graphs, mountain profile templates, atlases.
- **Joy:** "Climb the mountain" — step up a height ladder and 'feel' the temperature drop with cards.

### Lesson 23 — The Andes: farming at every height
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how farmers in the Andes use different altitudes for different crops and animals." (UK NC KS2 human and physical geography — mountains, land use; C3 D2.Geo.4.3-5)
- **Hook:** "Peru grows thousands of different kinds of potato. Why so many?"
- **Key activity:** Farming zones by height (maize and fruit lower, potatoes and quinoa higher, llama and alpaca herding highest); terraces built by the Inca (e.g. the circular terraces of Moray), many of which are still farmed today — how they create flat land, stop soil washing away and store heat; Quechua and Aymara farming communities today.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do Andean farmers grow different crops at different heights on the same mountain, and how do terraces help?" → temperature and conditions change with altitude, so each crop suits a different zone; terraces make flat fields on steep slopes and stop soil and water running off
- **Differentiation:** support: a mountain diagram with zones to label with crop pictures. / stretch: explain why growing many potato varieties protects farmers from pests and bad weather.
- **Materials:** photos, altitude diagram, potato varieties (real or pictures), clay for terraces.
- **Joy:** Build a terraced hillside from clay and pour 'rain' to test it.

### Lesson 24 — The Himalaya: living and working in Nepal
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how people live and work in the Himalaya of Nepal and weigh the benefits and problems of mountain tourism." (UK NC KS2 human geography — economic activity; C3 D2.Geo.5.3-5)
- **Hook:** "Every year thousands of trekkers visit the Everest region. Who carries their bags?"
- **Key activity:** Case study of the Khumbu region: Sherpa communities, farming and yak herding, mountain guiding and portering, lodges and schools built with tourism money; Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary's first ascent (1953). Sort tourism effects into benefits (jobs, schools, roads) and problems (litter on trails, dangerous work, crowding on Everest).
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one benefit and one problem that tourism brings to a Sherpa village, and suggest one way to reduce the problem." → e.g. benefit: jobs and income; problem: litter and waste on trails; solution: carry-out-your-rubbish rules or deposits on waste
- **Differentiation:** support: picture impact cards and a two-column frame. / stretch: compare mountain tourism in Nepal with tourism in Venice (Year 3) — what is similar?
- **Materials:** photos, maps of Nepal, impact cards.
- **Joy:** "Pack for base camp" — choose and justify ten items within a weight limit.

### Lesson 25 — Mountains as water towers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how mountain glaciers supply rivers and why their shrinking matters to people far away." (UK NC KS2 physical geography — rivers, glaciers; sustainability; C3 D2.Geo.8.3-5)
- **Hook:** "The Chacaltaya glacier in Bolivia once had a ski slope. By about 2009 it had disappeared. Why should people in La Paz worry?"
- **Key activity:** Model: an ice block on a sloping tray melts slowly into a 'river' channel — then a small ice cube melts fast. Map how Himalayan glaciers and snows feed great rivers such as the Indus, Ganges and Brahmaputra, on which more than a billion people depend, and how Andean glaciers help supply cities like La Paz in the dry season. Compare photos of glaciers then and now.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are glaciers especially important for water in the dry season, and what could happen to river flow if they disappear?" → they store water as ice and release it slowly as meltwater when there is little rain; without them rivers could run much lower in the dry season
- **Differentiation:** support: a before/after photo pair with guiding questions. / stretch: explain why rivers might at first get fuller as glaciers melt faster, then emptier.
- **Materials:** ice blocks and cubes, trays, glacier photo pairs, maps of Asian rivers.
- **Joy:** "Glacier race" — big block versus small cube.

### Lesson 26 — Comparing mountain regions with our region
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare a mountain region with my own region using physical and human geography." (UK NC KS2 understand similarities and differences; C3 D2.Geo.6.3-5)
- **Hook:** "What would a child in Cusco or in the Khumbu find surprising about where we live — and familiar?"
- **Key activity:** Comparison grid (altitude, climate, landscape, jobs, homes, food, transport, languages); use data (climate graphs, population) for both; worked example modelled for the teacher's own region. If your school is in a mountain region, compare with a lowland region instead.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one similarity and one difference between your region and the Andes or Himalaya, and explain the cause of the difference." → a specific, evidenced comparison with a cause (e.g. our winters are milder because we are much lower)
- **Differentiation:** support: a partly filled grid. / stretch: explain which of the differences is caused by physical geography and which by human choices.
- **Materials:** comparison grids, local and mountain-town climate data, photos.
- **Joy:** Write a postcard to a child in Cusco or Namche Bazaar.

### Lesson 27 — Unit 4 assessment: mountain explainer
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how altitude shapes climate and ways of life in a mountain region, using evidence." (UK NC KS2 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "The mountain museum needs a poster to explain life at altitude."
- **Key activity:** Each child makes an annotated mountain cross-section for the Andes or Himalaya: how the range formed, temperature and farming zones by height, one way people earn a living, and one sustainability issue (tourism or glaciers).
- **Check for understanding:** "Point to one annotation on your poster and explain the cause behind it." → an annotation linked to a correct cause (e.g. potatoes grow higher up because they tolerate cooler temperatures)
- **Differentiation:** support: a cross-section template with label boxes. / stretch: add a climate graph for a mountain town and interpret it.
- **Materials:** poster templates, atlases, data cards.
- **Joy:** Mountain museum gallery walk.

**End-of-unit check:** Explain how fold mountains form; estimate a temperature change with altitude; explain farming zones and terraces; weigh one benefit and one problem of mountain tourism; explain why glaciers matter for water.

## Unit 5 — Natural resources and energy

**Essential question:** Where do the resources we use every day come from — and how can we use them fairly and sustainably?

**Key vocabulary:** natural resource, raw material, renewable, non-renewable, energy, electricity, fossil fuel, solar, wind, hydroelectric, geothermal, mineral, mine, supply chain, grid reference, scale, sustainable

### Lesson 28 — What are natural resources, and where are they?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how natural resources are distributed around the world and classify them as renewable or non-renewable." (UK NC KS2 distribution of natural resources; C3 D2.Geo.9.3-5)
- **Hook:** "Where did everything in your pencil case come from?"
- **Key activity:** Trace pencil-case items back to raw materials (wood, graphite, oil for plastic, metals, rubber); sort resources as renewable/non-renewable; map where some come from (timber from managed forests, copper from Chile, oil from the Middle East and elsewhere, sunshine for solar power in deserts); discuss why resources are unevenly spread.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is wood from a managed forest renewable but oil is not?" → trees can be replanted and regrow within a human lifetime; oil took millions of years to form and will not be replaced
- **Differentiation:** support: picture sorting cards. / stretch: explain how a country with few natural resources can still become wealthy (e.g. through trade and skills, as in Singapore).
- **Materials:** pencil-case objects, resource cards, world map.
- **Joy:** "Pencil-case world map" — string from each item to its source.

### Lesson 29 — Energy: where does our electricity come from?
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how different countries generate electricity and compare renewable and non-renewable sources." (UK NC KS2 distribution of natural resources — energy; C3 D2.Geo.9.3-5)
- **Hook:** "Norway gets around nine-tenths of its electricity from rivers; Iceland heats most homes with heat from underground. What does your country use — and why?"
- **Key activity:** Energy-source stations: fossil fuels, hydroelectric, wind, solar and geothermal, each with a photo, how it works simply, and its advantages and disadvantages. Link each to geography (mountains and rain for hydro; volcanic ground for geothermal in Iceland and Kenya; sunny deserts for solar; windy coasts for wind). Read a simple pie chart of your own country's electricity mix.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does geography help explain why Iceland and Kenya use geothermal power but most countries do not?" → both sit on active plate boundaries with hot rocks near the surface; most countries don't have that heat close enough to use cheaply
- **Differentiation:** support: matching cards (energy source ↔ place ↔ picture). / stretch: suggest the best renewable source for your own region using its geography, and one drawback.
- **Materials:** station cards, photos, energy-mix pie charts, a small solar-powered toy or a pinwheel.
- **Joy:** Solar-toy race in the sunshine (or a desk-fan pinwheel generator demonstration).

### Lesson 30 — Minerals in our devices
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can trace minerals in a phone or tablet to where they are mined and discuss how to source them responsibly." (UK NC KS2 distribution of natural resources — minerals; C3 D2.Geo.9.3-5)
- **Hook:** An old phone taken apart (battery removed by an adult): "There are dozens of different elements in here. Where on Earth did they come from?"
- **Key activity:** Map key minerals: copper (Chile is the largest producer), lithium for batteries (Australia and Chile), cobalt (most comes from the Democratic Republic of the Congo). Discuss, calmly and factually, problems some mining causes (damage to land and rivers, unsafe work, sometimes including children) and solutions (recycling old devices, company checks on supply chains, keeping devices longer).
- **Check for understanding:** "Give two reasons why recycling old phones helps both people and the planet." → less new mining is needed, so less damage to land and fewer people in dangerous mines; valuable metals are reused rather than thrown away
- **Differentiation:** support: a picture map with three minerals and one fact each. / stretch: explain why the country where a mineral is mined is often not the country that earns the most from the finished phone.
- **Materials:** an old phone or tablet (adult removes battery; do not open batteries), mineral cards, world map, a recycling-point leaflet.
- **Joy:** Launch a class "device amnesty" to collect old phones for recycling.

### Lesson 31 — Reading a real map: resources and energy near us
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use four-figure grid references, symbols and scale on a published topographic map to find and describe resource and energy sites." (UK NC KS2 map skills — grid references, symbols, keys, scale; C3 D2.Geo.1.3-5)
- **Hook:** "This map shows a reservoir, a quarry, a wind farm and a power line. Can you find them — and work out how far the electricity travels?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve four-figure grid references and scale (Years 2–3). Pairs use a real 1:25,000 or 1:50,000 map (or a national equivalent) of the local area or a sample area: find resource and energy symbols, give their grid references, measure distances with the scale bar (including a winding route with string), and describe where each is and why it might be there (e.g. a reservoir in a valley, wind turbines on high ground).
- **Check for understanding:** "Give the grid reference of the wind farm and explain, using the map, why it was built there." → a correct four-figure reference and a reason from the map (e.g. high, open ground with few buildings, where the wind is strong)
- **Differentiation:** support: a map extract with fewer symbols and the key enlarged. / stretch: measure a winding route with string and explain why it is longer than the straight-line distance.
- **Materials:** published topographic maps or extracts, map keys, rulers, string.
- **Joy:** "Map detectives" race to find ten symbols.

### Lesson 32 — An energy plan for our town
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can design a more sustainable energy plan for my local area and justify it with maps and data." (UK NC KS2 human geography; sustainability; C3 D2.Geo.10.3-5)
- **Hook:** "The mayor wants the town to use more clean energy. Where should it come from?"
- **Key activity:** Groups use a local map and simple data (sunny hours, windiest places, rivers, roof space) to plan sites for solar panels, wind turbines or other sources, plus energy-saving ideas; mark sites with grid references; present, including one trade-off (e.g. views, cost, wildlife).
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain one site in your plan and why it suits that energy source, including one drawback." → a specific site with a geographical reason and an honest trade-off
- **Differentiation:** support: a template plan with feature stickers. / stretch: estimate how many homes your plan could power using given figures.
- **Materials:** local maps with grids, data cards, stickers, coloured pens.
- **Joy:** "Future town" energy showcase for another class.

**End-of-unit check:** Classify resources as renewable or non-renewable with reasons; explain how geography affects which energy a country uses; trace one mineral to where it is mined; give four-figure grid references and measure distances on a topographic map.

## Unit 6 — Fieldwork: microclimates of our school grounds

**Essential question:** Is every part of our school grounds the same — and how can we find out with measurements, not guesses?

**Key vocabulary:** microclimate, fieldwork, enquiry question, hypothesis, site, sample, thermometer, wind speed, shelter, shade, data, map, bar chart, conclusion, evaluation

### Lesson 33 — Planning the microclimate investigation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a fieldwork investigation with a question, a hypothesis, sites marked by grid reference and a fair method." (UK NC KS2 fieldwork; C3 D2.Geo.3.3-5)
- **Hook:** "On a sunny day, where is the warmest place to sit at break — and where is the most sheltered from wind?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve the fieldwork cycle (Year 3). Groups choose a question (e.g. Where is the warmest place in the grounds? Where is the windiest? Which spot is best for a new outdoor classroom?), write a hypothesis, choose 6–8 sites on a gridded school map, and plan how to measure temperature (thermometer in shade at the same height), wind (ribbon angle or a simple anemometer), ground surface and cloud cover at the same time at every site.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must every temperature reading be taken at the same height, in shade, and at about the same time?" → so the only difference between sites is the place itself; sunshine on the thermometer, height or a later time of day would change the reading and make the comparison unfair
- **Differentiation:** support: choose from three prepared questions with a method card. / stretch: plan repeat readings and explain how you will deal with an odd result.
- **Materials:** gridded school maps, thermometers, ribbons or simple anemometers, planning sheets.
- **Joy:** Children choose their investigation and name their field team.

### Lesson 34 — Collecting and mapping the data
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can collect accurate measurements in the field and present them on a map and in a graph." (UK NC KS2 fieldwork — observe, measure, record and present; C3 D2.Geo.3.3-5)
- **Hook:** Clipboards out: "Geographers measure the world."
- **Key activity:** Teams visit their sites in a set order and record readings; back inside, they show temperatures on the school map with coloured dots (a colour key for each temperature band) and draw a bar chart; annotate photos of the warmest and coolest sites.
- **Check for understanding:** "Look at your map. Which site was warmest, and what features of that place might explain it?" → names the site and links it to features (e.g. a sunny, sheltered corner with dark tarmac and a wall blocking the wind)
- **Differentiation:** support: fixed roles in the group and a recording sheet with the sites pre-listed. / stretch: add wind readings to the map with arrows scaled by strength.
- **Materials:** clipboards, thermometers, anemometers or ribbons, recording sheets, cameras/tablets, coloured pens. Safety: stay within the school grounds in sight of an adult; sun protection.
- **Joy:** Outdoor fieldwork with real instruments.

### Lesson 35 — Conclusions, recommendations and end-of-year assessment
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw an evidence-based conclusion, make a recommendation and show my geography learning from the year." (UK NC KS2 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Present your findings to the headteacher: where should the new bench, tree or outdoor classroom go?"
- **Key activity:** Write a conclusion answering the question with data; test the hypothesis; recommend a site and justify it; evaluate the method. Then a short end-of-year map and knowledge check (coasts, a region, Antarctica, mountains, resources, four-figure grid references and scale).
- **Check for understanding:** "What does your data show, what do you recommend, and how confident are you in your results?" → a conclusion supported by readings, a justified recommendation and an honest evaluation (e.g. readings taken over only one day)
- **Differentiation:** support: a conclusion frame. / stretch: suggest a follow-up investigation (e.g. repeat in winter) and predict the result.
- **Materials:** fieldwork results, maps, assessment sheet.
- **Joy:** Presenting to a real decision-maker.

**End-of-unit check:** A completed fieldwork report (question, hypothesis, method, map and graph, conclusion, recommendation, evaluation) and the end-of-year map-skills check.

## Teacher guidance

**Common misconceptions (and fixes).**
- *"Coasts stay the same."* — Show before-and-after photos of collapsed arches and cliffs; coasts change every storm.
- *"Tides are caused by wind."* — Wind makes waves; tides are caused mainly by the Moon's pull.
- *"Rivers always flow south (down the page)."* — Rivers flow downhill, whichever direction that is; the Colorado flows south-west, the Nile north.
- *"Deserts are always hot."* — Antarctica is the largest desert; deserts are defined by low precipitation.
- *"All melting ice raises sea level."* — Melting floating sea ice barely changes it; land ice does (Lesson 19 model).
- *"Mountains are cold because they are closer to space / the Sun."* — Air cools as it rises and thins; the extra distance to the Sun is tiny.
- *"Everyone in North America lives in big cities."* — Show deserts, farms, reservations, Arctic towns.
- *"Renewable means free and harmless."* — Every energy source has costs and trade-offs.

**Fieldwork and safety.** Carry out risk assessments; school-grounds fieldwork within sight of adults; sun protection for outdoor work; water-tray lessons need spill mats; an adult removes batteries from any device taken apart.

**Sensitive content.** Mining and child labour (Lesson 30) and sea-level rise (Lessons 6 and 19) can worry children: keep the focus on facts, on people's agency and on practical solutions.

**International notes.** Every lesson on "our region" or "local area" adapts to any country; the teacher models a worked example first. If your school is in the North American Southwest, compare Unit 2 with another dry region (e.g. the Murray–Darling basin in Australia); if it is in a mountain region, compare Unit 4 with a lowland region. Use your own national mapping agency's maps in Lesson 31.

**Vertical links.** Year 2: latitude and longitude, time zones, four-figure grid references, rivers, mountains, volcanoes and earthquakes. Year 3: climate zones and biomes, the Amazon, the Mediterranean, trade, water supply and the fieldwork cycle. Year 4: coasts, a drainage basin in a North American region, Antarctica, altitude, resources and energy, topographic maps. Year 5: six-figure grid references, contours and GIS; tectonic hazards and rivers in depth; trade and globalisation; cities.

**Cross-curricular links.** Science (Moon and tides; states of matter and melting; the Sun is a star; forces), maths (scale and distance, coordinates, temperature and negative numbers, pie charts, bar charts), history (Amundsen and Scott; the Inca), English (*Esperanza Rising*'s Mexican and Californian landscapes), Spanish (Andean and Mexican places), art (Andean textiles), life skills (sustainability and fair sharing).
