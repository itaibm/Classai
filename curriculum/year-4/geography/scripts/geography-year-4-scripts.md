Complete, minute-by-minute scripts for [Year 4 Geography](../geography-year-4.md), built to the [scripting guide](../../../SCRIPTING-GUIDE.md) and grounded in [`knowledge-base/subjects/history-geography/geography-ks2.md`](../../../knowledge-base/subjects/history-geography/geography-ks2.md). Every beat ⏱ sums to the lesson's duration, with verbatim Say / Show / Do / Ask, a real check with wrong-answer fixes pulled from the KB misconception lists, the mandatory "if the child is stuck" protocol, what to emphasize, differentiation, extension and assessment evidence.

Pin every fact to the **globe**, a **world map** and the **water-cycle / climate-zone diagrams**. The disciplinary questions this year sharpen: **"how do we know?"**, **"what changed and why?"** and **"is this fair and reliable?"** Three units, 9 lessons.

# Unit 1 — The Water Cycle: how rivers never run dry

**Essential question:** Where does rain come from, and why does the water never run out?

---

## Lesson 1 — The never-ending journey of water
**Geography · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Water cycle)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name the four stages of the water cycle in order."

**You will need:** a water-cycle diagram (sun, sea, evaporation arrow, cloud, rain, river); four stage cards (evaporation / condensation / precipitation / collection); arrows; a glass of water; the globe.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3's rivers — a river flows from its source (high ground) downhill to its mouth at the sea; evaporation informally (puddles dry up).
**Key vocabulary:** **water cycle** (water's never-ending loop), **evaporation** (water turns to invisible vapour and rises), **condensation** (vapour cools into cloud droplets), **precipitation** (rain/snow/hail falls), **collection** (water gathers back in seas and rivers), **vapour**, **the Sun** (the engine).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** the four stages run **in order and loop forever** — evaporation → condensation → precipitation → collection → back to the start. The **Sun powers the whole cycle**. One idea, taught well: the *order*. Pre-empt "rain is new water" (full lesson next time).

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Look at this glass of water. The water in here might once have been in a *dinosaur's* drink. I'm not joking. How can that be? Because water is never used up — it gets used again, and again, and again, forever. Today we follow one drop on its never-ending journey."
**Show:** the glass of water; the blank water-cycle diagram.  **Do:** swirl the glass; trace a circle in the air to signal "a loop."
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Last year we followed rivers. Where does a river start, and which way does it flow?" → *listen for:* "starts at the source on high ground; flows downhill to the sea."
*(If rivers are shaky: point on the globe — high mountains, then a finger sliding down to the sea.)*

### ⏱ 5–14 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch one drop go round. **One — evaporation.** The **Sun** heats the sea and turns water into invisible **vapour** that floats up. **Two — condensation.** High up it's cold, so the vapour cools into tiny droplets that gather into a **cloud**. **Three — precipitation.** The droplets get heavy and fall as rain, snow or hail. **Four — collection.** That water collects in streams and rivers and flows back to the sea… and then? The Sun heats it again. We're back at the start. It's a *cycle* — it never ends."
**Show / Do:** place the four stage cards onto the diagram in order, drawing an arrow from each to the next, closing the loop back to the sun. Point to the Sun: "the engine."
**Emphasize:** "Evaporation, condensation, precipitation, collection — and the **Sun** drives it all."

### ⏱ 14–22 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Now you place the cards with me. The Sun has just heated the sea — which card comes first? Good. The vapour is high and cold — which next? Then it falls — which card? Then it gathers in rivers — last card. Now draw the arrow that takes us *back to the start*."
**Do:** child places all four cards in order and draws the closing arrow; tutor narrates only if asked.
**Ask:** "What powers the whole cycle — what makes the water rise in the first place?" → *listen for:* "the Sun."

### ⏱ 22–30 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name the four stages of the water cycle, in order, starting from the sea." *(Distractor: the child may name them but jumble condensation and precipitation.)*
- ✅ **Expected:** evaporation → condensation → precipitation → collection — then probe: *"And what happens after collection?"* (it starts again — the Sun evaporates it).
- ❌ **If "rain comes before clouds form" (precipitation before condensation):** order slip — they picture rain first → **fix:** "Can rain fall before there's a cloud? Where does the cloud come from?" Re-walk vapour → cloud → fall.
- ❌ **If "evaporation, rain, clouds, river" (using everyday words, wrong order):** everyday words mask the order → **fix:** "Use our four words. The water rises *first*. Which of our words means 'rises as vapour'?"

### ⏱ 30–37 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child relabels a fresh blank diagram with the four stages and arrows, unaided. Once secure, interleave: "Start your loop at the *cloud* instead of the sea — does the order still work?" (yes — a cycle has no real start).
**Watch for:** swapping condensation/precipitation; forgetting the closing arrow (treating it as a line, not a loop).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — order slip vs missing the loop; 3) drop to the four picture cards (sun → cloud → rain → river) and order those first; 4) one hint: "The water has to *rise* before it can fall"; 5) re-ask just the first two stages, then build back up; 6) keep it safe — "Mistakes show us what to practise. Let's take a smaller step."

### ⏱ 37–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Say the four stages back to me in order, and tell me the one thing that powers it all." → *listen for:* "evaporation, condensation, precipitation, collection; the Sun."
**Process praise:** "You kept the stages in the right order *and* closed the loop — that's thinking like a geographer, not just memorising."
**Revisit later:** tomorrow — proving rain is *recycled*, not brand-new. Quick retrieval of the four stages at the start of next lesson, and again in a week.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** order four picture cards (sun → cloud → rain → river) before using the technical words.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain *what powers* the whole cycle and *why* the vapour rises (the Sun heats it).

**Extension (if time allows):** add labels for where each stage happens in real life — sea (evaporation), sky (condensation), hills (precipitation), river (collection).
**Assessment evidence:** child names the four stages in correct order and identifies the Sun as the driver.

## Lesson 2 — Watching the water cycle for real
**Geography · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Water cycle)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that rain is recycled water, not brand-new water made in clouds."

**You will need:** a clear bowl or jar with **warm** water and a lid (or cling film + a stone weight) — set up before the lesson; the water-cycle diagram; the glass of water from Lesson 1.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the four stages and their order (Lesson 1); the Sun as the engine.
**Key vocabulary:** **recycled** (used again and again), **vapour**, **condensation**, **brand-new** (the wrong idea — made from nothing).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** rain is **the same water recycled forever** — evaporated from seas and rivers, condensed into clouds, fallen again — **not** new water made inside clouds. The KB misconception to crush: "rain is brand-new water made in the clouds." Repeat the image: "today's rain could once have been in a dinosaur's drink."

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Clouds rain on us all the time. So clouds must *make* new water, right? But hang on — if clouds made new water from nothing, the seas would be overflowing by now. They're not. So where did all that rain actually come from?"
**Show:** the covered bowl of warm water (don't reveal droplets yet).  **Do:** point at the sealed lid mysteriously.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Quick — name the four stages of the water cycle." → *listen for:* "evaporation, condensation, precipitation, collection."
*(If shaky: rebuild the loop on the diagram in 20 seconds before moving on.)*

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Here's the truth: clouds **don't make new water**. Rain is the **same water recycled forever**. Follow it: the Sun **evaporated** water off the sea — that water rose as vapour. Up high it **condensed** into a cloud. The cloud got heavy and the water fell again as rain — **precipitation**. So the rain that lands on you was *already water* yesterday, in the sea, in a puddle, maybe in a dinosaur's drink a hundred million years ago. Nothing new was made. It just went round."
**Show / Do:** lift the bowl's lid — show the **droplets that have formed underneath**. "Look. I didn't add a single drop. This water *evaporated* from the warm water below, *condensed* on the cold lid, and now it's 'raining' back down. Recycled — right here."
**Emphasize:** "Same water, recycled. **Not** brand-new water made in the clouds."

### ⏱ 12–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's match each step of our mini water cycle to the real one. The warm water turning to vapour — which stage? The droplets forming on the cold lid — which stage? The drops falling back — which stage? And matching everyday life: a puddle drying up on a sunny day — which stage is *that*?"
**Do:** child names evaporation (warm water rising), condensation (lid droplets), precipitation (drops falling), and matches "puddle drying = evaporation."
**Ask:** "Did the droplets on the lid come from *new* water, or the *same* water from below?" → *listen for:* "the same water — it evaporated and condensed."

### ⏱ 20–27 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Is rain brand-new water made in the clouds? Explain how you know." *(Distractor: "yes, clouds make it" — the exact KB misconception.)*
- ✅ **Expected:** "No — it's the same water recycled: it evaporated from seas and rivers, condensed into clouds, then fell again." — probe: *"So where was that rain yesterday?"* (in the sea/a river/a puddle).
- ❌ **If "yes, clouds make new water":** rain seems to appear from nowhere → **fix (KB):** "If clouds *made* water, where would they get it from? Look at our bowl — did new water appear, or did the water below move up and back down?"
- ❌ **If "the water just disappears and comes back" (no mechanism):** vague → **fix:** "What's the proper word for water *rising* into the sky? And for it *turning into cloud*? Use evaporation and condensation."

### ⏱ 27–32 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child traces **one drop's full loop** in their own words, starting in the sea and ending back in the sea, using all four stage words. Once secure, interleave: "Now start your drop in a *puddle* in the garden — does it still complete the loop?"
**Watch for:** "new water" creeping back in; skipping condensation.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "new water" idea vs a missing stage; 3) point back to the bowl — concrete proof no water was added; 4) one hint: "Where was this rain *before* it was a cloud?"; 5) re-ask just "sea → sky" then build the rest; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise — smaller step."

### ⏱ 32–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me in one sentence: is rain new or recycled, and how do you know?" → *listen for:* "recycled — it evaporated and condensed before falling again."
**Process praise:** "You used real evidence from our bowl to argue your point — that's exactly how a scientist *knows*, not just guesses."
**Revisit later:** Unit 1 check next. Bring "recycled, not new" back when we study climate (where rain falls a lot vs almost never).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match each stage to an everyday example (puddle drying / clouds in the sky / rain falling).
- **Stretch (challenge):** trace one drop's full loop in their own words, naming all four stages unprompted.

**Extension (if time allows):** explain why the seas don't overflow even though it rains constantly somewhere on Earth (water leaves the sea as fast as it returns — it's balanced).
**Assessment evidence:** child states rain is recycled (not new) and explains it via evaporation → condensation → precipitation.

---

**End-of-unit check (Unit 1):** Order the four stages (evaporation → condensation → precipitation → collection); explain that water is **recycled, not new**; link the cycle to where river water comes from (collection feeds rivers; rivers flow to the sea; the Sun evaporates it again). **Misconception watch:** rain is recycled, not brand-new; the **Sun** drives the cycle.

# Unit 2 — Climate Zones & Biomes

**Essential question:** Why is one part of the world a steamy jungle and another a frozen desert?

---

## Lesson 3 — Weather vs climate
**Geography · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Climate & biomes)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the difference between weather and climate."

**You will need:** weather/climate statement cards (e.g. "It's raining right now" / "The UK is usually mild and wet" / "It snowed yesterday" / "Summers here are warm"); two labelled boxes or hoops — **Weather (today)** / **Climate (usual pattern)**; a UK seasons chart.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** KS1 seasons — the UK has four seasons; weather changes day to day.
**Key vocabulary:** **weather** (what's happening *today* — changes fast), **climate** (the *usual* pattern of weather over many years), **pattern**, **average**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **weather = today and changes fast; climate = the long-term usual pattern.** The KB misconception to pre-empt: "weather and climate are the same." Repeated line: "One hot day doesn't change the climate."

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Big news: London had a baking hot, sunny day yesterday — 30 degrees! So… that makes London a hot country, like Egypt? Put your thumb up if you agree, down if you don't. Hmm. Let me prove you right or wrong today."
**Show:** the two boxes — Weather / Climate.  **Do:** fan yourself dramatically ("one hot day!").
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's the weather usually like in the UK across the year?" → *listen for:* "mild, often rainy, four seasons, not very hot or very cold."

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Two words that sound similar but mean different things. **Weather** is what's happening **today** — sunny now, rain this afternoon. It changes fast. **Climate** is the **usual pattern over many years** — what a place is *normally* like. London's climate is 'mild and rainy' — and one rare hot day doesn't change that, because climate is about the *long run*, not one day. Think of it like this: your *mood* today is weather; your *personality* over years is climate."
**Show / Do:** hold up "It's raining right now" → drop it in **Weather**. Hold up "The UK is usually mild and wet" → drop it in **Climate**, narrating why.
**Emphasize:** "Weather = today, fast. Climate = the usual pattern over years. **One hot day doesn't change the climate.**"

### ⏱ 12–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Sort the next ones with me. 'It snowed last night' — weather or climate? Why? 'Summers here are usually warm' — which box? Listen for the clue words: 'right now', 'today' point to weather; 'usually', 'over the years' point to climate."
**Do:** child sorts 3–4 cards, saying the clue word that decides each.
**Ask:** "So does London's one hot day make it a hot country?" → *listen for:* "no — that's just weather; its climate is still mild."

### ⏱ 20–27 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What's the difference between weather and climate? Give me one example of each." *(Distractor: "weather is rain and climate is sun" — treating them as types, not timescales.)*
- ✅ **Expected:** "Weather is today's conditions; climate is the usual pattern over many years." — probe: *"Which one changes fast?"* (weather).
- ❌ **If "they're the same / both mean hot and cold":** both feel like the same topic → **fix (KB):** "One sunny December day — does that change whether the UK is a mild country? Weather is *today*; climate is *years*."
- ❌ **If "weather is rain, climate is the temperature":** treats them as kinds of thing → **fix:** "It's not *what* it is, it's *how long*. Is 'raining now' a day or many years? What about 'usually rainy'?"

### ⏱ 27–32 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child sorts a fresh set of statements into Weather / Climate alone, writing the clue word for each. Once secure, interleave: write one *new* weather statement and one *new* climate statement of their own.
**Watch for:** sorting by topic (rain/sun) instead of timescale (today/usual).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — sorting by topic not time; 3) reduce to a two-card sort ("right now" vs "usually"); 4) one hint: "Circle the time word"; 5) re-ask one easy card, build back up; 6) "smaller step — mistakes show us what to practise."

### ⏱ 32–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Finish my sentence: weather is ___, climate is ___." → *listen for:* "weather is today/changes fast; climate is the usual pattern over many years."
**Process praise:** "You spotted the *time* clue every card, not just the rain or sun — that's the real difference, and you nailed it."
**Revisit later:** next lesson — *why* climate is hot in some places and cold in others (latitude). Retrieve "weather vs climate" then.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** a two-box sort with the clue words ("right now" / "usually") underlined for them.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain *why* one hot day doesn't change the UK's climate (climate is an average over many years).

**Extension (if time allows):** explain how scientists could *measure* a place's climate (record the weather every day for many years and find the usual pattern).
**Assessment evidence:** child defines weather and climate by timescale and correctly sorts statements.

## Lesson 4 — Climate zones follow latitude
**Geography · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Climate & biomes)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that climate zones follow latitude — hot at the Equator, cold at the poles."

**You will need:** a blank world map; a climate-zone colour key (tropical = red/orange, temperate = green, polar = blue); the **globe**; a torch (to show the angle of sunlight); country cards (Brazil, UK, Greenland, Egypt, Canada).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3's global grid — latitude lines run *across*; the **Equator** is 0°; the **poles** are at the top and bottom; hot near the Equator, cold near the poles.
**Key vocabulary:** **latitude** (across-lines; how far north/south you are), **Equator**, **poles**, **climate zone**, **tropical** (hot all year), **temperate** (mild, with seasons), **polar** (cold all year).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **climate follows latitude** — Equator = hot (tropical), poles = cold (polar), in between (like the UK) = temperate. Pin every zone to its latitude on the globe. Stretch idea: it's the *angle of sunlight* (direct at the Equator, slanted at the poles).

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's a puzzle that's true everywhere on Earth: it's *always* hot near the Equator and *always* cold at the poles — every single year. Why? It's not luck. It comes down to those invisible lines we drew on the globe last year. Let me show you."
**Show:** the globe.  **Do:** run a finger along the Equator ("hottest"), then up to the North Pole ("coldest").
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Point to the Equator on the globe — and is it hot or cold near it?" → *listen for:* finger on the middle line; "hot."
*(If the grid is shaky: name latitude lines as the across-lines, Equator = 0°, poles at top/bottom.)*

### ⏱ 5–15 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Climate follows **latitude** — how far north or south you are. Three big zones. Near the **Equator** the Sun is almost straight overhead, so it's **hot all year** — that's the **tropical** zone. Near the **poles** the Sun's light hits at a slant and spreads thin, so it's **cold all year** — the **polar** zone. In between — where the UK is — it's **temperate**: mild, not too hot or too cold, with proper seasons. So the bands go: hot middle, cold ends, mild in between. It's a pattern you can colour."
**Show / Do:** shine the torch straight down on the globe's middle ("direct, strong heat — tropical") then at a slant near the pole ("spread out, weak — polar"). Colour the three bands on the demo map.
**Emphasize:** "Hot at the **Equator** (tropical), cold at the **poles** (polar), mild in between (temperate). **Climate follows latitude.**"

### ⏱ 15–24 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Colour your map with me, band by band. Find the Equator — colour the band around it tropical (red). Find the poles — colour those bands polar (blue). The big middle bands — temperate (green). Now where's the UK? Which colour band is it in?"
**Do:** child colours the three bands and locates the UK in the temperate (green) band.
**Ask:** "The UK is in which zone — and why isn't it tropical like Brazil?" → *listen for:* "temperate; because it's far from the Equator (higher latitude)."

### ⏱ 24–31 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Where is the climate hot all year, and what zone is the UK in?" *(Distractor: "it's hot near the poles because that's where it's icy" — confusing cold with the wrong end.)*
- ✅ **Expected:** "Hot all year near the **Equator** (tropical); the UK is **temperate**." — probe: *"And what's near the poles?"* (cold, polar).
- ❌ **If "hot at the poles" / mixes Equator and poles:** ends confused → **fix (KB):** "Point to the Equator — middle or ends? The Sun is straight overhead in the *middle*. Poles are the cold ends."
- ❌ **If "the UK is tropical/hot":** thinks the UK is hot → **fix:** "Look how *far* the UK is from the Equator on the globe. Far from the Equator = which zone? Mild — that's temperate."

### ⏱ 31–37 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child places country cards (Brazil, Egypt, UK, Canada, Greenland) into the correct zone on their coloured map and writes the zone name beside each. Once secure, interleave: "Two countries are both far from the Equator but one's hotter — could anything *other* than latitude matter?" (introduce height/coast lightly for stretch).
**Watch for:** placing by *guesswork* not latitude; calling the UK tropical.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — not using latitude; 3) go back to the globe and the torch (direct vs slanted); 4) one hint: "How far from the Equator is it?"; 5) re-ask with one easy country (Brazil), build back up; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 37–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the three zones from the Equator outwards, and which one the UK is in." → *listen for:* "tropical (Equator), temperate (middle), polar (poles); UK is temperate."
**Process praise:** "You used *latitude* to decide each zone instead of guessing — that's real geographical reasoning."
**Revisit later:** next — biomes (the living regions that sit inside these zones). Retrieve the three zones next lesson.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** colour just the three main bands on a world map with the key beside them.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain *why* latitude causes the zones (the angle of sunlight — direct at the Equator, slanted at the poles).

**Extension (if time allows):** mark the Tropics and the Arctic/Antarctic Circles as the *edges* of the hot and cold zones.
**Assessment evidence:** child colours the three zones correctly and states the UK is temperate, hot is near the Equator.

## Lesson 5 — Biomes: nature's big regions
**Geography · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Climate & biomes)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name three biomes and match them to their climate."

**You will need:** biome photo cards (tropical rainforest, hot desert, grassland/savanna, temperate forest, tundra, polar ice); the coloured climate-zone map from Lesson 4; a biome→climate matching mat ("hot & wet / dry / cold").
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** climate zones follow latitude (Lesson 4) — tropical, temperate, polar.
**Key vocabulary:** **biome** (a large region with its own climate, plants and animals), **tropical rainforest** (hot, wet), **desert** (very dry), **grassland/savanna**, **temperate forest** (the UK), **tundra** (cold, treeless), **polar** (ice).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** a **biome is defined by its climate** — rainforest = hot & wet, desert = dry, tundra/polar = cold. The KB misconception to pre-empt: **a desert is defined by being *dry*, not hot or sandy** (Antarctica is a cold desert). Pin each biome to its climate zone on the map.

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Three places: a steamy rainforest dripping with vines; a baking desert with not a drop of water; a frozen, treeless tundra. Each is a whole world of its own — its own weather, its own plants, its own animals. We call each one a **biome**. Today: where on Earth does each one belong, and *why*?"
**Show:** three contrasting biome photo cards fanned out.  **Do:** hold up the climate-zone map beside them.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Which climate zone is hot all year — and where is it?" → *listen for:* "tropical, near the Equator."

### ⏱ 5–15 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **biome** is a big natural region with its **own climate, plants and animals**. And here's the key: the **climate decides the biome**. **Tropical rainforest** — hot *and* very wet — grows near the Equator: dense trees, huge variety of life. **Desert** — almost *no* rain — only tough plants like cacti survive. **Tundra** — cold and treeless, frozen ground near the Arctic. **Temperate forest** — mild with four seasons — that's the UK's leafy woods. So if I tell you the climate, you can often tell me the biome."
**Show / Do:** place each photo card onto the climate-zone map where it belongs (rainforest on the tropical band, tundra near the pole, temperate forest on the UK).
**Emphasize:** "Climate decides the biome. Rainforest = hot **and** wet; desert = **dry**; tundra/polar = cold."

### ⏱ 15–24 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Match these with me. Here's 'hot and wet, near the Equator' — which biome card fits? Here's 'very dry' — which fits? Here's 'cold and frozen' — which fits? Now place each card on the right band of the map."
**Do:** child matches three biomes to their climate on the mat, then places them on the zone map.
**Ask:** "Name three biomes for me — any three." → *listen for:* any three of rainforest, desert, grassland/savanna, temperate forest, tundra, polar.

### ⏱ 24–31 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name three biomes — and tell me, is a desert defined by being *hot* or by being *dry*?" *(Distractor: "a desert is hot and sandy" — the exact KB misconception.)*
- ✅ **Expected:** three valid biomes; "a desert is defined by being **dry**." — probe: *"So could there be a *cold* desert?"* (yes — Antarctica).
- ❌ **If "a desert is hot/sandy":** the Sahara is the famous image → **fix (KB):** "Antarctica gets almost no rain or snow — barely any water. Is it hot? So what really makes a desert — heat, or dryness?"
- ❌ **If names habitats not biomes (e.g. "a pond"):** scale confusion → **fix:** "A biome is a *huge* region across the world. Think bigger — a whole rainforest, a whole desert."

### ⏱ 31–37 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child sorts six biome cards onto the matching mat (hot & wet / dry / cold) and labels each, then places three on the climate-zone map. Once secure, interleave: "Where on the map would a *cold desert* go?" (near a pole — dry *and* cold).
**Watch for:** sorting deserts only as "hot"; muddling biome with a single habitat.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — desert-as-hot vs biome scale; 3) reduce to three cards and the "hot&wet / dry / cold" mat; 4) one hint: "What does *every* desert have almost none of?" (water/rain); 5) re-ask one easy match, build back up; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 37–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Name three biomes and the climate that goes with each." → *listen for:* e.g. "rainforest — hot & wet; desert — dry; tundra — cold."
**Process praise:** "You defined a desert by *dryness*, not just heat — that's a tricky one and you reasoned it out."
**Revisit later:** next — *adaptation*: how plants and animals are built for their biome. Retrieve "three biomes + climate" next lesson.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match three biomes to "hot & wet / dry / cold" with photo cards.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain why a desert is defined by being *dry*, not hot (Antarctica is a cold desert).

**Extension (if time allows):** match a famous place to its biome (Sahara → desert, Amazon → rainforest, Arctic → tundra/polar).
**Assessment evidence:** child names three biomes and matches each to its climate; states deserts are defined by dryness.

---

## Lesson 6 — Adaptation: built for the biome
**Geography · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Climate & biomes)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can give one way a plant or animal is adapted to its biome."

**You will need:** a small **cactus** (or a clear photo) and a **broad leaf** to compare side by side; animal/plant cards (camel, polar bear, cactus, sloth, reindeer); biome mats from Lesson 5.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** biomes and their climates (Lesson 5) — desert is dry, polar/tundra is cold.
**Key vocabulary:** **adaptation** (a special feature that helps a living thing survive its home), **adapted**, **survive**, **spines**, **store water**, **insulate**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** living things are **adapted to a particular biome** — the cactus stores water and has spines instead of leaves to lose less water (desert); the polar bear has thick fur and fat (polar). The KB misconception: **"animals can live anywhere"** — they're built for *one* biome. Repeated question: "Why is it built that way?"

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Two mysteries. Why does a cactus have sharp **spines** instead of soft leaves? And why does a polar bear wear a thick fur coat it never takes off? Neither is an accident. Each one is *perfectly built* for its home. Today you become an **adaptation detective** — working out *why* each creature is the way it is."
**Show:** the cactus next to the broad leaf.  **Do:** point to the cactus spines, then the wide flat leaf.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's the climate like in a desert?" → *listen for:* "very dry, little water."

### ⏱ 5–15 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "An **adaptation** is a special feature that helps a living thing survive *its* home. Watch the cactus. A desert has almost no water — so a cactus has a fat stem to **store water** for months. And those **spines**? They're its leaves — but tiny and thin, so the plant loses *less* water than this big flat leaf would. Every part of the cactus is solving the desert's problem: not enough water. Now the polar bear: the Arctic is freezing, so it has **thick fur and a layer of fat** to stay warm. The biome sets the problem; the adaptation is the answer."
**Show / Do:** hold the broad leaf ("loses lots of water — fine in the rainforest, deadly in a desert") next to the cactus ("spines lose almost none").
**Emphasize:** "Each living thing is **adapted to one biome**. Ask: *why is it built that way?*"

### ⏱ 15–24 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Detective work together. The cactus stores water in its stem — *why*? The polar bear has thick fur — *why*? Now match these cards to a biome: camel, sloth, reindeer — and for one of them, tell me a feature that helps it survive."
**Do:** child matches animals to biomes and explains *one* adaptation (e.g. camel stores fat/water → desert; reindeer thick coat → tundra).
**Ask:** "Could a polar bear survive in a hot desert? Why not?" → *listen for:* "no — its thick fur and fat are for cold; it's adapted to the Arctic, not the desert."

### ⏱ 24–31 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Give one way a cactus is adapted to the desert." *(Distractor: "it just lives there" — no mechanism; or "animals can live anywhere" surfacing.)*
- ✅ **Expected:** "It stores water in its stem" *or* "it has spines instead of leaves to lose less water." — probe: *"And how does that help in a *dry* place?"*
- ❌ **If "animals/plants can live anywhere":** sees pets everywhere → **fix (KB):** "Could a camel survive the freezing Arctic? Could a polar bear survive a hot desert? Each is built for *one* biome."
- ❌ **If names a feature but not *why* (e.g. "it has spines"):** half an answer → **fix:** "Good — now *why* are spines better than big leaves in a place with no water?" (lose less water).

### ⏱ 31–37 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child matches each animal/plant card to its biome and writes **one adaptation that explains why it survives there**. Once secure, interleave: "Pick one creature and swap its biome — what would go wrong?" (e.g. polar bear in desert: overheats).
**Watch for:** "lives anywhere"; naming a feature without the *why*.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "lives anywhere" vs missing the reason; 3) go back to cactus vs leaf in hand; 4) one hint: "What problem does this biome cause — and how does this feature solve it?"; 5) re-ask with the cactus (easiest), build back up; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 37–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Give me one adaptation and the reason it helps." → *listen for:* e.g. "a cactus stores water in its stem because the desert is dry."
**Process praise:** "You didn't just spot the feature — you explained *why* it helps. That's real detective reasoning."
**Revisit later:** Unit 2 check next. Bring "adapted to one biome" back when we compare regions in later years.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match animal → biome with the biome mats as support, then say one feature.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain why a camel couldn't survive the Arctic (built for heat/dryness, not cold).

**Extension (if time allows):** invent an imaginary creature perfectly adapted to one biome and explain two of its adaptations.
**Assessment evidence:** child gives one adaptation of a plant or animal *and* explains how it helps survival in that biome.

---

**End-of-unit check (Unit 2):** Explain weather vs climate (timescale); explain that climate zones follow latitude (tropical / temperate / polar); name three biomes and match to climate; give one adaptation. **Misconception watch:** weather ≠ climate; deserts are defined by **dryness** (not heat/sand); wild animals are **adapted to a particular biome**.

# Unit 3 — Fieldwork: being a geographer outdoors

**Essential question:** How do geographers collect their own reliable evidence about a real place?

---

## Lesson 7 — The fieldwork cycle
**Geography · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Fieldwork)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can put the steps of a fieldwork investigation in order."

**You will need:** five step cards (ask a question / choose a method / collect data / present it / explain & conclude); method cards (tally count, survey/questionnaire, sketch map, measuring); example question cards ("How many cars pass our house?", "What do people like about the park?", "Where are the trees on our street?").
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** map skills so far — geographers use maps, but they also gather their *own* information.
**Key vocabulary:** **fieldwork** (collecting your own data outdoors), **data** (the information you collect), **method** (the way you collect it), **tally**, **survey/questionnaire**, **sketch map**, **present**, **conclude** (the answer you reach).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** fieldwork follows a **cycle in order — ask → choose a method → collect → present → conclude.** The KB misconception: fieldwork is **structured, not "a day off."** Repeated line: "First the question, *then* the method — never the other way round."

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Geographers don't just *read* maps — they go outside and find things out for themselves. That's called **fieldwork**. But you can't just wander off and look around — there's a proper way to do it, in the right order. Today you learn the steps real geographers follow."
**Show:** the five step cards (shuffled).  **Do:** mime grabbing a clipboard and stepping outside.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If you wanted to find out how busy our street is, how could you find out *for sure* — not just guess?" → *listen for:* "go out and count / watch and record."

### ⏱ 5–15 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Fieldwork is a **cycle** — five steps in order. **One: ask a question** — what do we actually want to know? **Two: choose a method** — *how* will we find out? A **tally count**, a **survey** where you ask people, a **sketch map**, or **measuring**. **Three: collect the data** — go out and record it. **Four: present it** — turn it into a chart or table so others can see it. **Five: conclude** — answer the question, using your data. And the golden rule: the **question comes first**. You pick the method to fit the question, not the other way round."
**Show / Do:** lay the five step cards left to right in order; under "choose a method", fan out the four method cards.
**Emphasize:** "Ask → choose a method → collect → present → conclude. **Question first, always.**"

### ⏱ 15–24 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Order these with me. Which step is *first*? Which is *last*? Now match a method to a question: 'How many cars pass our house?' — tally count or questionnaire? 'What do people like about the park?' — which method? 'Where are the trees on our street?' — which?"
**Do:** child orders the five steps and matches each question to a sensible method (cars → tally; opinions → survey; locations → sketch map).
**Ask:** "Why does the *question* have to come before the *method*?" → *listen for:* "because the method has to fit what you're trying to find out."

### ⏱ 24–31 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Put the fieldwork steps in order: present, ask a question, collect data, choose a method, conclude." *(Distractor: starting with 'collect data' — diving in before asking a question.)*
- ✅ **Expected:** ask a question → choose a method → collect data → present → conclude — probe: *"What goes wrong if you collect data before asking a question?"* (you don't know what to collect).
- ❌ **If "collect data first":** keen to dive in → **fix (KB):** "Before you collect anything — what are you trying to *find out*? You need the question first or you won't know what to count."
- ❌ **If "conclude before present":** mixes the last two → **fix:** "Can you explain your answer *before* you've organised your data into a chart? Present it, *then* conclude from it."

### ⏱ 31–37 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child orders the five steps unaided, then **plans a real mini-investigation** about their own street or home: writes a question and chooses a fitting method. Once secure, interleave: match all four methods to four different questions.
**Watch for:** method-before-question; choosing a method that can't answer the question (e.g. a survey to count cars).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — order slip vs mismatched method; 3) reduce to ordering with picture support; 4) one hint: "What do you want to *find out* first?"; 5) re-ask just "first step / last step", build back up; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 37–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Say the five fieldwork steps in order." → *listen for:* "ask a question, choose a method, collect data, present, conclude."
**Process praise:** "You put the *question* first and picked a method that actually fits it — that's exactly how a real geographer plans."
**Revisit later:** next — the golden rule that makes fieldwork *fair*. Retrieve the five steps next lesson.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** order the five steps with picture support beside each card.
- **Stretch (challenge):** match three methods to three different questions and justify each choice.

**Extension (if time allows):** plan a full investigation for a question they care about — question, method, and how they'd present it.
**Assessment evidence:** child orders the five-step cycle and chooses a method that fits a given question.

---

## Lesson 8 — Be systematic and fair
**Geography · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Fieldwork)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why fieldwork must use the same method each time to be fair."

**You will need:** a **flawed-plan** example written up ("Count cars at the gate for 2 minutes; count cars at the corner for 10 minutes"); a tally-chart template; a clock/timer.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the fieldwork cycle (Lesson 7) — collecting data; method comes after the question.
**Key vocabulary:** **systematic** (same way every time, organised), **fair**, **compare**, **reliable**, **consistent**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** to compare results, you must be **systematic and fair — same method, same time, same question** — or the data is meaningless. The KB misconception watch: **the method must stay consistent.** Repeated line: "Same method every time, or you can't compare."

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "I did some 'fieldwork'. I counted cars at the gate for **2 minutes** and got 6. Then I counted cars at the corner for **10 minutes** and got 20. So the corner is busier — twice as busy! …Is that fair? Can I really compare those two counts? Put your thumb up or down. Today you'll catch the cheat in my plan."
**Show:** the flawed-plan example.  **Do:** point to "2 minutes" and "10 minutes" with raised eyebrows.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What are the steps of fieldwork, quickly?" → *listen for:* "ask, choose a method, collect, present, conclude."

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Here's the golden rule of good fieldwork: be **systematic and fair**. That means the **same method, same length of time, same question** — every single time. Look at my plan: 2 minutes here, 10 minutes there. Of *course* the 10-minute spot got more cars — I gave it more time! That doesn't mean it's busier. My data is **meaningless** because I wasn't fair. If I count for the *same* time at both spots, *then* I can actually compare. Systematic data is trustworthy; messy data isn't."
**Show / Do:** circle the unfair part of the plan; rewrite it so both counts are "10 minutes, same time of day."
**Emphasize:** "**Same method every time, or you can't compare.** Systematic and fair."

### ⏱ 12–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Fix my plan with me. What needs to be the *same* at both places? The time? The length? Anything else — like the time of day? Now let's design a *fair* tally count: same 10 minutes, same time of day, same way of counting."
**Do:** child identifies the unfair difference, fixes it, and helps set up a fair tally chart.
**Ask:** "Why does the *length of time* have to be the same at both spots?" → *listen for:* "so it's a fair comparison — more time would always mean more cars."

### ⏱ 20–27 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Why must you use the same method each time in fieldwork?" *(Distractor: "so it's easier" — missing the *fairness/comparison* point.)*
- ✅ **Expected:** "So it's **fair** and you can **compare** the results; systematic data is meaningful, messy data isn't." — probe: *"What would go wrong if you didn't?"* (you couldn't trust the comparison).
- ❌ **If "so it's easier/quicker":** misses the point → **fix (KB):** "It's not about easy — if you counted 2 minutes here and 10 there, would more cars mean *busier*, or just *more time*? You need it fair to *compare*."
- ❌ **If "it doesn't really matter":** doesn't see the flaw → **fix:** "I counted longer at the corner. Did that make it busier, or just give it more chances? Be the judge — is that fair?"

### ⏱ 27–32 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child is given a second flawed plan and must **spot and fix the unfair choice(s)** (e.g. different times, different counters, different lengths). Once secure, interleave a plan with *two* unfair choices to find.
**Watch for:** fixing one unfairness but missing another; thinking "more data = better" regardless of fairness.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — not seeing the comparison problem; 3) reduce to two plans, "choose the fair one"; 4) one hint: "What's *different* between the two counts that shouldn't be?"; 5) re-ask the time-length example, build back up; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 32–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Finish this: fieldwork must use the same method each time so that ___." → *listen for:* "it's fair and you can compare the results."
**Process praise:** "You caught the unfairness *and* fixed it — that's a geographer making sure their evidence is reliable."
**Revisit later:** next — actually collecting, presenting and concluding from data. Retrieve "same method = fair" next lesson.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** choose the "fair" plan from two options and say why.
- **Stretch (challenge):** spot and fix *two* unfair choices in a sample plan and explain each.

**Extension (if time allows):** write a one-line "fairness rule" for a fieldwork poster to display in the learning space.
**Assessment evidence:** child explains that the method must stay the same so results are fair and comparable, and fixes a flawed plan.

## Lesson 9 — Collect, present and conclude
**Geography · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Fieldwork)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can present fieldwork data and use it to answer a question."

**You will need:** a tally chart with the sample data (9am: 4 park users; 4pm: 22 park users); bar-chart paper (labelled axes: time on the bottom, number of people up the side); coloured pencils; *(if a safe space is available with a grown-up)* a clipboard for a real 10-minute count.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the fieldwork cycle (Lesson 7) and the fairness rule (Lesson 8) — same method, present then conclude.
**Key vocabulary:** **tally**, **bar chart**, **present** (show data clearly), **conclude** (answer the question using the data), **busiest**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** data must be **presented *and* explained, not just collected** — turn the tally into a **bar chart**, then **conclude** an answer to the question. The KB misconception watch: collecting isn't enough — you must present and *conclude*. Repeated question: "So what's our answer — and how does the data show it?"

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "We did a real count. At **9am**, only **4** people were in the park. At **4pm**, **22** people were there. So… is our park well used, and *when*? You can't answer that just by staring at two numbers. We need to *show* them, then *read* the answer off. Let's do it."
**Show:** the tally chart with 4 (9am) and 22 (4pm).  **Do:** tap the two numbers, then point to the empty bar-chart paper.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What were the last two steps of the fieldwork cycle, after collecting data?" → *listen for:* "present it, then conclude."

### ⏱ 5–15 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Two jobs left: **present** and **conclude**. First, present — I turn the numbers into a **bar chart** so anyone can see the pattern in a glance. Time goes along the bottom: 9am, 4pm. Number of people goes up the side. The 9am bar is short — 4. The 4pm bar is tall — 22. Now **conclude** — I answer the question *using* the chart: 'The park is used **much more after school**, at 4pm. We know because the 4pm bar is over five times taller than the 9am bar.' Collecting the data wasn't enough — I had to *show* it and *explain* what it means."
**Show / Do:** draw both bars to the right height on the chart, narrating; then say the conclusion aloud while pointing at the tall bar as evidence.
**Emphasize:** "Present it, then **conclude** — answer the question *using* the data. Collecting alone isn't fieldwork finished."

### ⏱ 15–25 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Build the chart with me. How tall is the 9am bar — find 4 up the side. How tall is the 4pm bar — find 22. Now you tell me the conclusion: when is the park busier, and how does the chart prove it?"
**Do:** child draws both bars to the correct height and states the conclusion with the chart as evidence.
**Ask:** "When is the park busier, and how do you *know* from the chart?" → *listen for:* "4pm — its bar is much taller (22 vs 4)."

*(If a safe space and a grown-up are available: spend this beat doing a real 10-minute count instead, recording with a tally — same method, same length — then return to charting.)*

### ⏱ 25–32 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What is fieldwork in geography?" *(Distractor: "going outside / a day out" — the 'day off' misconception.)*
- ✅ **Expected:** "Collecting your own data outdoors by observing, measuring, counting and recording — to **answer a question**." — probe: *"And after collecting, what two things must you still do?"* (present it; conclude).
- ❌ **If "going outside / a fun day out":** the 'day off' misconception → **fix (KB):** "Is it just looking around? What did we *do* with our counts — and *why* did we count at all?" (to answer a question, then present and conclude).
- ❌ **If "counting things" (collecting only):** stops at collection → **fix:** "We counted — but were we finished? What did we make from the numbers, and what did we *conclude*?"

### ⏱ 32–37 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child completes a part-made bar chart from a fresh tally and **writes a one-sentence conclusion** answering the question. Once secure, interleave: add a third time (e.g. 12pm: 11) and re-read which time is busiest.
**Watch for:** bars drawn to the wrong height; reading the chart but not *concluding* an answer; concluding without using the data.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — chart height vs missing conclusion; 3) reduce to filling one bar then reading the tallest; 4) one hint: "Point to the tallest bar — that's your busiest time"; 5) re-ask "which bar is tallest?", build to the full conclusion; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 37–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me our whole investigation in one breath: the question, what we found, and our conclusion." → *listen for:* "We asked when the park is busiest; we counted at 9am and 4pm; the chart shows 4pm is much busier."
**Process praise:** "You didn't stop at the numbers — you presented them *and* concluded an answer with evidence. That's a complete piece of fieldwork."
**Revisit later:** end-of-year mastery checks. Bring the fieldwork cycle back any time you can investigate a real local question.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** fill in a part-made bar chart and read off the busiest time.
- **Stretch (challenge):** write a full conclusion explaining *why* the busier time is busier (e.g. school has finished, so families visit after 3:30pm).

**Extension (if time allows):** suggest one *new* fieldwork question their data raises (e.g. "Is the park busier on weekends?") and the fair method to answer it.
**Assessment evidence:** child presents data as a correct bar chart and writes a conclusion that answers the question using the data.

---

**End-of-unit check (Unit 3):** Order the fieldwork cycle (ask → choose a method → collect → present → conclude); explain why the method must stay the same (fair, comparable); present data and draw a conclusion that answers the question. **Misconception watch:** fieldwork is **structured** (not "a day off"); the method must be **consistent**; data must be **presented and explained**, not just collected.

# End-of-year mastery checks (Year 4 Geography)

Use these as a spaced retrieval review across the final weeks. A Year 4 child who has mastered this year's geography should be able to:

1. **(Water cycle — order)** "Name the four stages of the water cycle in order." → evaporation → condensation → precipitation → collection. *Probe:* "What powers it?" (the Sun).
2. **(Water cycle — recycled)** "Is rain brand-new water? Explain." → no — it's the same water recycled: evaporated, condensed, fallen again. *Misconception watch:* "new water made in clouds."
3. **(Climate)** "What's the difference between weather and climate, and where is it hot all year?" → weather is today's conditions; climate is the usual pattern over many years; hot all year near the **Equator** (tropical); the UK is **temperate**. *Misconception watch:* one hot day ≠ a hot climate.
4. **(Biomes + adaptation)** "Name three biomes, match one to its climate, and give one adaptation." → e.g. rainforest (hot & wet), desert (dry), tundra (cold); cactus stores water / has spines to lose less water. *Misconception watch:* deserts are defined by **dryness**; animals are adapted to **one** biome.
5. **(Fieldwork — the cycle)** "Order the fieldwork cycle and say why the method must stay the same." → ask → choose a method → collect → present → conclude; the same method keeps it **fair and comparable**.
6. **(Fieldwork — data)** "Present this data and draw a conclusion." → child turns a tally into a bar chart and concludes an answer to the question, using the data as evidence.

## Closing note

Year 4 geography moved the child from *naming places* to *understanding systems and processes*: the **water cycle** (why water never runs out), **climate zones and biomes** (why latitude decides rainforest, desert or tundra), and **fieldwork** (collecting their own fair, reliable evidence). The three strands lock together — the water cycle feeds the rivers of Year 3 and the civilisations of history; climate zones pin to the global grid; fieldwork turns map skills into real investigation.

Three disciplinary habits should now be visible in how the child *thinks*, not just what they recall: **"how do we know?"** (evidence over guessing — the bowl demo, the bar chart), **"what changed and why?"** (the cycle, the bands of latitude), and **"is this fair and reliable?"** (the systematic-fieldwork rule). End every lesson on an earned success with specific **process praise** — the reasoning, the strategy, the careful method — never "you're so clever." Hand the four mastery threads to spaced review so they stay alive into Year 5's mountains, volcanoes, settlements and map skills.
