> reasoning probes ("how do you know?", "would it always?"). Every CHECK gives the expected answer plus

## Lesson 1 — Classifying living things
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Living things & habitats)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can classify living things by observable features and explain there's more than one sensible way."

**You will need:** a set of **living-thing picture cards** (e.g. spider, ladybird, bee, robin, frog, snail, butterfly, earthworm, fish, dog); **3–4 sorting hoops** (or labelled paper plates / drawn circles); a sticky note pad for writing group labels; a whiteboard.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Years 1–2 — can name animal groups (mammal, bird, fish, insect) and observe body features (legs, wings, fur).
**Key vocabulary:** **classify** (sort things into groups by features they share); **feature** (a part you can observe — legs, wings, fur); **observable** (you can actually see or check it); **group**; **grouping rule** (the yes/no test you sort by).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Scientists **classify** by **observable features** — clear, you-can-see-it rules, not opinions. The big idea to land: **the same set can be grouped sensibly in more than one way** (by body, by diet, by habitat). Pre-empt "there's only one right way to group." Repeat: **"A good grouping rule has a clear yes/no answer you can see."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Imagine you tipped out a whole bucket of garden minibeasts onto the table — spiders, beetles, worms, snails, all wriggling about. Your job: sort every single one into tidy groups. How would you even start? There's no wrong place to begin — but there IS a clever way scientists do it. Let's find it."

**Show:** the picture cards spread in a messy pile.  **Do:** make the pile genuinely chaotic; look slightly overwhelmed to invite the child in.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From before — tell me two animal groups you already know, and one feature that puts an animal in that group." → *listen for:* e.g. "birds — they have feathers"; "insects — six legs."
*(If the prerequisite is shaky: hold up the robin and the spider — "What's different about their bodies? Legs? Wings? Feathers?" Accept everyday answers.)*

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore first)
> **Say:** "Let's be sorting scientists. Don't pick group names yet — first just *look* at what's the same and what's different. Put all the ones with wings here, all the ones without wings there."

**Do:** hand the child the cards; let them physically sort into two hoops by **wings / no wings**. Then say: "Now mix them up and sort the SAME cards a different way — by **number of legs**."
**Ask:** "Did the same spider end up in different groups depending on the rule we used?" → *listen for:* yes — "no wings" group, then "eight legs" group. "So is there only one right way to sort them?" → *listen for:* no, there are several.

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXPLAIN (I do — now name it)
> **Say:** "You've just discovered what scientists call **classifying** — sorting living things into groups by **features** they share. The secret is the rule you sort by. A *good* rule is **observable** — you can see it and it has a clear yes-or-no answer: 'Does it have wings?' 'Does it have six legs?' A *bad* rule is an opinion: 'Is it scary?' or 'Is it cute?' — because your answer and mine might be different. And here's the clever bit: the *same* set of creatures can be grouped sensibly in more than one way — by their **body** (legs, wings), by what they **eat**, or by where they **live**."

**Show / Do:** write two columns on the board — **Good rules** (Does it have wings? Does it have fur? More than four legs?) and **Opinion rules** (Is it nice? Is it fast? Is it scary?). Tick the good ones.
**Emphasize:** "A good grouping rule has a **clear yes/no answer you can see**. And there's more than one sensible way to group."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Is **'Does it have wings?'** a good grouping question? Why? And what about **'Is it friendly?'**"
- ✅ **Expected:** "'Does it have wings?' is good — clear yes/no you can observe. 'Is it friendly?' is not — it's an opinion." Then probe: *"What makes one a science question and the other not?"*
- ❌ **If "'Is it friendly?' is fine":** the child thinks any question works for sorting. *Why:* every question feels usable. → **fix:** "If I say the spider is friendly and you say it's scary — who's right? Can we both look and *agree* on the answer? A grouping rule has to give the SAME answer for everyone."
- ❌ **If "there's only one right way to group these":** the child treats the first sort as the only sort. *Why:* groups feel fixed. → **fix:** "Remember we sorted the same cards by wings, AND by legs? Both worked. Could you also sort them by what they eat? Then how many sensible ways are there?"

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Give the child the full card set and challenge: **"Group these two completely different sensible ways, and tell me your rule each time."** (e.g. first by *number of legs*, then by *flies / doesn't fly* or *lives in water / on land*.) They must state each rule as a clear observable yes/no question.
**Watch for:** the child slipping into an opinion rule ("the nice ones"). Catch it: "Can we both check that by looking?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the grouping; 2) name *why* — "I think the tricky part is making the rule one we can both *see*"; 3) go concrete — hand them two cards and ask "What's ONE thing different about these two bodies?"; 4) give ONE hint — a sentence starter: "Does it have ___ ?"; 5) re-ask the smaller version (sort into just two groups by that one feature), then build back to a second way; 6) keep it safe — "Mistakes show us what to practise. Let's take a smaller step."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the teacher: what makes a grouping rule a GOOD one? And is there only one way to sort a set of living things?" → *listen for:* a clear yes/no observable feature; no — there are several sensible ways.
**Process praise:** "You didn't just guess groups — you tested every rule against 'can we both see the answer?', and you found more than one way to sort. That's exactly how a classifying scientist thinks."
**Revisit later:** Bring back "good rule or opinion?" as a 1-minute retrieval starter at the top of Lesson 2 (keys are built from good rules).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** sort by **one obvious feature** first (wings / no wings), with cards in hand, before attempting a second way.
- **Stretch (challenge):** group the same set **two different ways** AND name a *third* sensible rule; ask "would your rule always work, even for an animal we haven't seen?"

**Extension (if time allows):** Invent a secret grouping rule, sort the cards by it, and challenge the tutor to guess the rule by looking at the groups.
**Assessment evidence:** Child classifies the set in **two sensible ways**, states each rule as an observable yes/no question, and explains that more than one grouping is valid.

## Lesson 2 — Making and using a classification key
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Living things & habitats)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a yes/no key to identify a living thing."

**You will need:** a **sample classification key** (a simple branching one, e.g. for garden birds, ending in robin / blackbird / pigeon / sparrow); a robin picture (or other bird the key identifies); **4 toys or objects** that differ clearly (e.g. red car, blue brick, teddy, ball); a **blank key template** (boxes joined by yes/no arrows); pencil.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 1 — a good grouping rule is a clear observable yes/no feature, not an opinion.
**Key vocabulary:** **classification key** (a tool that identifies a living thing by asking yes/no questions); **yes/no question**; **identify** (work out exactly what something is); **split** (each question divides the group in two).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A **key** asks a chain of **yes/no questions**, and **each answer splits the group in two** until only one thing is left. Key questions must be **observable** (no opinions), just like Lesson 1's rules. Pre-empt "key questions can be opinions ('is it cute?')." Repeat: **"Each yes/no question cuts the group in half until one is left."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Have you ever played 20 Questions — where I think of something and you guess it with yes/no questions? 'Is it an animal?' 'Does it have four legs?' Here's a secret: that game is actually a real science tool. Scientists use the very same trick to identify any creature they find. It's called a **classification key**. Want to see one work?"

**Show:** the sample key, folded so only the first question shows.  **Do:** look conspiratorial, as if revealing a magic trick.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From last time — what makes a question good enough to sort by?" → *listen for:* a clear yes/no answer you can observe; not an opinion.
*(If shaky: "Is 'Does it have feathers?' good? Is 'Is it pretty?' good? Why?")*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do — follow a key)
> **Say:** "Let's use this key to identify a mystery bird together. I'll read each question; you look at the bird and answer yes or no, and we follow the arrow."

**Show / Do:** put the robin picture on the table. Walk down the key aloud:
- *"Does it have feathers?"* → Yes → "Good — that means it's a **bird**, and we follow this arrow."
- *"Does it have a red front?"* → Yes → follow the arrow.
- *"Is it smaller than a pigeon?"* → Yes → "**Robin!**"
**Ask:** "Notice what happened at each question — how many choices were left before, and how many after?" → *listen for:* it kept cutting the group in two; each yes/no got rid of half. "If the bird had NO red front, where would the key have sent us?" → *listen for:* down the 'no' arrow to a different bird.

### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name it)
> **Say:** "That tool is a **classification key**. Here's how it works: every step is a **yes/no question**, and each answer **splits the group in two** — it throws away half the possibilities. Question by question, you narrow down until only ONE living thing is left. And the rule from last lesson still holds: every key question must be **observable** — 'Does it have a red front?' works; 'Is it a friendly bird?' does NOT, because we couldn't agree on the answer."

**Show / Do:** trace the key path with your finger, saying "split… split… one left." Point to a branch and show how the 'no' answer leads somewhere different.
**Emphasize:** "**Each yes/no question cuts the group in half until one is left** — and every question must be one we can both check by looking."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Is **'Is it friendly?'** a good key question? Why or why not?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "No — it's an opinion, not an observable feature." Then probe: *"What kind of question SHOULD a key use instead?"* (one with a clear yes/no you can see).
- ❌ **If "yes, it's fine":** the child accepts any yes/no question. *Why:* it sounds like a yes/no question. → **fix:** "Could you and I disagree about whether a bird is friendly? Yes. A key has to give EVERYONE the same answer. Change it to something we can both see — 'Does it have a curved beak?'"
- ❌ **If the child changes it to another opinion ("Is it pretty?"):** still not observable. *Why:* swapping one feeling for another. → **fix:** "Pretty is still in your head, not on the bird. Point to a *part* of the bird we could measure or count or see — a colour, a beak shape, the number of toes."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Put the **4 objects** in front of the child and give the **blank key template**. Challenge: **"Write a key with yes/no questions that always leads to exactly the right object."** (e.g. "Does it have wheels?" → car; "Is it red?" splits the rest, etc.) Then test it: you pick an object secretly, answer their questions truthfully, and see if the key lands on the right one.
**Watch for:** questions that don't split the group cleanly (two objects both "yes"), or opinion questions. Catch it: "If I answer yes, how many objects are still possible? Can you make it just one?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the question; 2) name *why* — "the tricky part is finding a feature only SOME of them have"; 3) go concrete — line the 4 objects up and ask "What does this one have that the others don't?"; 4) give ONE hint — a sentence starter: "Does it have ___ ?"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (split just two objects first), then add the rest; 6) keep it safe — "Good scientists test and fix their keys. Let's adjust one step."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me in your own words — what does a classification key *do*, and what happens at each yes/no question?" → *listen for:* it identifies a living thing; each question splits the group in two until one is left.
**Process praise:** "You built a key and then TESTED it on a hidden object to check it really worked — testing your own tool is exactly what a scientist does."
**Revisit later:** Use "what does each yes/no question do?" as a retrieval warm-up before the end-of-unit check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** **follow a ready-made key** to identify two things, rather than building one.
- **Stretch (challenge):** write a **3-question key for four items** with no overlaps, then prove it works for every item; ask "could one extra question handle a fifth object?"

**Extension (if time allows):** Make a real-life key for the family — yes/no questions that always land on the right family member (Has glasses? Taller than the table?).
**Assessment evidence:** Child uses a yes/no key to correctly identify a living thing, and writes/uses at least one valid observable key question that splits a group.

## Lesson 3 — Environments change, and the dangers
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Living things & habitats)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how environmental change can endanger living things, and how humans help."

**You will need:** a **pond story sequence** (4–5 picture/sentence cards: healthy pond → litter & dirty water → fewer frogs/fish → people clean & plant → wildlife returns); **help/harm action cards** (litter, cut down trees, oil spill, recycle, plant trees, make a nature reserve, clean a river, leave a pond for wildlife); two labelled areas: **HELPS wildlife / HARMS wildlife**; a picture of an endangered animal the child likes (panda, tiger).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Year 2 — habitats give food, water and shelter; living things depend on their environment.
**Key vocabulary:** **environment** (the conditions and surroundings of living things); **change** (natural or human-caused); **natural** vs **human-caused**; **danger / endangered** (so few left they could disappear); **harm**; **help**; **conservation** (protecting living things).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Environments **change** — some changes **natural**, some **human-caused** — and big changes can put living things in **danger** by removing their food, water or shelter. Crucially, humans **both harm AND help**. Pre-empt "humans only harm" and "animals just move somewhere else easily." Repeat: **"When a habitat changes too much, living things can lose food, water or shelter — and humans can help fix it."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Picture a lovely pond. Frogs, fish, dragonflies, all happy. Now picture people starting to drop litter in it — plastic bottles, bags — and the water turning dirty and green. What do you think starts to happen to those frogs and fish? Let's follow this pond's story and find out — it doesn't end the way you might expect."

**Show:** the first card (healthy pond) and a hint of the second (litter).  **Do:** lay the story face-down so it can be revealed one card at a time.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From your habitat work — what three things does a habitat give living things?" → *listen for:* food, water, shelter.
*(If shaky: "What do the frogs need from the pond to stay alive? Clean water? Food like insects? A safe place?")*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do — follow the story)
> **Say:** "Let's turn the cards one at a time and you tell me what happens to the wildlife at each step."

**Do:** reveal the cards in order. At each, ask the child to narrate.
**Ask:**
- (litter & dirty water) "Is this change **natural**, or caused by **humans**? What does the dirty water take away from the frogs?" → *listen for:* human-caused; clean water and food.
- (fewer frogs/fish) "So when the habitat changed too much, what happened to the animals?" → *listen for:* fewer survived; they're in danger.
- (people clean & plant) "Now what are the humans doing — harming or helping?" → *listen for:* helping.
- (wildlife returns) "Same pond, same people — but two completely different effects. What does that tell us about humans?" → *listen for:* humans can both harm and help.

### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name it)
> **Say:** "Here's the science. The **environment** — everything around living things — can **change**. Some changes are **natural**, like a drought or a flood. Some are **human-caused**, like litter, pollution, or cutting down forests. When a habitat changes too much, the living things can lose their **food, water or shelter** — and their numbers drop. If a kind of animal gets so few that it could disappear, we say it's **endangered** and in **danger**. But — and this matters — humans don't only harm. We also **help**: cleaning litter, planting trees, making nature reserves and ponds, recycling, protecting endangered animals. That's called **conservation**."

**Show / Do:** point to the panda/tiger picture as an example of an endangered animal; point back along the pond story to show harm then help.
**Emphasize:** "**Humans both harm AND help.** And a habitat change is dangerous because it can take away **food, water or shelter**."

### ⏱ 16–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name **one way humans help** the environment. And then — true or false: 'If a pond gets ruined, the frogs can just easily hop off and live somewhere else.'"
- ✅ **Expected:** a help (nature reserves / recycling / planting trees / cleaning litter); and "false — a good new home might be far away or already full, and many animals can't move easily." Probe: *"Why isn't moving as easy as it sounds?"*
- ❌ **If "humans only harm the environment":** the news focuses on damage. *Why:* harm is what the child hears about. → **fix:** "Think back to the pond — what did the people do in the LAST picture? Cleaning, planting — is that harm or help? So can humans help too?"
- ❌ **If "yes, animals just move somewhere else easily":** moving seems simple. *Why:* it sounds easy to relocate. → **fix:** "If your house disappeared, could you instantly find an empty one nearby with food and a bed? The next good pond might be miles away, or already full of frogs. Many animals can't travel far, so they're in danger."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Give the child the **help/harm action cards** and the two labelled areas. They sort each action into **HELPS wildlife** or **HARMS wildlife**, saying *why* for each ("Recycling helps because it means less rubbish in habitats"). Then: **"Choose one helpful action you could really do this week, and tell me what good it would do."**
**Watch for:** sorting by gut feeling without a reason, or thinking every human action is harmful. Catch it: "What does this action do to the animals' food, water or shelter?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the sort; 2) name *why* — "let's check what the action does to food, water or shelter"; 3) go concrete — point back to the pond story for a matching example; 4) give ONE hint — "Does this give animals MORE of what they need, or take it away?"; 5) re-ask with just three clear cards, then add the rest; 6) keep it safe — "Sorting these is tricky; let's reason through one together."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the scientist: why can a change to a habitat put an animal in danger? And give me one way humans can help." → *listen for:* it can lose food/water/shelter; reserves / recycling / planting / cleaning litter.
**Process praise:** "You gave a *reason* for every card — you didn't just sort, you explained how each action changes food, water or shelter. That's real cause-and-effect thinking."
**Revisit later:** Follow up next week — "Did you do your helpful action? What happened?" Bring "name one help, one harm" into the end-of-unit check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** sort just **three** clear help/harm actions with the reason said aloud for each.
- **Stretch (challenge):** choose one helpful action to do this week and **explain its chain of effects** ("less litter → cleaner water → frogs have what they need → more survive"); ask "would this always help, or could it ever go wrong?"

**Extension (if time allows):** Design a small "help wildlife" plan for home or school (a bug hotel, a bird feeder, a recycling rule) and predict what it will do for the animals.
**Assessment evidence:** Child explains how a habitat change endangers living things (lost food/water/shelter), names at least one way humans help, and rejects the "animals just move easily" idea with a reason.

---

**End-of-unit 1 check:** Classify a set **two sensible ways**; **use a yes/no key** to identify something; **explain how a change can endanger** living things and **name one way humans help**.

## Lesson 4 — The digestive journey
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Animals including humans)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can order the parts of the digestive system and say what each does."

**You will need:** a **digestive-system diagram** (labelled: mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine); a large **body outline** on paper or floor (or a child to draw around); **ordering cards** for the five parts with their jobs; a real **apple** (to bite, for the hook); optional: a long piece of tubing/tights stuffed with a ball to show "pushing food down."
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Year 3 (Nutrition) — we eat food to get what our body needs; we have a mouth, throat and tummy.
**Key vocabulary:** **digestive system** (the parts that break food down so the body can use it); **mouth**, **oesophagus** (tube that pushes food down), **stomach** (churns food with juices), **small intestine** (absorbs nutrients into the blood), **large intestine** (takes back water, makes waste); **absorb** (take into the blood); **nutrient** (the goodness in food); **waste**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Digestion is a **whole journey** in order — mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine — not just "into the tummy." The key correction: **most nutrients are absorbed in the SMALL INTESTINE**, not the stomach. Pre-empt "food goes to the stomach and digestion's done." Repeat: **"The stomach is just one stop — the real absorbing happens in the small intestine."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Take a big bite of an apple in your imagination — *crunch*. Now here's my question: where does that bite actually GO? Most people say 'to your tummy' and stop there. But that bite is about to go on a long, secret journey through your body — metres and metres of tubes — and it doesn't even finish in your stomach. Let's follow it the whole way."

**Show:** the apple and the body outline.  **Do:** mime biting; trace a vague path down your body with your hand to build curiosity.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From Year 3 — why do we even eat food? What does our body get from it?" → *listen for:* energy / goodness / nutrients to grow and be healthy.
*(If shaky: "What happens if you don't eat all day? Food gives your body fuel and the goodness it needs.")*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do — trace the apple)
> **Say:** "Let's lay the journey out along this body outline, step by step. I'll start the bite, you tell me where it goes next."

**Do:** place the **mouth card** at the top of the outline.
**Ask, building the path:**
- "First, the apple is in your **mouth**. What two things start breaking it down here?" → *listen for:* teeth (chewing) and saliva (spit). "Now you swallow."
- "It slides down a tube called the **oesophagus**. Does it just fall, or does the tube push it?" → *listen for:* the tube squeezes/pushes it down. (Demo with the stuffed tubing if you have it.)
- "Now it reaches the **stomach** — a stretchy bag. What does the stomach do to it?" → *listen for:* churns/mixes it with juices into mush.
- "Next, the long, twisty **small intestine**. This is where the **nutrients** — the goodness — pass into your **blood**." 
- "Finally the **large intestine** takes back water; what's left becomes **waste**." Lay each card in order as you go.

### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name and order it)
> **Say:** "That whole set of parts is your **digestive system**, and the order matters. **Mouth** (teeth and saliva start the job) → **oesophagus** (pushes food down) → **stomach** (churns it with juices) → **small intestine** (where most of the goodness, the **nutrients**, gets **absorbed** into the blood) → **large intestine** (takes back water; the rest becomes **waste**). Here's the part most people get wrong: the famous stomach is NOT where the goodness gets taken in. The real absorbing happens later, in the **small intestine**."

**Show / Do:** point to each labelled part on the diagram in order; tap the small intestine twice and say "this is the goodness-absorbing part."
**Emphasize:** "Digestion is a **whole journey** — and **most nutrients are absorbed in the small intestine**, not the stomach."

### ⏱ 17–23 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Where are **most nutrients absorbed** — in the stomach, or somewhere else?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "In the **small intestine**." Then probe: *"So what's the stomach's actual job, if it's not the absorbing part?"* (churning food into mush with juices).
- ❌ **If "the stomach":** the stomach is the famous part. *Why:* the child thinks the stomach finishes the job. → **fix:** "The stomach is a mixing bag — it churns food into mush. But the goodness gets taken into the blood AFTER the stomach. Which long twisty part comes next, and does the absorbing?"
- ❌ **If "the mouth":** confusing where food is broken up with where goodness is absorbed. *Why:* chewing feels like the main event. → **fix:** "The mouth and teeth break food into small bits — that's the start. But the bits still have to travel on for the goodness to be taken into the blood. Where does that happen?"

### ⏱ 23–30 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Shuffle the **ordering cards** (parts + jobs jumbled). The child puts the five parts in the **correct order along the body outline** and says the **job of each** as they place it. Then a fresh question: "Trace a bite of toast from mouth to waste, naming each stop." Interleave: "Which stop does the GOODNESS leave the food and enter your blood?"
**Watch for:** swapping the order of small and large intestine, or saying the stomach absorbs nutrients. Catch the latter: "Is the stomach the mixer or the absorber?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the order; 2) name *why* — "I think the tricky bit is which intestine does the absorbing"; 3) go concrete — point along the real body outline, top to bottom; 4) give ONE hint — "Follow the food the way gravity takes it: down from the mouth"; 5) re-ask a smaller version (just order mouth → stomach first), then add the intestines; 6) keep it safe — "This is a long journey to remember; let's rebuild it one stop at a time."

### ⏱ 30–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the body scientist: list the five parts in order, and tell me which one absorbs most of the nutrients." → *listen for:* mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine; small intestine absorbs.
**Process praise:** "You followed the food all the way and caught the tricky part — that the small intestine, not the stomach, does the absorbing. You reasoned it, you didn't just memorise it."
**Revisit later:** Use "name the five parts in order" as a retrieval starter before Lesson 5 and again at the end-of-unit check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** **order the parts** using the body outline with the diagram visible as a model; name the job of just mouth, stomach and small intestine.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain the **job of each part in turn** without the diagram; ask "why does the body bother having such a LONG small intestine?" (more space and time to absorb the goodness).

**Extension (if time allows):** Make a "journey of a chip" comic strip with one box per part, drawing what happens at each stop.
**Assessment evidence:** Child orders the five parts correctly and states where most nutrients are absorbed (small intestine), rejecting the "stomach finishes it" idea.

## Lesson 5 — Teeth for different jobs
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Animals including humans)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name the three kinds of teeth and their jobs and say how to keep teeth healthy."

**You will need:** a **mirror** (one each if possible); a **tooth diagram** (labelled incisors, canines, molars); **carnivore and herbivore skull pictures** (e.g. lion/dog vs cow/sheep); **tooth–job matching cards** (incisor→cut/bite, canine→tear, molar→grind); an apple or carrot (to demonstrate biting vs grinding).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 4 — digestion starts in the mouth, where teeth break food down.
**Key vocabulary:** **incisor** (flat, sharp front teeth that **cut/bite**); **canine** (pointy teeth that **tear**); **molar** (big flat back teeth that **grind**); **cut**, **tear**, **grind**; **decay** (teeth rotting); **carnivore** / **herbivore**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** We have **three kinds of teeth with three different jobs** — incisors cut, canines tear, molars grind — because **different shapes do different jobs**. Pre-empt "all teeth do the same job." Plus: keep teeth healthy by **brushing twice a day and eating less sugar**. Repeat: **"Different teeth, different shapes, different jobs."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Think about a dog — it's got those big pointy fangs at the front, hasn't it? Now think about a cow chewing grass all day — its teeth are flat, like grinding stones. Same mouths, totally different teeth. Why would a dog and a cow have such different teeth? The answer is hiding in YOUR mouth too — you've got more than one kind of tooth, and each does a different job."

**Show:** the carnivore and herbivore skull pictures side by side.  **Do:** bare your teeth and point to a pointy one and a flat back one.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From last lesson — where does digestion begin, and what's the first thing that happens to food?" → *listen for:* in the mouth; teeth break it down / chewing.
*(If shaky: "What do your teeth do when you eat an apple — all the same thing, or different things at the front and back?")*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do — mirror hunt)
> **Say:** "Grab your mirror. We're going on a tooth hunt in your own mouth. Smile big!"

**Do:** guide the child through their own teeth with the mirror.
**Ask:**
- "Look at your **front** teeth — flat and a bit sharp like little chisels. Bite the air with just those. What are they good for?" → *listen for:* biting/cutting a chunk off. "Those are **incisors**."
- "Now find the **pointy** ones just to the sides. What might a pointy tooth be good at — grabbing and pulling?" → *listen for:* tearing. "Those are **canines**."
- "Run your tongue to the **back** — big, flat, bumpy teeth. Pretend to chew toffee. What are they doing?" → *listen for:* grinding/crushing. "Those are **molars**."
**Ask:** "Bite a carrot with your front teeth, then move it to the back — which teeth crunch it up small?" → *listen for:* the back molars.

### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name the three)
> **Say:** "You've found all three kinds. At the **front**, the flat sharp **incisors** **cut and bite**. Just beside them, the pointy **canines** **tear** food (think of a dog's fangs ripping meat). At the **back**, the big flat **molars** **grind and crush**, like a cow chewing grass. Three shapes, three jobs. And to keep them all healthy and stop them rotting — that's called **decay** — you **brush twice a day** and eat and drink **less sugar**."

**Show / Do:** point to each tooth type on the diagram; link the lion's big canines to "tear meat" and the cow's flat molars to "grind grass."
**Emphasize:** "**Different teeth, different shapes, different jobs** — and brush twice a day, less sugar, to beat decay."

### ⏱ 16–21 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which teeth **grind** your food — and where are they?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "**Molars** — the big flat ones at the **back**." Then probe: *"Why are the grinding teeth flat and wide instead of pointy?"* (a flat surface crushes; a point would just poke).
- ❌ **If "all teeth grind / all teeth do the same job":** teeth look similar at a glance. *Why:* the child hasn't connected shape to job. → **fix:** "Could you grind toffee with a sharp pointy tooth? Try biting the air with your front teeth, then chewing with your back ones — do they feel like they do the same job? Which shape is built for grinding?"

Grinding means crushing it small. Which teeth are big, flat and wide enough to crush?"

> **Say:** "Be the dentist: name the three kinds of teeth, their jobs, and tell me two ways to keep teeth healthy." → *listen for:* incisors cut/bite, canines tear, molars grind; brush twice a day, less sugar.

## Lesson 6 — Solids, liquids and gases
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 3 (States of matter)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can sort materials into solid, liquid and gas and describe how each behaves."

**You will need:** a **solid** to handle (ice cube in a bag, a wooden block); a **liquid** in a clear container plus a differently-shaped empty one (water; milk); a **gas** you can feel — a **sealed balloon of air** (and optionally a clear bottle to "pour" air); **mixed example cards/objects** to sort (rock, juice, steam, brick, syrup, air, oil); three labelled sorting areas: **SOLID / LIQUID / GAS**.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Years 1–2 (materials) — names of materials; the everyday fact that ice, water and steam are all "water" in different forms.
**Key vocabulary:** **solid** (keeps its shape; particles packed tight), **liquid** (flows; takes the container's shape), **gas** (spreads to fill any space); **particle** (tiny piece everything is made of); **shape**; **flow**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** The three **states** behave differently: **solid keeps its shape**, **liquid flows and takes the container's shape**, **gas spreads to fill any space**. The clincher misconception is that **a gas is still "something"** (air has substance). Pre-empt "a gas is nothing / not really there." Repeat: **"Solid holds its shape, liquid takes the cup's shape, gas fills the whole room."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's a brain-bender. Ice… water… and steam from a kettle — they're ALL just water. The exact same stuff. So why does ice sit hard in your hand, water pours out of a cup, and steam float away and vanish? Same water, three totally different behaviours. By the end of today you'll know exactly why."

**Show:** the ice cube, a cup of water, the balloon of air.  **Do:** hold the ice (hard), tip the water (it pours and changes shape), squeeze the balloon (air pushes back).
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Ice, water and steam — are they the same stuff or different stuff?" → *listen for:* the same (water) in different forms.
*(If shaky: "If you left the ice on the table, what would it turn into? And if you boiled that water?")*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do — explore the three)
> **Say:** "Let's investigate each one with our hands and eyes. Don't name the groups yet — just tell me how each one BEHAVES."

**Do:** hand over each item.
**Ask:**
- (ice / wood) "Does this keep its shape when you hold it, or change shape?" → *listen for:* keeps its shape. "Can you pour it?" → *listen for:* no.
- (water) "Pour this from the tall cup into the wide bowl. Did its shape change?" → *listen for:* yes — it took the new container's shape; it flows.
- (balloon of air) "Squeeze the balloon. Is there *something* inside pushing back, or nothing?" → *listen for:* something — air pushes back. "If I let it go in the room, where would the air spread?" → *listen for:* everywhere / fill the space.

### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name the states)
> **Say:** "Everything is made of tiny **particles**, and how they're arranged makes three **states of matter**. In a **solid**, the particles are packed tightly together, locked in place — so a solid **keeps its shape** (ice, wood, a brick). In a **liquid**, the particles can slide past each other — so a liquid **flows** and **takes the shape of its container** (water, milk). In a **gas**, the particles zoom around freely and **spread out to fill any space** they're in (air, steam). And notice — even though you can't see the air, it's still REAL stuff; it pushed back when you squeezed the balloon."

**Show / Do:** clench your fist tight = solid particles; wiggle loose fingers = liquid; fling hands wide = gas spreading. Sort the demo items into the three labelled areas.
**Emphasize:** "**Solid holds its shape, liquid takes the cup's shape, gas fills the whole room** — and a gas is still something real."

### ⏱ 17–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "How is a **liquid different from a solid**? Tell me what each one does with its shape."
- ✅ **Expected:** "A liquid **flows and takes the shape of its container**; a solid **keeps its own shape**." Then probe: *"How could you PROVE water is a liquid using two different cups?"* (pour it; it changes shape).
- ❌ **If "they're the same / both just sit there":** the child hasn't tested behaviour. *Why:* both feel like ordinary "stuff." → **fix:** "Try it: tip the water into a different shaped bowl — what happened to its shape? Now try to 'tip' the ice into the bowl — does it pour and change shape? What's the difference?"
- ❌ **If "a gas is nothing, so there are only two real states":** air feels like emptiness. *Why:* you can't see a gas. → **fix:** "Squeeze the sealed balloon again — is there something pushing back on your hands? If it were nothing, the balloon would squash flat. Air is real — it's a gas that fills the space."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Give the **mixed example cards/objects** (rock, juice, steam, brick, syrup, air, oil). The child sorts each into **SOLID / LIQUID / GAS** and says how it behaves ("Syrup is a liquid — it flows slowly and takes the jar's shape"). Interleave a tricky one: "Is thick syrup a solid or a liquid? How do you know?" (liquid — it still flows and takes the container's shape, just slowly).
**Watch for:** putting thick liquids (syrup, honey) as solids, or leaving out the gas because "it's nothing." Catch it: "Does it flow and take a container's shape? Then which state?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the group; 2) name *why* — "the tricky one is thick liquids / the gas"; 3) go concrete — hand them the real water and the real ice to compare; 4) give ONE hint — "Ask: does it keep its OWN shape, take a CONTAINER'S shape, or fill the WHOLE space?"; 5) re-ask with just solid vs liquid first, then add gas; 6) keep it safe — "Three states is a lot to juggle; let's test one behaviour at a time."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the matter scientist: describe what a solid, a liquid and a gas each do with their shape." → *listen for:* solid keeps shape; liquid flows/takes container shape; gas fills any space.
**Process praise:** "You sorted by how each material BEHAVED — by pouring and squeezing to test it — instead of just guessing. That's exactly how scientists decide a state."
**Revisit later:** Bring "how is a liquid different from a solid?" back as a retrieval starter before Lesson 7 (changing state).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** sort **solid vs liquid** only at first (the clearest contrast), with real items to handle; add gas once secure.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain each state **using the word "particles"** (packed / sliding / zooming free); ask "is honey a solid or a liquid — and would that change if you cooled it right down?"

**Extension (if time allows):** Go on a "three states hunt" around the house — find one solid, one liquid and one gas, and write one behaviour sentence for each.
**Assessment evidence:** Child sorts mixed materials correctly into solid/liquid/gas and describes the shape behaviour of each, recognising a gas as real matter.

## Lesson 7 — Changing state: melting, freezing, evaporating, condensing (investigation)
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 3 (States of matter)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name the four changes of state and measure one with a timer."

**You will need:** **ice cubes** (at least 2 the same size); **2 saucers**; a **timer/stopwatch** (seconds); a **warm spot** and a **cool spot** (sunny windowsill vs fridge-side/shady shelf); a **results table** (Place | Time to melt in seconds); a cold glass/window for the condensation demo; a whiteboard for the four-changes diagram.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 6 — solid keeps shape, liquid flows, gas fills space; heating and cooling can change things.
**Key vocabulary:** **melt** (solid→liquid), **freeze** (liquid→solid), **evaporate** (liquid→gas), **condense** (gas→liquid); **water vapour** (water as an invisible gas); **fair test**; **measure** (in standard units — seconds).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Heating and cooling cause **four changes of state** — melt, freeze, evaporate, condense — and we can **measure** one (melting) accurately **in seconds** and record it in a **table** (working scientifically). Two big corrections: when a puddle dries, the water **evaporated** (didn't vanish); and condensation is **water vapour cooling**, not air turning into water. Repeat: **"Heating melts and evaporates; cooling condenses and freezes."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "After it rains, there are puddles everywhere. Come back a few hours later on a sunny day — gone! No one tipped them out, no one drank them. So… where did all that water actually GO? Did it just vanish into nothing? A scientist NEVER says 'it vanished.' Today we'll catch the water in the act of changing — and we'll time it with a real stopwatch."

**Show:** the two ice cubes, two saucers, the timer.  **Do:** hold up the stopwatch like a race official.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From last lesson — name the three states, and what happens to ice if you warm it?" → *listen for:* solid/liquid/gas; ice melts into water.
*(If shaky: "What's ice made of? What does it turn into in your hand? That change has a name we'll learn today.")*

### ⏱ 4–9 min · EXPLAIN + set up the fair test (I do)
> **Say:** "Here's our investigation. I have two ice cubes the SAME size. I'll put one on a saucer in a **warm** spot and one in a **cool** spot, and we'll **time how long each takes to melt completely, in seconds**. To make it a **fair test**, what must stay the same about the two ice cubes?"

**Show / Do:** place the cubes, start a timer (note the time), write the table headings: **Place | Time to melt (seconds)**.
**Ask:** "What's the ONE thing we're changing between them?" → *listen for:* the place (warm vs cool / how warm it is). "And what stays the same?" → *listen for:* same size cube, same saucer, started at the same time.
**Emphasize:** "Change ONE thing (the warmth), keep the rest the same, and **measure in seconds** — that's a fair, measured test."

### ⏱ 9–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do — name the four changes while we wait)
> **Say:** "While the ice melts, let's name ALL four changes of state, because melting is just one of them."

**Show / Do:** build a four-arrow diagram on the board (SOLID ⇄ LIQUID ⇄ GAS):
- "**Melt** — solid turns to liquid. That's what our ice is doing right now."
- "**Freeze** — liquid turns to solid. The opposite. How would we freeze water?" → *listen for:* cool it / put it in the freezer.
- "**Evaporate** — liquid turns to gas. THIS is what happened to your puddle — it turned into invisible **water vapour** in the air. It didn't vanish."
- "**Condense** — gas turns back to liquid." (Breathe on the cold glass.) "See the droplets? That's water vapour from my breath **cooling and condensing** — NOT air turning into water."
**Ask:** "Which two changes need **heating**, and which two need **cooling**?" → *listen for:* heating = melt + evaporate; cooling = freeze + condense.

### ⏱ 16–21 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What is it called when a **liquid turns into a gas** — like your puddle drying up?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "**Evaporation** (it evaporated)." Then probe: *"So did the puddle water get destroyed, or where is it now?"* (it's in the air as water vapour).
- ❌ **If "the water just vanished / disappeared / was destroyed":** you can't see where it went. *Why:* invisible water vapour looks like nothing. → **fix:** "Scientists never let water vanish — it has to go somewhere. It turned into an invisible gas called **water vapour** floating in the air. What do we call a liquid turning into a gas?"
- ❌ **If "condensation" (mixed up with the cold-glass droplets):** confusing the two opposite changes. *Why:* both involve gas and liquid. → **fix:** "Careful — condensing is gas turning BACK into liquid (the droplets on the cold glass). The puddle going the OTHER way — liquid turning INTO gas — is the one we want. What's that called?"

### ⏱ 21–28 min · PRACTICE (you do — record the data)
**Do:** Check the ice cubes. When each melts, the child **reads the timer and records the time in seconds in the table**. (If timing isn't finished, record the running times and the clear trend.) Then they **compare**: "Which melted faster, and WHY — using a change-of-state word?" Interleave: label each of these as melt/freeze/evaporate/condense — wet washing drying on a line; ice forming on a pond; steam fogging a mirror.
**Watch for:** sloppy reading of the timer; saying "the warm one melted because it's warm" without the word *melt*; mixing evaporate and condense. Catch it: "Read the timer to the nearest second — what does the table show?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "let's match each picture to one of our four arrows"; 3) go concrete — point to the board diagram and our real melting ice; 4) give ONE hint — "Ask: is it going solid→liquid, liquid→gas, gas→liquid, or liquid→solid?"; 5) re-ask with just melt vs freeze first, then add evaporate/condense; 6) keep it safe — "Four changes is a lot; let's sort them two at a time."

### ⏱ 28–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the scientist: name the four changes of state, and tell me from your TABLE which ice cube melted faster and why." → *listen for:* melt, freeze, evaporate, condense; the warm-spot cube melted faster (more heat melts it quicker), shown by a smaller number of seconds.
**Process praise:** "You measured in real seconds and wrote it in a table, so anyone can SEE which melted faster — that's working like a proper scientist. And you used 'evaporate' instead of 'vanished.'"
**Revisit later:** Bring the table back at the start of Lesson 8 — "evaporation and condensation are the two changes that drive the whole water cycle."

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** **read and report one** melt time from the timer and place it in the table; name just melt and freeze.
- **Stretch (challenge):** **compare both times** and explain the difference using "more heat → melts faster," then predict how many seconds a cube in the freezer would take (it wouldn't melt — it would stay/freeze); ask "would the result always be the same on a cold day?"

**Extension (if time allows):** Set up a "puddle watch" or a saucer of water by a sunny window and predict how long it takes to fully evaporate; draw the four-change diagram from memory.
**Assessment evidence:** Child names the four changes of state, **measures a melt time in seconds and records it in the table**, and explains which melted faster using the word *melt* (and rejects "the water vanished").

## Lesson 8 — The water cycle
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 3 (States of matter)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can order the water cycle and explain what drives it."

**You will need:** a **water-cycle diagram** (Sun, sea, evaporation, clouds, rain, rivers back to sea); **ordering cards** for the four stages (evaporate → condense into clouds → precipitation/rain → flow back to sea); optional: a **"water cycle in a bag"** — a zip bag with a little water and blue marker, taped to a sunny window the day before so droplets have formed.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 7 — evaporate (liquid→gas) and condense (gas→liquid); heat causes evaporation.
**Key vocabulary:** **water cycle** (water moving round and round between sea, sky and land); **evaporate**, **condense**, **water vapour**, **cloud**, **precipitation** (rain, snow, hail); **the Sun** (the energy that drives it).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** The water cycle is the **same water recycled forever**, driven by **heat from the Sun**: Sun **evaporates** sea water → vapour rises and **condenses** into clouds → falls as **precipitation (rain)** → flows back to the sea. The key idea: it's a **cycle with no beginning or end**, and **the Sun is the engine**. Repeat: **"The Sun lifts the water up by evaporation; it falls back as rain — round and round forever."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's something amazing and a bit weird: the rain that falls today might have been a drink in a DINOSAUR'S puddle millions of years ago. The Earth never makes new water — it just uses the same water over and over again. How on earth does water get from the sea all the way up into a rain cloud and back? Let's follow it round."

**Show:** the (optional) water-cycle-in-a-bag with its droplets, and the diagram.  **Do:** point to the droplets that formed on the bag overnight.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From last lesson — what does it mean to **evaporate**, and what makes water evaporate?" → *listen for:* liquid turns to gas (water vapour); heat (from the Sun).
*(If shaky: "Where did your puddle go? It turned into invisible gas in the air — that's evaporation, and that's where the water cycle begins.")*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do — follow the droplet)
> **Say:** "Let's BE a droplet of water in the sea and travel the whole loop. I'll set the scene; you tell me what change happens at each step."

**Show / Do:** trace the diagram with your finger as you go.
**Ask:**
- "The hot **Sun** shines on the sea. What change of state happens to the surface water?" → *listen for:* it evaporates (turns to vapour / gas) and rises.
- "The vapour rises high where the air is **cold**. What change happens now?" → *listen for:* it condenses into tiny droplets — clouds.
- "The cloud gets heavy with droplets. What falls out?" → *listen for:* rain (precipitation) — or snow/hail.
- "The rain lands on hills and rivers. Where does it flow?" → *listen for:* back down to the sea — and the whole thing starts again.

### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name and order the cycle)
> **Say:** "That whole loop is the **water cycle**, and it runs on **heat from the Sun**. Stage one: the Sun **evaporates** water from seas and rivers into **water vapour** that rises. Stage two: high up where it's cold, the vapour **condenses** into clouds. Stage three: the droplets join up, get heavy and fall as **precipitation** — rain, snow or hail. Stage four: that water flows back to the sea — and the Sun starts it all again. There's no real start or finish; it's a **cycle**, going round forever, and the **Sun is the engine** that lifts the water up."

**Show / Do:** lay the four ordering cards in a circle, not a line, to show there's no end; tap the Sun and say "engine."
**Emphasize:** "**The Sun drives it** — evaporation lifts water up, precipitation brings it down — round and round, the same water forever."

### ⏱ 16–21 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "In the water cycle, what **makes water evaporate** from the sea in the first place?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "**Heat from the Sun**." Then probe: *"So what would happen to the whole cycle if the Sun stopped shining?"* (no evaporation, no clouds, no rain — the cycle would stop).
- ❌ **If "the wind / the clouds make it evaporate":** confusing what moves water with what lifts it. *Why:* wind and clouds are visible parts of weather. → **fix:** "Wind can blow clouds around, but what gives the sea water the ENERGY to turn into gas and rise? Think back to your puddle — what dried it up on a sunny day?"
- ❌ **If "the rain makes it evaporate / mixing up the order":** the child loops the stages wrongly. *Why:* the cycle has no obvious start. → **fix:** "Rain is water coming DOWN. Evaporation is water going UP. Which comes from the SEA being heated? Put the four cards in a circle and start with the Sun on the sea."

### ⏱ 21–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Shuffle the **four ordering cards**. The child puts them in **correct cycle order** and, for stretch, **names the change of state at each step** (evaporate / condense / precipitation / flow). Then connect to real life: "It's a hot sunny day over the sea, then grey clouds build, then it rains on the hills. Match each part of that day to a stage of the cycle."
**Watch for:** ordering it as a straight line with a "start," or mixing evaporate and condense. Catch it: "Does this stage go UP (evaporate) or come DOWN (rain)?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the order; 2) name *why* — "let's decide which way the water is moving at each card"; 3) go concrete — point to the diagram and the bag's droplets; 4) give ONE hint — "Start with the Sun heating the sea — what happens FIRST?"; 5) re-ask with just two stages (evaporate then condense), then add rain and flow; 6) keep it safe — "A cycle is tricky because it has no start; let's build it from the Sun outward."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the weather scientist: tell me the water cycle in order, and what drives the whole thing." → *listen for:* evaporate → condense into clouds → rain/precipitation → flow back to sea; driven by heat from the Sun.
**Process praise:** "You connected today's cycle straight back to evaporation and condensation from last lesson — you saw the water cycle is just our four changes of state happening on a giant scale. Brilliant joining-up."
**Revisit later:** Use "what drives the water cycle?" as a retrieval starter at the end-of-unit check, and link back when weather comes up.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** **order four cycle cards** with the diagram visible as a model; name just evaporation and rain.
- **Stretch (challenge):** **name the change of state at each step** and explain why clouds form high up (the air is colder there, so vapour condenses); ask "why does the same water never run out?"

**Extension (if time allows):** Draw and label your own water cycle from memory, including the Sun as the engine; check the "cycle in a bag" and explain the droplets using evaporate + condense.
**Assessment evidence:** Child orders the water cycle correctly and states that **heat from the Sun** drives it, linking at least two stages to changes of state (evaporation, condensation).

---

**End-of-unit 3 check:** **Sort materials** into solid/liquid/gas; **name the four changes of state** and **time a melt** (in seconds, recorded in a table); **order the water cycle** and say what drives it.

## Lesson 9 — Sound is made by vibrations
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 4 (Sound)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that sounds are made by vibrations that travel to our ears."

**You will need:** a **rubber band** (to pluck); a **drum or empty box** with something to tap it; a few grains of rice or salt (to scatter on the drum and watch jump); **two paper/plastic cups + string** for a string telephone (string threaded through and knotted); a picture of an ear (optional); space and quiet.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Everyday — we hear with our ears; we make and hear lots of sounds.
**Key vocabulary:** **vibration / vibrate** (shaking quickly back and forth); **sound wave** (vibrations travelling along); **medium** (the stuff sound travels through — air, water, solid); **ear**; **vacuum** (empty space with nothing in it).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **Every sound is made by something vibrating**, and the vibrations travel through a **medium** (air, water or solid) to your **ear**. The clincher: sound **cannot travel through empty space** because there's nothing there to vibrate. Pre-empt "sound travels through empty space (like in space movies)." Repeat: **"No vibration, no sound — and no medium to carry it, no sound either."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Put your hand gently on the front of your throat, like this, and hum a long 'mmmmmm.' Don't stop — what can you FEEL under your fingers? That tickly buzzing is the secret of every single sound in the universe. Your voice box is doing it right now. Let's find out what it is."

**Show:** your hand on your own throat, humming.  **Do:** hum and let the child feel their own throat buzz, then stop ("Feel it stop the instant I stop humming?").
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What part of your body do you use to HEAR a sound?" → *listen for:* ears.
*(If shaky: "Cover your ears tight — can you still hear me well? Your ears catch the sound.")*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXAMPLE (we do — feel the vibration)
> **Say:** "Let's hunt for the buzzing — the **vibration** — in lots of sounds. Every time you HEAR a sound, something is shaking."

**Do, one at a time:**
- **Rubber band:** stretch and pluck it. "Watch it — can you SEE it shaking? Touch it gently while it sounds." → *listen for:* yes, it's vibrating; you can feel it tickle.
- **Drum/box + rice:** scatter rice on top, then tap the drum. "Watch the rice — what's it doing, and why?" → *listen for:* the rice jumps because the drum skin is vibrating.
- **Throat again:** "Hum — feel the vibration. Now whisper with no voice — less buzzing, quieter sound."
**Ask:** "What did EVERY one of these have in common when it made a sound?" → *listen for:* something was vibrating/shaking.

### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name it, and the medium)
> **Say:** "Here's the rule for ALL sound: a sound is made by something **vibrating** — shaking quickly back and forth. The guitar string, the drum skin, your voice box. Those vibrations push on the **air** around them, and the shaking travels along as a **sound wave** until it reaches your **ear**, which turns it into the sound you hear. But sound needs something to travel THROUGH — we call that a **medium**: air, water, or even a solid like string or a wall. And here's the big one: in empty space — a **vacuum** with NOTHING in it — there's nothing to vibrate, so sound **cannot** travel. Space is silent, even though films make it loud."

**Show / Do:** demonstrate the **string telephone** — speak into one cup while the child listens at the other with the string pulled taut. "The sound is travelling along the SOLID string!"
**Emphasize:** "**No vibration, no sound.** And **no medium, no sound** — that's why space is silent."

### ⏱ 17–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Could you **hear an explosion in outer space**? Why or why not?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "**No** — space is empty (a vacuum), there's no air or medium to carry the vibrations." Then probe: *"So what would you NEED in space for sound to travel?"* (some medium — air, water or a solid).
- ❌ **If "yes, you'd hear it (like in films)":** movies play loud sound in space. *Why:* films add sound for excitement. → **fix:** "What does a sound need to travel through — what's the *medium*? Is there any air out in empty space? If there's nothing to vibrate, can the sound get to your ear?"
- ❌ **If "no, because there's no air to breathe":** right answer, muddled reason. *Why:* mixing up needing air to breathe with needing air to carry sound. → **fix:** "Good that it's 'no' — but the reason isn't breathing. Sound is a *vibration travelling through stuff*. With no air or any medium out there, the vibration has nothing to travel through."

### ⏱ 22–27 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** For each of these, the child names **what is vibrating** and **what medium carries the sound** to the ear: a humming throat; a plucked guitar; the tutor calling from across the room; tapping on the bottom of a swimming pool (sound through water); the string telephone. Then explain: "WHY does the string telephone work? Use the words *vibrate*, *medium* and *ear*."
**Watch for:** naming the ear as "what makes the sound" (it receives, doesn't make), or forgetting the medium. Catch it: "What's actually SHAKING to make that sound? And what does the shaking travel through?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "let's find the thing that's vibrating first, then the medium"; 3) go concrete — touch the buzzing rubber band / throat again; 4) give ONE hint — "Something has to SHAKE to make the sound, and the shaking has to travel through SOMETHING"; 5) re-ask just "what's vibrating?" before adding the medium; 6) keep it safe — "Two parts to remember; let's get the vibration first."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the sound scientist: how is EVERY sound made, and why is space silent?" → *listen for:* by something vibrating; space has no air/medium to carry the vibrations.
**Process praise:** "You proved sound is vibration with your OWN throat and the jumping rice — you felt and saw the science, then explained why space is silent. That's evidence-based thinking."
**Revisit later:** Use "what makes a sound?" as a retrieval starter before Lesson 10 (pitch and volume are both about the vibration).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** name **what is vibrating** in two clear examples (rubber band, drum) with the object in hand.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain **why the string telephone works** using vibrate/medium/ear, and predict what happens if the string goes slack (it stops carrying the vibrations); ask "would sound travel faster through air or through a solid wall?" (it travels through solids well — invite a test).

**Extension (if time allows):** Build the string telephone properly and test it taut vs slack, around a corner, with a longer string — and explain each result with "vibration through a medium."
**Assessment evidence:** Child explains sound is made by vibrations that travel through a medium to the ear, and correctly says there is no sound in space because there is no medium.

## Lesson 10 — Pitch and volume (investigation)
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 4 (Sound)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can change a sound's pitch and volume and explain the difference."

**You will need:** an open **box** (e.g. a shoebox or empty tissue box) with **rubber bands of different thicknesses/tensions** stretched over the opening (a "rubber-band guitar"); a **results sheet** (What I changed | What happened to the sound); space to step back from the source for the distance test.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 9 — sound is made by something vibrating that travels to the ear.
**Key vocabulary:** **pitch** (how **high or low** a sound is — faster vibration = higher pitch); **volume / loudness** (how **loud or quiet** — bigger vibration = louder); **distance** (sound gets quieter further away); changing **one thing at a time** (fair test).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **Pitch and volume are different things.** **Pitch** = high/low, set by the **speed/size** of the band (tighter/thinner = faster vibration = higher); **volume** = loud/quiet, set by **how hard you pluck** (bigger vibration = louder). And sound gets **quieter with distance**. Pre-empt "louder and higher mean the same thing." Repeat: **"Pluck HARDER for LOUDER (volume); TIGHTEN for HIGHER (pitch)."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Imagine a guitar. Pull a string really tight and pluck it — it sings a high, squeaky note. Leave a string loose and floppy and pluck it — it growls a deep, low note. Same string, totally different note. Why does tightness change the note? And is a 'high' note the same as a 'loud' note — or are those two completely different things? Let's build a guitar and find out."

**Show:** the rubber-band guitar box.  **Do:** pluck a tight band (high) then a loose band (low) so the child hears the difference straight away.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From last time — what makes ANY sound, including this band?" → *listen for:* something vibrating.
*(If shaky: "Pluck the band and watch — is it shaking? That shaking IS the sound.")*

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXAMPLE (we do — change ONE thing)
> **Say:** "We're going to be sound investigators. The golden rule: change just **ONE thing at a time** so we know what caused the change. First, let's change **how HARD** we pluck."

**Do:** keep the same band. Pluck it gently, then hard.
**Ask:** "Pluck it gently… now hard. What changed about the SOUND? And what did the band's shaking look like — small or big?" → *listen for:* harder = louder; the band moved more (bigger vibration). "That loudness is called **volume**."
> **Say:** "Now change a DIFFERENT thing — the **tightness**. Pluck a loose band, then a tight one (or pull a band tighter)."
**Ask:** "Loose band vs tight band — what changed about the note? Higher or lower?" → *listen for:* tighter = higher note; it vibrated faster. "That high-or-low is called **pitch**."

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name pitch vs volume)
> **Say:** "Two completely different things describe a sound. **Volume** is how **loud or quiet** it is — and it depends on how **big** the vibration is. Pluck harder → bigger shake → **louder**. **Pitch** is how **high or low** it is — and it depends on how **fast** the band vibrates. A tighter or thinner band vibrates faster → **higher** pitch. So loud is NOT the same as high! You can have a loud LOW note (a big drum) and a quiet HIGH note (a tiny squeak). And one more: as you move **further away** from a sound, it gets **quieter** — the vibrations spread out and fade."

**Show / Do:** big arm-shake = big vibration = LOUD; fast little shakes = fast vibration = HIGH. Pluck a loud-low and a quiet-high to prove they're independent.
**Emphasize:** "**Pluck HARDER for LOUDER (volume); TIGHTEN for HIGHER (pitch).** Loud and high are NOT the same."

### ⏱ 16–21 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "If the vibrations get **faster**, what changes — the **pitch** or the **volume**?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "The **pitch** — it gets **higher**." Then probe: *"And what would make it LOUDER instead?"* (a bigger vibration — plucking harder).
- ❌ **If "the volume / it gets louder":** the child treats all "more vibration" as louder. *Why:* "more" feels like "louder." → **fix:** "Careful — there are two things: how BIG the shake is, and how FAST it shakes. *Bigger* shake = louder. *Faster* shake = higher. Faster changes which one — loud, or high?"
- ❌ **If "both / they're the same":** the louder=higher misconception. *Why:* both seem like "more sound." → **fix:** "Let me play a LOUD LOW note (boom) and a QUIET HIGH note (eee). If loud and high were the same, that couldn't happen — but it just did. So are pitch and volume the same thing?"

### ⏱ 21–29 min · PRACTICE (you do — investigate and record)
**Do:** The child runs the investigation, changing **one thing at a time** and recording on the results sheet (**What I changed | What happened**): (1) pluck harder vs softer → volume; (2) tighter vs looser band → pitch; (3) step back several metres while you pluck the same → quieter with distance. They must write a sentence for each: "When I tightened the band, the pitch got higher."
**Watch for:** changing two things at once (e.g. a tighter AND harder pluck) so the cause is unclear; writing "louder" when they mean "higher." Catch it: "You changed two things — which one caused the change? Change just ONE next time."
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "I think we changed two things at once, so let's isolate one"; 3) go concrete — hold the band's tension fixed and ONLY vary the pluck strength; 4) give ONE hint — "Big shake or small shake = volume; fast shake or slow shake = pitch"; 5) re-ask just the volume test alone, then the pitch test alone; 6) keep it safe — "Mixing up two changes is the commonest mistake scientists make too — let's change one at a time."

### ⏱ 29–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the sound scientist: tell me the difference between pitch and volume, and how you'd make this band play a HIGH note and then a LOUD note." → *listen for:* pitch = high/low (faster/tighter), volume = loud/quiet (bigger pluck); tighten for high, pluck hard for loud.
**Process praise:** "You changed ONLY one thing each time and wrote down what happened — that's a proper fair test, and it's the only reason you could tell pitch and volume apart so clearly."
**Revisit later:** Use "faster vibration changes which — pitch or volume?" as a retrieval starter at the end-of-unit check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** make a **louder vs quieter** sound only (volume), describing each, before tackling pitch.
- **Stretch (challenge):** change **pitch and volume separately** and explain each in terms of vibration speed vs size; produce a **loud LOW** note and a **quiet HIGH** note on purpose to prove they're independent; ask "would a thicker band always sound lower?"

**Extension (if time allows):** Tune the bands to compose a short tune by tightening and plucking; record on the sheet which band/tension gave which note.
**Assessment evidence:** Child changes pitch and volume independently, records what changed and what happened, and explains pitch (faster vibration = higher) vs volume (bigger vibration = louder) as different things.

---

**End-of-unit 4 check:** Explain that sound is **made by vibrations needing a medium**; say **why there's no sound in space**; **change and explain pitch vs volume**.

## Lesson 11 — Simple circuits and switches (investigation)
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 5 (Electricity)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can build a circuit that lights a bulb and explain why a gap stops it."

**You will need:** **cells (batteries)** in a holder; **insulated wires with clips/ends**; a **bulb in a holder**; **paperclips** (for a switch); optional **buzzer or motor**; a circuit diagram on the board. **SAFETY: cells/batteries ONLY — never mains plugs or sockets.**
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Everyday — has seen batteries, bulbs, torches and switches; knows mains electricity is dangerous.
**Key vocabulary:** **circuit** (the path electricity flows around); **complete loop** (the path is joined all the way round); **cell / battery** (pushes the electricity); **wire**, **component** (bulb/buzzer/motor — the part that does a job); **switch** (a controllable gap); **gap / broken** (the loop is open, so nothing flows).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** A circuit only works when it's a **complete loop** — out from the cell, through the bulb, and **back to the cell**. A **gap** (or an open switch) **breaks** the loop and stops everything. The clincher misconception: **one wire to the bulb is NOT enough — it must be a complete loop back to the cell**. **Safety: batteries only.** Repeat: **"Electricity needs a complete loop, all the way back to the cell."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "You know those strings of fairy lights? Sometimes ONE little bulb breaks and the WHOLE string goes dark — not just that one bulb, all of them. That used to drive people mad. Why would one tiny gap turn off every light? The answer is the most important idea in all of electricity, and you're about to build it with your own hands. First — our golden safety rule: we ONLY ever use these little batteries, called cells. **Never, ever mains plugs or sockets** — those are dangerous. Deal?"

**Show:** the cell, wires, bulb laid out.  **Do:** hold up a battery and a bulb; make the safety rule a clear handshake moment.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Where have you seen a battery, a bulb and a switch all working together?" → *listen for:* a torch / fairy lights / a toy.
*(If shaky: "Think of a torch — what's inside? A battery to power it, a bulb to glow, a switch to turn it on.")*

### ⏱ 4–10 min · EXAMPLE (we do — build the loop)
> **Say:** "Let's build a circuit together. I'll give you the parts; you connect them so the bulb lights. Take your time."

**Do:** hand over cell, two wires, bulb in holder. Guide: cell → wire → bulb → wire → back to cell.
**Ask, as they build:** "Connect ONE wire from the cell to the bulb. Is it lighting?" → *listen for:* no, not yet. "Now connect the OTHER side of the bulb back to the cell. NOW?" → *listen for:* yes — it lights! "What did you have to do before it worked — how many connections to the cell?" → *listen for:* both ends connected; a full loop back to the cell.

### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXPLAIN (I do — name the complete loop)
> **Say:** "You built a **circuit** — the path electricity flows around. The most important rule: it only works when it's a **complete loop**. The **cell** (the battery) pushes the electricity out, it travels along a **wire**, through the **component** — your **bulb** — and then it MUST travel all the way **back to the cell**. Out AND back. If electricity could only reach the bulb from one wire and stop there, nothing would happen — it has to flow round and round the whole loop. Break the loop anywhere with a **gap**, and everything stops. That's exactly why one broken fairy light kills the whole string: it makes a gap, and the loop is broken."

**Show / Do:** trace the loop with your finger, out and back, saying "cell… wire… bulb… wire… back to cell." Then pull one wire loose. "Gap! Loop broken — bulb out."
**Emphasize:** "**Electricity needs a complete loop, all the way back to the cell** — not just one wire to the bulb."

### ⏱ 16–21 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Why **won't a bulb light if there's a gap** in the circuit?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "The **loop is broken**, so the electricity **can't flow all the way round** back to the cell." Then probe: *"Where does the electricity have to get back to?"* (the cell).
- ❌ **If "electricity only needs to reach the bulb from one wire":** the child imagines a single wire feeding the bulb. *Why:* it looks like the bulb just needs "feeding." → **fix:** "Try it — connect just ONE wire from cell to bulb. Does it light? No. Now add the wire from the bulb's other side BACK to the cell. Why did THAT make it work? Because the electricity needs a way back."
- ❌ **If "the battery ran out / the bulb is broken":** blaming the parts, not the gap. *Why:* a dark bulb feels like a fault. → **fix:** "The battery's fine and the bulb's fine — watch: when I close the gap, it lights again instantly. So it wasn't broken — it was the GAP. What does a gap do to the loop?"

### ⏱ 21–29 min · PRACTICE (you do — add a switch)
**Do:** Have the child **snip a gap** in their working circuit (disconnect one point) and bridge it with a **paperclip switch**: touching the paperclip across the gap = **closed** = bulb ON; lifting it = **open** = bulb OFF. They make it switch on and off five times and explain each. Stretch: swap the bulb for a **buzzer or motor** and predict before trying. **Reminder: cells only.**
**Watch for:** a loose connection mistaken for a "broken" switch; thinking the switch creates electricity rather than closing the loop. Catch it: "Is your switch CLOSING the loop or just touching one wire?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal the fix; 2) name *why* — "let's check the loop is complete all the way round"; 3) go concrete — trace the path with a finger from the cell, out and back; 4) give ONE hint — "Follow the electricity from the cell — can it get ALL the way back, or is there a gap?"; 5) re-ask the smaller version (rebuild the basic lit loop first), then add the switch; 6) keep it safe — "Loose wires fool everyone — let's check each join in turn."

### ⏱ 29–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the electrician: what does a circuit need to make the bulb light, and what does a switch actually DO?" → *listen for:* a complete loop from the cell through the bulb and back; a switch opens (gap = off) and closes (loop complete = on) the loop.
**Process praise:** "You tested it instead of guessing — one wire didn't work, then you completed the loop and it lit. And you used a paperclip to make a real switch. That's hands-on circuit thinking."
**Revisit later:** Use "why does a gap stop the bulb?" as a retrieval starter before Lesson 12 (where we deliberately put materials in the gap).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** **complete a part-built loop** (you've connected most of it; the child adds the last wire) to light the bulb, then describe the loop.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add a **working switch** and explain it as a "controllable gap"; add a buzzer in the loop and predict; ask "would adding a SECOND bulb make each one brighter or dimmer?" (dimmer — same cell pushing through more).

**Extension (if time allows):** Design a circuit on paper for a torch or a doorbell, marking where the switch goes and tracing the complete loop with an arrow.
**Assessment evidence:** Child builds a complete circuit that lights a bulb, explains a gap breaks the loop so electricity can't flow, demonstrates a switch opening/closing the loop, and states the cells-only safety rule.

## Lesson 12 — Conductors and insulators (investigation)
**Science · Year 4 · Unit 5 (Electricity)** | **Duration:** 30 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can sort materials into conductors and insulators by testing them."

**You will need:** a **battery-and-bulb circuit with a gap** left in it (two free wire ends to bridge); a set of **test objects** with predictions in mind: **coin, metal paperclip, metal spoon, plastic spoon, wooden peg/lolly stick, rubber band, pencil "lead" (graphite), glass marble**; a **results/sorting sheet** (Object | I predict | Result: bulb lit? | Conductor or insulator); two sorting mats: **CONDUCTOR / INSULATOR**. **SAFETY: cells/batteries ONLY — never mains.**
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** From Lesson 11 — a circuit lights only when the loop is complete; a gap stops it.
**Key vocabulary:** **conductor** (lets electricity flow — bulb lights — metals); **insulator** (stops electricity — bulb stays off — plastic, wood, rubber, glass); **predict**; **test**; **fair test** (same circuit, only the object changes).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Some materials **conduct** (metals — bulb lights) and some **insulate** (plastic, wood, rubber — bulb stays off). We find out by **predicting then testing** — bridging the gap with each object and watching the bulb. Connect to real life: wires are **metal inside (conductor) with a plastic coat (insulator)** to keep us safe. Working-scientifically: **record predictions and results in a table.** Repeat: **"Metals conduct and light the bulb; plastic, wood and rubber are insulators."**

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Have a careful look at an electrical wire. Inside, it's shiny **metal**. Outside, it's wrapped in **plastic**. Why two different materials? Why not just make the whole thing out of plastic, or the whole thing out of metal? There's a brilliant reason, and you're going to discover it yourself by testing what lets electricity through and what doesn't. Same safety rule as last time — **batteries only, never mains.**"

**Show:** the circuit-with-a-gap and the tray of test objects.  **Do:** point to the metal core and plastic coat of a real wire if you have one.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From last lesson — what does the bulb need to light, and what stops it?" → *listen for:* a complete loop; a gap breaks it.
*(If shaky: "If I leave a gap in the circuit, does the bulb light? What if I fill the gap with something — would it depend on WHAT I fill it with?")*

### ⏱ 4–9 min · EXPLAIN + set up (I do — predict first)
> **Say:** "Here's the clever test. I've left a **gap** in this circuit, so the bulb is OFF. If I bridge the gap with a material that lets electricity flow, the loop is complete again and the bulb lights. If the material blocks electricity, the bulb stays dark. Before we test anything, scientists **predict**. To keep it a **fair test**, the ONLY thing we change is the object in the gap — same battery, same bulb, same wires."

**Show / Do:** hold the coin across the gap (don't reveal the result yet); set up the results sheet columns: **Object | I predict | Bulb lit? | Conductor / Insulator.**
**Ask:** "Predict: will this **metal coin** let the bulb light — yes or no? And this **plastic spoon**?" → *listen for:* predictions (metal yes, plastic no, or their genuine guess). "Write your predictions down BEFORE we test."

### ⏱ 9–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do — test a few together)
> **Say:** "Now let's TEST and see if the world agrees with our predictions. Bridge the gap and watch the bulb."

**Do:** test 3–4 together, recording each result.
**Ask:**
- (paperclip / coin / spoon — metal) "Bulb lit? So does metal let electricity flow?" → *listen for:* yes — metals conduct.
- (plastic spoon / wooden peg / rubber band) "Bulb lit? So does plastic/wood/rubber let it flow?" → *listen for:* no — they block it.
- "What's the pattern — which kind of material always lights the bulb?" → *listen for:* metals.

### ⏱ 15–20 min · EXPLAIN→name + CHECK
> **Say:** "Materials that LET electricity flow are called **conductors** — and they're nearly all **metals** (that's why wires are metal inside). Materials that STOP electricity are **insulators** — plastic, wood, rubber, glass. Now you see why a wire is metal inside (a conductor, to carry the electricity) AND plastic outside (an insulator, so it can't shock us). Clever design!"

**Check — Ask:** "Is **plastic** a conductor or an insulator?"
- ✅ **Expected:** "An **insulator** — it doesn't let electricity flow (the bulb stayed off)." Then probe: *"So why is the OUTSIDE of a wire made of plastic?"* (to stop electricity reaching us — to keep us safe).
- ❌ **If "a conductor":** unsure which materials conduct. *Why:* the child guesses or generalises. → **fix:** "Let's test it again — put the plastic spoon in the gap. Did the bulb light? If it stayed off, electricity didn't get through. What do we call a material that BLOCKS it?"
- ❌ **If "it depends / all materials conduct a bit":** overgeneralising from metals. *Why:* thinks everything carries electricity. → **fix:** "Test wood, rubber and plastic in turn — did ANY of them light the bulb? They all stayed dark. So those are insulators; only the metals lit it."

### ⏱ 20–27 min · PRACTICE (you do — test and sort the rest)
**Do:** The child tests the **remaining objects**, recording **prediction → result → conductor/insulator** for each, then places each on the **CONDUCTOR or INSULATOR mat**. Include the surprise of **pencil graphite** (conducts — it's a special non-metal that does). Then explain in their own words why electrical wires have a plastic coat.
**Watch for:** sorting by prediction without testing; a metal object not lighting because of a loose contact (not because it's an insulator). Catch the latter: "Press it firmly across BOTH wire ends — now does it light?"
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — "let's make sure the object is touching BOTH wire ends properly"; 3) go concrete — re-test a known conductor (paperclip) to confirm the circuit works; 4) give ONE hint — "Did the bulb light? Lit = conductor; dark = insulator"; 5) re-ask with one clear metal and one clear plastic, then the rest; 6) keep it safe — "Loose contacts trick everyone — let's test a paperclip first to check our circuit."

### ⏱ 27–30 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Be the materials scientist: what's a conductor, what's an insulator, and why is a wire metal inside but plastic outside?" → *listen for:* conductor lets electricity flow (metals); insulator blocks it (plastic/wood/rubber); metal inside to carry it, plastic outside to keep us safe.
**Process praise:** "You PREDICTED first, then tested, and wrote both down — so you could see exactly where the world surprised you (that pencil lead!). Recording predictions and results in a table is real working-scientifically."
**Revisit later:** Use "conductor or insulator?" with everyday objects as a retrieval starter; link to the end-of-year measurement check (recording results in a table).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** **test three objects** (one metal, one plastic, one wood) and sort them, saying lit/not-lit for each.
- **Stretch (challenge):** **predict before testing** every object and explain the **plastic-coat-on-a-metal-wire** design; investigate the pencil-graphite surprise; ask "would a metal spoon be safe to poke into a plug socket?" (NO — that's exactly why we use cells only).

**Extension (if time allows):** Hunt the house for objects, predict conductor/insulator for each, then design a "safe switch" using a conductor to bridge a gap and an insulator for the handle.
**Assessment evidence:** Child predicts then tests materials, **records predictions and results in a table**, sorts them correctly into conductors (metals) and insulators (plastic/wood/rubber), explains the wire's design, and states the cells-only safety rule.

## End-of-year mastery checks

Use these as a spaced review across the final fortnight — a few each session, mixing units so the child retrieves across the whole year. Each is "show me," not "tell me": the child *does* the science.

1. **Classification (L1–2):** Classify a set of living things **two sensible ways** and **use a yes/no key** to identify one. *Look for:* observable rules, not opinions.
2. **Environments (L3):** **Explain how a change can endanger** living things (lost food/water/shelter) and **name one way humans help**. *Look for:* "humans both harm and help."
3. **Digestion & teeth (L4–5):** **Order the digestive system**, say **where nutrients are absorbed** (small intestine), and **name the three teeth and their jobs**. *Look for:* not "the stomach finishes it."
4. **States of matter (L6–7):** **Sort materials** into solid/liquid/gas and **name the four changes of state**. *Look for:* a gas is real; "evaporated," not "vanished."
5. **Water cycle (L8):** **Order the water cycle** and say **what drives it** (heat from the Sun). *Look for:* it's a cycle, no start or end.
6. **Sound (L9–10):** Explain that sound is **made by vibrations needing a medium**, why **there's no sound in space**, and the difference between **pitch and volume**. *Look for:* faster = higher; bigger = louder.
7. **Circuits (L11):** **Build a circuit** that lights a bulb, explain the **complete loop**, and explain **what a switch does**. *Look for:* loop back to the cell, not one wire.
8. **Conductors & measurement (L12 + working scientifically):** **Sort materials** into conductors and insulators; and (working scientifically) **take a measurement in standard units and record it in a table** — e.g. re-time an ice melt in **seconds**, or record bulb-lit results in a results table. *Look for:* accurate reading; clear table.

---

### Closing note to the tutor

Year 4 is where the working-scientifically thread grows up: the child stops saying "it was faster" and starts saying "it took 47 seconds, and the table shows it." Keep pulling them back to **measure in standard units** and **record in a table or bar chart** — in the ice-melt (L7), the rubber-band guitar (L10), and the conductor test (L12) especially. Keep the inquiry spirit (predict → test → notice → name) and keep every check honest: never reveal the answer, diagnose the *why*, step down a level, and rebuild. End every lesson on an **earned success** with **process praise** — what the child *did* and *how they reasoned* — so they leave the year not just knowing more science, but trusting themselves to do it.
