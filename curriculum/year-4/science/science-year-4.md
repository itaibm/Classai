> Codes: **UK NC Y5** (England National Curriculum, Year 5 science) and **NGSS** (US Next Generation Science Standards, Grades 4–5).

## Year overview

Year 4 science is the year children **own the whole scientific method**: they choose the type of enquiry, plan a fair test, decide which variable to change, measure and repeat, record in tables and graphs, and write conclusions that explain *why* — not just *what* — happened. Every unit contains at least one full investigation and a "how do we know?" story about a real scientist from somewhere in the world.

**The four strands:**
- **Chemistry — Properties and changes of materials (Term 1).** Comparing materials by hardness, solubility, transparency, conductivity and magnetism; dissolving and the idea that a dissolved solid is still there; separating mixtures by sieving, filtering, evaporating and magnets; reversible and irreversible changes, including new materials made by burning and chemical reactions.
- **Earth and space (Term 2).** The Sun, Earth and Moon as roughly spherical bodies; the Solar System; day and night from Earth's rotation; the Moon's orbit and phases; how people from Ptolemy to Aryabhata, Copernicus and Galileo built our model of the heavens.
- **Physics — Forces (Term 2).** Gravity and weight (newtons); air resistance, water resistance and friction; levers, pulleys and gears that let a small force have a big effect.
- **Biology — Life cycles, reproduction and growing up (Term 3, timed for spring and summer when plants flower and tadpoles hatch).** Life cycles of mammals, amphibians, insects and birds; sexual and asexual reproduction in plants; human development from birth to old age.

**Working scientifically (every unit):** question → predict → plan a fair test (one independent variable, controlled variables) → measure with increasing precision (thermometers, newton meters, stopwatches, measuring cylinders, data loggers where available) → record (tables, bar and line graphs — linked to maths Unit 9) → conclude and explain → evaluate (how could we trust our results more?). Children learn to repeat readings and spot anomalous results.

**What Year 4 assumes (from Year 3):** classification of living things and simple keys; digestion and teeth; states of matter and the water cycle (melting, freezing, evaporating, condensing); sound as vibration; simple circuits, conductors and insulators; making a fair test with help and recording in tables.

**What Year 5 relies on from Year 4:** confident planning of controlled investigations (for the Year 5 capstone investigation); understanding of dissolving, mixtures and irreversible change (for later chemistry); life cycles and reproduction (for inheritance, variation and evolution); forces and measurement in newtons; the Earth–Sun–Moon model.

## Unit 1 — Properties of materials

**Essential question:** How do scientists and engineers choose the right material for the job?

**Key vocabulary:** material, property, hardness, strength, absorbent, waterproof, flexibility, transparent, translucent, opaque, electrical conductor, electrical insulator, thermal conductor, thermal insulator, magnetic, soluble, insoluble, fair test, variable

### Lesson 1 — Why materials matter
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe materials using several properties and explain why a material suits its use." (UK NC Y5 properties of materials; NGSS 5-PS1-3)
- **Hook:** A mystery bag: a metal spoon, a rubber glove, a glass jar, a wooden block, a plastic bottle. "Why do so many saucepans have plastic or wooden handles?"
- **Key activity:** Five-minute retrieval: from Year 2 (magnets) and Year 3 (circuits), children sort samples into 'magnetic?' and 'conducts electricity?' from memory, then check two with a magnet and a tester circuit. Then explore and describe objects with property words; match property to purpose (glass — transparent for windows; rubber — flexible and waterproof for boots). Children record in a property table.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a frying pan made of metal but its handle of plastic or wood?" → Metal conducts heat to cook the food; plastic/wood are thermal insulators so the handle stays cool enough to hold.
- **Differentiation:** support: property word cards with pictures. / stretch: suggest a new use for a material based on two of its properties.
- **Materials:** a tray of everyday objects in different materials, hand lenses, property tables.
- **Joy:** "Mystery bag" — identify a material by touch and describe it for a partner to guess.

### Lesson 2 — Hardness: the scratch test
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can carry out a fair scratch test to compare the hardness of materials." (UK NC Y5 compare materials — hardness; NGSS 5-PS1-3)
- **Hook:** "Which is harder — a fingernail, a copper coin or a steel nail? How could we find out fairly?"
- **Key activity:** Plan a fair test (same scratcher, same pressure); scratch samples (chalk, wood, soap, plastic, a glass tile — scratch only with a steel nail under supervision); order by hardness. Introduce Mohs' scale (from the German mineralogist Friedrich Mohs).
- **Check for understanding:** "What did you keep the same to make the test fair, and why?" → The same scratching object and similar pressure, so only the material changes.
- **Differentiation:** support: fewer samples and a results table provided. / stretch: explain why engineers need hardness data (e.g. for flooring or drill bits).
- **Materials:** samples (chalk, soap, wood, plastic, a small glass tile), steel nails, safety goggles, results tables. Safety: goggles on; adult handles glass.
- **Joy:** Crowning the "hardest material" in the class hardness league.

### Lesson 3 — Transparent, translucent and opaque
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can test and classify materials by how much light passes through them." (UK NC Y5 compare materials — transparency)
- **Hook:** "Design curtains for a baby's bedroom — they must block light completely. Which fabric?"
- **Key activity:** Shine a torch through samples onto a white screen; classify as transparent, translucent or opaque; record; discuss measuring more precisely (a light sensor or data logger if available).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is frosted glass used in a bathroom window?" → It is translucent: light comes in but you can't see through clearly, so it gives privacy.
- **Differentiation:** support: three sorting hoops labelled. / stretch: use a light meter app or data logger to rank samples with numbers.
- **Materials:** torches, fabric and plastic samples, tracing paper, cling film, white card screen.
- **Joy:** "Shadow puppet test" — which material makes the best shadow puppet?

### Lesson 4 — Strength: which thread holds the most? (investigation)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can carry out a fair test to compare the strength of materials and use the results to choose a material for a job." (UK NC Y5 compare and group materials by their properties; give reasons for particular uses)
- **Hook:** "A climbing rope, a fishing line and a kite string all have to hold weight without snapping. Which material would you trust?"
- **Key activity:** Groups test equal lengths of cotton thread, wool, nylon fishing line, paper string and raffia: hang each from a clamp or a table edge, hook a small bag on the end and add washers (or read the pull on a newton meter) until it snaps. Repeat each three times; record the load at breaking in a table; rank the materials and link to uses (nylon climbing ropes, cotton sewing thread).
- **Check for understanding:** "Group A says nylon is stronger than wool because their nylon held 40 N and their wool held 25 N — but their wool piece was much thinner. Can they be sure? What should they change?" → No — thickness was not kept the same, so it may have caused the difference; repeat with pieces of equal thickness and length
- **Differentiation:** support: three materials only, with the results table and a 'keep the same' checklist provided. / stretch: explain why engineers test many samples of a material, not just one, before building a bridge cable.
- **Materials:** threads and strings of equal length, washers and a small bag or newton meters (0–50 N), clamps, results tables. Safety: goggles on; keep feet clear of falling masses (a tray of sand or a cushion underneath).
- **Joy:** The "snap-o-meter" league table — watch the class strongman thread crowned.

### Lesson 5 — Thermal insulators: a fair test (planning)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a fair test to find which material keeps a drink warm longest." (UK NC Y5 compare materials — thermal conductivity; working scientifically — plan)
- **Hook:** "Explorers in Antarctica, desert nomads, and your lunchbox all need insulation. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 3's ice-cube wrapping test (which only timed melting); this time measure temperature with a thermometer so the results make a cooling curve. Identify variables: change (material wrapped around cup), measure (temperature every 5 min), keep the same (starting temperature, volume of water, cup type, time). Plan a results table; make predictions with reasons.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which variable are you changing and which must stay the same? Why does it matter?" → Change the wrapping material; keep water volume, start temperature and cup the same, so any difference is caused by the material.
- **Differentiation:** support: a planning frame with variable boxes. / stretch: plan to repeat readings and explain why.
- **Materials:** planning frames, cups, thermometers, wrapping materials (foil, wool, bubble wrap, newspaper, cotton).
- **Joy:** "Keep the tea warm" challenge set up for next lesson.

### Lesson 6 — Thermal insulators: carrying out and graphing
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can carry out the insulation test, record results and plot a line graph." (UK NC Y5 working scientifically — record with line graphs)
- **Hook:** Start the clock — "Science happens in the waiting."
- **Key activity:** Groups test one material each; record temperatures every 5 minutes for 25 minutes; plot line graphs (link to maths); compare cooling curves.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which line on the graph shows the best insulator? How can you tell?" → The line that falls least steeply — its temperature stays higher for longer.
- **Differentiation:** support: axes pre-drawn and labelled. / stretch: identify any anomalous reading and suggest a cause.
- **Materials:** warm (not hot) water from a flask, thermometers or data loggers, stopwatches, graph paper. Safety: water no hotter than a warm bath; adults pour.
- **Joy:** Watching the class results come together on one big graph.

### Lesson 7 — Absorbency: which material soaks up most? (investigation)
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can measure how absorbent different materials are and explain why absorbency suits some jobs and not others." (UK NC Y5 compare and group materials by their properties)
- **Hook:** "A spill on the kitchen floor, a nappy, a raincoat: when do we want a material to soak up water — and when do we definitely not?"
- **Key activity:** Groups cut equal squares of kitchen towel, cotton cloth, sponge cloth, newspaper and plastic; dip each in water for 10 seconds, let it drip for 10 seconds, then squeeze the water into a measuring cylinder (or weigh before and after). Record in ml, repeat, and rank; match results to real uses (towels, mops, tents, sports shirts that 'wick').
- **Check for understanding:** "The sponge cloth held the most water. Would it make a good tent? Use two properties to explain." → No — a tent needs to be waterproof (not absorbent) so rain runs off; the sponge cloth soaks water up and would get heavy and drip
- **Differentiation:** support: three materials and a pre-drawn results table. / stretch: explain why weighing before and after might be more accurate than squeezing out the water.
- **Materials:** equal squares of five materials, trays of water, timers, measuring cylinders or digital scales, results tables.
- **Joy:** "Spill rescue" — use the winning material to mop a (water) disaster fastest.

### Lesson 8 — Soluble and insoluble
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can test which solids dissolve in water and use the words soluble and insoluble." (UK NC Y5 dissolving; NGSS 5-PS1-4)
- **Hook:** "Where does sugar go when you stir it into tea?"
- **Key activity:** Add a teaspoon of each solid (sugar, salt, sand, flour, coffee granules, chalk dust) to the same volume of water, stir the same number of times, observe; classify.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is the sugar still there after it dissolves? How could you prove it?" → Yes; the water tastes sweet (in a safe food test) and you can get it back by evaporating the water; the mass is the same.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture results table. / stretch: weigh water and sugar before and after dissolving to show mass is conserved.
- **Materials:** clear cups, water, measuring spoons, solids listed, stirrers, digital scales.
- **Joy:** The "vanishing sugar" mystery.

### Lesson 9 — What affects how quickly sugar dissolves? (investigation)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan and carry out a fair test on one factor that affects dissolving." (UK NC Y5 working scientifically — plan and control variables; NGSS 5-PS1-4)
- **Hook:** "Sugar cubes or granulated sugar in cold drinks — which dissolves faster?"
- **Key activity:** Groups choose one independent variable (water temperature, stirring, particle size), plan, time how long until no crystals are visible, repeat three times, calculate the typical time.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did you repeat your test three times?" → To check results are reliable and spot any unusual (anomalous) readings.
- **Differentiation:** support: variable chosen for them with a planning frame. / stretch: explain the result using the idea of tiny particles moving faster in warm water.
- **Materials:** sugar cubes and granulated sugar, warm and cold water, stopwatches, measuring cylinders, thermometers. Safety: warm water only.
- **Joy:** Class "dissolving race" with predictions.

### Lesson 10 — Materials scientists: inventing new materials
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how scientists create materials with useful properties, with examples." (UK NC Y5 properties of materials — scientists and inventors)
- **Hook:** A bulletproof vest is made from a fibre invented in a laboratory. Who invented it?
- **Key activity:** Stories: Stephanie Kwolek (USA) invented Kevlar in 1965; Charles Macintosh (Scotland) made waterproof fabric in the 1820s; discuss how properties were tested. Children design a new material for a purpose and list the properties it needs.
- **Check for understanding:** "Name two properties Kevlar needs to protect a firefighter or police officer." → Very strong and hard to tear; heat-resistant.
- **Differentiation:** support: property menu cards. / stretch: describe a test to prove your material works.
- **Materials:** fact cards, sample of waterproof fabric, design sheets.
- **Joy:** "Invent a super-material" posters.

### Lesson 11 — Choosing materials: a design challenge
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use test results to choose and justify materials for a design." (UK NC Y5 give reasons, based on evidence, for particular uses of materials)
- **Hook:** "Design a lunchbox for a school trip in a hot country — it must keep food cool, be light and not break."
- **Key activity:** Use results from Lessons 2–9 to choose materials; build a prototype from recycled materials; justify with evidence.
- **Check for understanding:** "Justify one material choice using a result from our tests." → e.g. "Bubble wrap, because it was the best thermal insulator in our cup test."
- **Differentiation:** support: choose from three materials. / stretch: balance two conflicting properties (strong but light).
- **Materials:** recycled boxes, fabric, foil, bubble wrap, tape, scissors.
- **Joy:** Prototype showcase and an inventors' pitch to a panel of 'investors' from the class.

### Lesson 12 — Unit 1 review and assessment
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain material properties and describe a fair test clearly." (UK NC Y5 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Materials museum — curate and explain."
- **Key activity:** Short quiz with reasoning questions; each child writes a fair-test plan for a new question (e.g. which fabric is most waterproof?).
- **Check for understanding:** "Plan how to test which fabric is most waterproof — what will you change, measure and keep the same?" → Change fabric; measure water that passes through (ml); keep water volume, fabric size and time the same.
- **Differentiation:** support: planning frame. / stretch: include repeats and how to present results.
- **Materials:** assessment sheet.
- **Joy:** "Materials museum" labels.

**End-of-unit check:** Sort 8 materials by three properties with evidence; write a fair-test plan for waterproofing; justify one material choice using two test results from this unit.

## Unit 2 — Changes of materials: dissolving, separating, reversible and irreversible

**Essential question:** Which changes can be undone — and how can we get materials back once they're mixed?

**Key vocabulary:** mixture, solution, solute, solvent, dissolve, sieve, filter, evaporate, condense, reversible change, irreversible change, new material, burning, rusting, reaction, gas, carbon dioxide

### Lesson 13 — Mixtures and solutions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can tell the difference between a mixture and a solution." (UK NC Y5 dissolving and solutions; NGSS 5-PS1-4)
- **Hook:** Two jars: sand in water and salt in water. "Which one is hiding something?"
- **Key activity:** Observe mixtures (sand and water, rice and peas) vs solutions (salt water, sugar water); vocabulary solute, solvent, solution; model with particle diagrams.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can't you see the salt in salt water, but you can see the sand?" → Salt has dissolved — broken into tiny particles spread through the water; sand is insoluble and stays as visible grains.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards to sort. / stretch: draw particle diagrams for a mixture and a solution.
- **Materials:** jars, salt, sand, sugar, rice, dried peas, water.
- **Joy:** "Magic" disappearing salt demonstration.

### Lesson 14 — Separating by sieving and magnets
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose sieving or magnets to separate a mixture and explain why each works." (UK NC Y5 separating mixtures)
- **Hook:** "Oh no — the paper clips, rice, flour and dried peas were all spilled together!"
- **Key activity:** Plan a separation sequence: magnet for steel clips, coarse sieve for peas, fine sieve for rice and flour.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a sieve separate rice from flour?" → The holes let the small flour particles through but stop the larger rice grains.
- **Differentiation:** support: step cards to order. / stretch: design the most efficient order and justify it.
- **Materials:** sieves of different mesh sizes, magnets, trays, mixtures.
- **Joy:** "Spill rescue" race.

### Lesson 15 — Filtering
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can filter an insoluble solid from a liquid and explain what the filter paper does." (UK NC Y5 separating mixtures — filtering)
- **Hook:** Muddy water: "Can we make it clear?"
- **Key activity:** Fold filter paper into a funnel; filter sand/soil and water; compare with trying to filter salt water (salt goes through).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can't filtering remove salt from salt water?" → The salt has dissolved into particles small enough to pass through the filter paper's holes.
- **Differentiation:** support: step-by-step picture guide. / stretch: explain why filtered water still isn't safe to drink (microbes and dissolved substances).
- **Materials:** filter paper or coffee filters, funnels, beakers, muddy water, salt water.
- **Joy:** Watching muddy water drip clear.

### Lesson 16 — Evaporating to recover a dissolved solid
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recover a dissolved solid by evaporation and explain it as a reversible change." (UK NC Y5 recover a substance from a solution)
- **Hook:** Photos of salt pans in Gujarat, India, and on the coast of Senegal: "How do people get salt from the sea?"
- **Key activity:** Pour salt water into shallow dishes; leave on a sunny windowsill or near a radiator for several days (set up now, observe later); link to salt production around the world.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where does the water go, and why is the salt left behind?" → The water evaporates into the air as water vapour; the salt can't evaporate at room temperature so it stays.
- **Differentiation:** support: labelled diagram to complete. / stretch: design a way to also collect the water (condensation, e.g. a solar still).
- **Materials:** shallow dishes, salt, water, hand lenses, photos of salt pans.
- **Joy:** Seeing salt crystals appear days later under a hand lens.

### Lesson 17 — Separation challenge (investigation)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan and carry out the separation of a complex mixture using several methods." (UK NC Y5 separating mixtures — plan)
- **Hook:** "A detective's evidence bag: sand, salt, iron filings and water. Recover each one."
- **Key activity:** Plan the order (magnet → filter → evaporate), carry it out, record with a flow diagram, evaluate.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must you remove the iron filings before filtering?" → Otherwise they would stay in the filter paper mixed with the sand; a magnet separates them cleanly first.
- **Differentiation:** support: a three-step plan to follow. / stretch: explain what would go wrong in a different order.
- **Materials:** iron filings (in a sealed bag or with the magnet wrapped in cling film), sand, salt, water, filter kit, evaporating dishes, magnets. Safety: iron filings kept away from eyes.
- **Joy:** "Evidence recovered" detective certificate.

### Lesson 18 — Reversible changes
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify reversible changes and explain how to reverse them." (UK NC Y5 reversible changes)
- **Hook:** An ice lolly melting: "Can we get the lolly back?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval of Year 3's changes of state (melting, freezing, evaporating, condensing); the new idea is that dissolving and mixing are also reversible. Stations: recover salt from salt water (the dishes set up in Lesson 16), sieve sand back out of a sand–water mixture, and refreeze melted juice. Children record each change, how it was reversed and the evidence that no new material was made.
- **Check for understanding:** "Salt dissolving in water and juice freezing are both reversible. How would you reverse each one, and what is the same about the two changes?" → evaporate the water to get the salt back; warm the ice to get juice again; in both, no new material is made — the same substances are there before and after
- **Differentiation:** support: change-and-reverse matching cards. / stretch: explain why dissolved salt can be recovered but the water is lost unless you condense it.
- **Materials:** salt-water dishes from Lesson 16, sand, water, sieves, juice, zip bags, ice, salt.
- **Joy:** Instant slush: squeeze a bag of juice inside a bag of ice and salt and watch it freeze in minutes.

### Lesson 19 — Irreversible changes: new materials
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that some changes make new materials and cannot easily be reversed." (UK NC Y5 irreversible changes; NGSS 5-PS1-4)
- **Hook:** "Can you un-bake a cake?"
- **Key activity:** Demonstrations and observations: bicarbonate of soda + vinegar (fizzing = a new gas, carbon dioxide); a candle burning (teacher-only demo); iron nails rusting over a week; baking. List evidence of a new material (gas, colour change, heat, a new substance).
- **Check for understanding:** "What evidence shows that mixing vinegar and bicarbonate of soda makes a new material?" → Fizzing shows a gas (carbon dioxide) is made that wasn't there before; you can't separate the vinegar and bicarbonate back out.
- **Differentiation:** support: evidence checklist. / stretch: explain why burning is useful and dangerous.
- **Materials:** vinegar, bicarbonate of soda, balloons and bottles, candle and holder (teacher demo only), nails, water, salt water. Safety: fire demo by adult only, fire blanket/extinguisher nearby; goggles for fizzing reactions.
- **Joy:** Inflate a balloon with gas from the reaction.

### Lesson 20 — Rusting investigation (set-up)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan an investigation into what makes iron rust." (UK NC Y5 irreversible changes — working scientifically)
- **Hook:** Photos of a rusty ship and a shiny bridge: "Why does one rust and not the other?"
- **Key activity:** Set up nails in: dry air (with a drying agent), tap water, salt water, water that an adult has boiled and cooled with a layer of oil on top (boiling removes dissolved air; the oil stops air getting back in), and a painted nail in water; predict; observe over a week.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the variable you are changing, and what will you observe?" → The conditions around the nail; the amount of rust.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-labelled jars. / stretch: predict which condition rusts fastest and explain.
- **Materials:** iron nails, jars, water, salt, cooking oil, paint, silica gel (for dry air).
- **Joy:** A "rust race" with photos each day.

### Lesson 21 — Rusting results and conclusions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw a conclusion from my rusting results and explain how to prevent rust." (UK NC Y5 report conclusions)
- **Hook:** The reveal — photos from each day.
- **Key activity:** Compare results; conclude that iron needs water and oxygen to rust and salt speeds it up; relate to painting, oiling and galvanising.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does painting a bridge stop it rusting?" → The paint keeps water and air away from the iron.
- **Differentiation:** support: conclusion sentence frames. / stretch: evaluate the investigation and suggest improvements.
- **Materials:** nails from last lesson, photos, conclusion frames.
- **Joy:** "Rust-proof" design brief for a bicycle.

### Lesson 22 — Changes around us: cooking and burning
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can classify everyday changes as reversible or irreversible with reasons." (UK NC Y5 reversible and irreversible changes)
- **Hook:** Photos of making flatbread, boiling an egg, melting butter, dissolving jelly.
- **Key activity:** Card sort; discuss tricky cases (dissolving jelly and letting it set — can you tell?); reason with evidence of a new material.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is boiling an egg reversible? How do you know?" → No — the egg white turns solid and white; it can't be turned back into runny egg.
- **Differentiation:** support: two-way sort with picture cards. / stretch: debate the tricky cases.
- **Materials:** change cards, photos.
- **Joy:** Make popcorn (irreversible — adult-supervised) and discuss.

### Lesson 23 — Retrieval: materials and changes
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall and explain properties, separating methods and types of change." (UK NC Y5 — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "Science quiz show."
- **Key activity:** Team quiz with reasoning rounds; one "explain the mistake" round.
- **Check for understanding:** "Leo says dissolving is irreversible because you can't see the salt. What would you tell him?" → The salt is still there; evaporating the water gets it back, so it is reversible.
- **Differentiation:** support: word banks. / stretch: bonus "design an experiment" round.
- **Materials:** quiz cards.
- **Joy:** Buzzers and team names.

### Lesson 24 — Unit 2 assessment
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how to separate mixtures and classify changes." (UK NC Y5 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Lab technician's exam."
- **Key activity:** Assessment tasks: plan a separation; classify changes; interpret a results table.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain how to get clean water and salt from muddy salt water." → Filter out mud; evaporate water to leave salt; to collect water, condense the vapour.
- **Differentiation:** support: step prompts. / stretch: explain a solar still.
- **Materials:** assessment sheet.
- **Joy:** "Lab licence" certificate.

**End-of-unit check:** Explain how to separate sand, salt and iron filings; classify five changes as reversible or irreversible with evidence; write a conclusion from given rusting data.

## Unit 3 — Earth and space

**Essential question:** How do we know the Earth spins and orbits the Sun, when it feels as if the Sun moves around us?

**Key vocabulary:** Sun, star, planet, dwarf planet, moon, orbit, rotate, axis, Solar System, spherical, geocentric, heliocentric, day, night, year, phase, crescent, gibbous, time zone

### Lesson 25 — The Sun, Earth and Moon: spheres in space
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the Sun, Earth and Moon as approximately spherical and explain the evidence." (UK NC Y5 Earth and space; NGSS 5-ESS1-1)
- **Hook:** Photographs of Earth from space (e.g. "Earthrise", Apollo 8, 1968): "People once argued about the shape of Earth. How did they know?"
- **Key activity:** Evidence for a round Earth: ships disappearing hull-first over the horizon; Earth's curved shadow on the Moon during a lunar eclipse (noted by Aristotle); photos from space. Model with balls.
- **Check for understanding:** "Give one piece of evidence that Earth is round that people had before space travel." → Ships disappear hull-first over the horizon, or Earth's round shadow on the Moon in an eclipse.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture evidence cards. / stretch: explain how Eratosthenes estimated Earth's size using shadows in Egypt (about 240 BCE).
- **Materials:** balls of different sizes, torch, toy boat and globe, space photographs.
- **Joy:** Model a ship sailing over a globe's "horizon".

### Lesson 26 — The Solar System
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can name the planets in order from the Sun and describe some of their features." (UK NC Y5 the Solar System; NGSS 5-ESS1-1)
- **Hook:** "My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming" — what could this mean?
- **Key activity:** Order the eight planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune); rocky vs gas and ice giants; Pluto reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006. Create planet fact files.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is Pluto no longer called a planet?" → Scientists (the International Astronomical Union, 2006) decided a planet must have cleared its orbit of other objects; Pluto shares its region with many others, so it is a dwarf planet.
- **Differentiation:** support: planet cards with pictures to order. / stretch: explain why the inner planets are rocky and outer ones are giants of gas and ice (introduce simply).
- **Materials:** planet cards, fact books, a mnemonic poster.
- **Joy:** Invent the class's own planet-order mnemonic.

### Lesson 27 — Scale of the Solar System (outdoor model)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a scale model to show the sizes and distances in the Solar System." (UK NC Y5 Solar System; working scientifically — models)
- **Hook:** "If the Sun were a football, how big would Earth be — and how far away?"
- **Key activity:** Toilet-roll model of distances on the playground (using a calculated scale); compare sizes (Earth a peppercorn-sized ball compared with a football Sun at the same scale); discuss limits of models.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it hard to show both the sizes and the distances of planets on one poster?" → Distances are so huge compared with sizes that the planets would be too small to see.
- **Differentiation:** support: roles in the model (planet holders). / stretch: calculate distances for the model using a given scale.
- **Materials:** toilet roll or measuring tape, football, peppercorns/beads, planet labels, scale table.
- **Joy:** Walking the Solar System across the playground.

### Lesson 28 — Day and night: a rotating Earth
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use Earth's rate of spin (360° in 24 hours) to explain and predict how much later sunrise comes in places further west." (UK NC Y5 day and night; NGSS 5-ESS1-2)
- **Hook:** "In Year 2 we learned that Earth spins towards the east, so the Sun rises in the east. But how fast does it turn — and can we use that to predict sunrise?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval with torch (Sun) and globe: Earth spins west to east (anticlockwise seen from above the North Pole). New: 360° in 24 hours means 15° every hour. Children mark lines of longitude 15° apart on a paper strip round the globe, turn the globe one 'hour' at a time and watch sunrise move west by one strip each hour; then test the idea with real sunrise times for cities in one country on the same date (e.g. Shanghai, about 121° E, and Kashgar, about 76° E, which both use Beijing time); children act as Earth spinning in the classroom.
- **Check for understanding:** "Earth turns 360° in 24 hours. How many degrees does it turn in one hour — and so about how much later does sunrise come in a town 30° further west?" → 15° per hour (360 ÷ 24); about 2 hours later, because the town needs 30 ÷ 15 = 2 more hours of spin to turn into the sunlight
- **Differentiation:** support: guided model with the teacher, with east and west labelled on the globe. / stretch: explain why it is night in Tokyo when it is midday in Lagos.
- **Materials:** globe, strong torch or lamp, stickers, dark room.
- **Joy:** "Human Earth" spinning under a lamp "Sun".

### Lesson 29 — The Sun is a star (investigation)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that the Sun is a star and use evidence to show that it looks brighter than other stars because it is much closer." (NGSS 5-ESS1-1; UK NC Y5 Earth and space)
- **Hook:** "Some of the stars you see at night give out far more light than the Sun. So why do they look like tiny dots?"
- **Key activity:** In a darkened room, measure the brightness of the same torch at 1, 2, 4 and 8 metres with a light-meter app (or a brightness scale on a white card); repeat, record and plot a line graph. Conclude that the same light looks dimmer the further away it is. Apply to stars: the Sun is an ordinary star about 150 million km away; the next nearest star is about 270,000 times further. Share star stories from several cultures (e.g. Matariki / the Pleiades in Aotearoa New Zealand; the Southern Cross in many southern-hemisphere traditions).
- **Check for understanding:** "Sirius gives out about 25 times as much light as the Sun, yet it looks like a tiny dot. Use your torch results to explain why." → Sirius is vastly further away than the Sun; our results showed that the same light source looks dimmer the further away it is, so a brighter star can look much fainter
- **Differentiation:** support: two distances only and a brightness card instead of the app. / stretch: explain why two torches, one brighter than the other, could look equally bright from where you stand.
- **Materials:** identical torches, light-meter app or brightness cards, measuring tapes, a darkened room, star-map printouts. Safety: never look directly at the Sun or shine torches into eyes.
- **Joy:** Make a pinhole "star lantern" of a constellation from another culture.

### Lesson 30 — Time zones
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how time zones follow Earth's spin (15° per hour) and why clock time and Sun time do not always match." (UK NC Y5 Earth's rotation; links to maths time)
- **Hook:** "China is about as wide as the USA, but the whole country uses one clock time. What happens to sunrise in the far west?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 2's world clocks in two minutes. New: time zones are roughly 15° of longitude wide because Earth turns 15° each hour; but borders and governments bend them. Compare a time-zone map with lines of longitude; look at China (one time zone across about 60° of longitude) and at countries that use half-hour zones (e.g. India); link to Lesson 28's sunrise times.
- **Check for understanding:** "China stretches across about 60° of longitude but uses one clock time. About how many hours apart is sunrise at its eastern and western edges, and what problem might that cause?" → about 4 hours (60 ÷ 15); in the far west the Sun rises very late by the clock, so the school and work day can start in the dark
- **Differentiation:** support: a world clock display. / stretch: explain the International Date Line simply.
- **Materials:** globe, lamp, world time-zone map, clocks.
- **Joy:** "Wake up, world!" — act out the world waking up around the globe.

### Lesson 31 — Earth's orbit: a year and the seasons
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that Earth orbits the Sun once a year and that its tilt causes the seasons." (UK NC Y5 Earth's movement relative to the Sun; NGSS 5-ESS1-2)
- **Hook:** "Is it summer in December? Ask someone in Australia!"
- **Key activity:** Model the orbit with a tilted globe (about 23.4°) around a lamp; see the Northern Hemisphere tilted towards the Sun in June and away in December. Address the misconception that seasons are caused by distance from the Sun.
- **Check for understanding:** "If seasons were caused by distance from the Sun, why would Australia have summer when Europe has winter?" → Both hemispheres would have the same season at the same time; opposite seasons show the tilt is the cause.
- **Differentiation:** support: labelled diagram of four orbit positions. / stretch: explain why the equator has little seasonal change.
- **Materials:** globe on a tilted stand, lamp, labels for months, orbit mat.
- **Joy:** "Seasons dance" around the lamp.

### Lesson 32 — The Moon's orbit and phases
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that the Moon orbits Earth and that its phases come from how much of its sunlit side we see." (UK NC Y5 the Moon; NGSS 5-ESS1-2)
- **Hook:** "Does the Moon really change shape?"
- **Key activity:** In a darkened room with one lamp, children hold a ball on a stick at arm's length and turn slowly; watch the lit part change (new, crescent, quarter, gibbous, full). The Moon reflects sunlight; one cycle of phases takes about 29.5 days.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we sometimes see only a crescent Moon?" → We are seeing only a small part of the half of the Moon that is lit by the Sun.
- **Differentiation:** support: phase cards to order. / stretch: explain why we always see the same face of the Moon.
- **Materials:** polystyrene balls on sticks, a single bright lamp, dark room, Moon-phase calendar.
- **Joy:** A month-long "Moon diary" of observations at home.

### Lesson 33 — How we know: from Ptolemy to Galileo
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how ideas about the Solar System changed and what evidence changed them." (UK NC Y5 Earth and space — scientists; NGSS 5-ESS1-1)
- **Hook:** "For over a thousand years, most scholars believed the Sun circled the Earth. Why did people change their minds?"
- **Key activity:** Timeline and role cards: Ptolemy (geocentric model, Alexandria, 2nd century CE); Aryabhata (India, 5th century CE, proposed Earth rotates on its axis); Nicolaus Copernicus (Poland, heliocentric model published 1543); Galileo Galilei (Italy, used a telescope in 1610 to observe Jupiter's moons and the phases of Venus). Discuss evidence and why new ideas are resisted.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why was Galileo's sighting of moons orbiting Jupiter important?" → It showed not everything orbits the Earth, supporting the Sun-centred model.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture timeline. / stretch: explain what a good model must do (fit all the evidence and make predictions).
- **Materials:** timeline cards, portraits and diagrams, a simple telescope or binoculars.
- **Joy:** Role-play a "debate across the centuries".

### Lesson 34 — Space exploration today
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe milestones in space exploration from different countries." (UK NC Y5 Earth and space — science in the world)
- **Hook:** "Who was the first human in space — and what have we learned since?"
- **Key activity:** Milestones: Yuri Gagarin (1961, first human in space), Valentina Tereshkova (1963, first woman), Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin (1969, Moon landing), Mae Jemison (1992), the International Space Station, India's Chandrayaan-3 landing near the Moon's south pole (2023). Build a class timeline.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do countries work together on the International Space Station?" → Space exploration is expensive and complex; sharing knowledge, money and skills achieves more.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards. / stretch: research a current mission and present one fact.
- **Materials:** timeline strips, space-agency images (NASA, ESA, ISRO, JAXA).
- **Joy:** Design a mission patch.

### Lesson 35 — Retrieval: Earth and space
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain day, night, years, seasons and Moon phases using models." (UK NC Y5 — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "Teach it back — you are the planetarium guide."
- **Key activity:** Groups use models to explain one phenomenon to another group; peer questions.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain the difference between rotation and orbit with an example." → Rotation is spinning on an axis (Earth, once a day); orbit is travelling around another body (Earth around the Sun, once a year).
- **Differentiation:** support: model cards with prompts. / stretch: answer "tricky questions" from peers.
- **Materials:** globes, lamps, balls.
- **Joy:** Planetarium guide role.

### Lesson 36 — Unit 3 assessment
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain Earth and space ideas with diagrams and evidence." (UK NC Y5 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Mission control qualification."
- **Key activity:** Diagram-based assessment: label a Solar System, explain day/night, order Moon phases, explain a historical change of model.
- **Check for understanding:** "Draw and label a diagram to explain why we have night." → Earth, Sun, axis, lit and unlit halves, rotation arrow.
- **Differentiation:** support: partly drawn diagrams. / stretch: explain seasons in the same diagram.
- **Materials:** assessment sheet.
- **Joy:** Astronaut qualification badge.

## Unit 4 — Forces

**Essential question:** What makes things move, slow down and stop — and how can simple machines help us?

**Key vocabulary:** force, push, pull, gravity, weight, mass, newton (N), newton meter, air resistance, water resistance, friction, streamlined, lever, fulcrum, pulley, gear, mechanism

### Lesson 37 — Forces around us
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify forces as pushes and pulls and show them with arrows." (UK NC Y5 forces; NGSS 3-PS2-1 review)
- **Hook:** Tug-of-war with a rope: "Why doesn't the rope move when both sides pull equally?"
- **Key activity:** Force arrows show direction and size; balanced vs unbalanced forces; label forces on photos (a kite, a swimmer, a cyclist).
- **Check for understanding:** "A book rests on a table. Are there forces on it? Explain." → Yes: gravity pulls it down and the table pushes it up; the forces are balanced, so it doesn't move.
- **Differentiation:** support: arrow cards to place on pictures. / stretch: draw arrows of different sizes for unbalanced forces.
- **Materials:** rope, photos, arrow cards.
- **Joy:** Balanced tug-of-war.

### Lesson 38 — Gravity and weight
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that gravity pulls objects towards Earth and measure weight in newtons." (UK NC Y5 gravity; NGSS 5-PS2-1)
- **Hook:** "An astronaut's mass is the same on the Moon — so why can they jump so high?"
- **Key activity:** Use newton meters to weigh classroom objects; discover about 10 N per kilogram on Earth; distinguish mass (kg — how much matter) from weight (N — the pull of gravity); the Moon's gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's.
- **Check for understanding:** "A bag has a mass of 2 kg. What is its weight on Earth, and what would change on the Moon?" → About 20 N; its mass stays 2 kg but its weight would be about one-sixth (roughly 3 N).
- **Differentiation:** support: guided measuring with a partner. / stretch: explain why "I weigh 30 kg" is scientifically inaccurate.
- **Materials:** newton meters (0–10 N and 0–50 N), bags, objects, kg masses.
- **Joy:** "Moon weight" calculator for their school bag.

### Lesson 39 — Falling objects: Galileo and the hammer and feather
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain why objects of different mass fall at the same rate without air resistance." (UK NC Y5 gravity — scientists; working scientifically)
- **Hook:** Drop a book and a sheet of paper together; then put the paper on top of the book. "What changed?"
- **Key activity:** Galileo's ideas about falling objects (the Leaning Tower of Pisa experiment may be a story, but his reasoning and ramp experiments were real); watch the Apollo 15 hammer-and-feather drop on the Moon (David Scott, 1971).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did the hammer and feather hit the Moon's surface together?" → There is no air on the Moon, so no air resistance; gravity alone makes them fall at the same rate.
- **Differentiation:** support: demonstration with commentary. / stretch: predict what happens with two balls of the same size but different masses dropped together on Earth.
- **Materials:** book, paper, balls, video of the Apollo 15 demonstration (NASA archive).
- **Joy:** Recreate the "paper on the book" trick.

### Lesson 40 — Air resistance
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain air resistance as a force that slows moving objects." (UK NC Y5 air resistance)
- **Hook:** Run with and without a large sheet of card: "What do you feel?"
- **Key activity:** Drop flat and crumpled paper; compare; relate to parachutes, sycamore seeds and cyclists crouching.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does crumpled paper fall faster than flat paper of the same mass?" → It has a smaller surface area, so less air resistance acts against its fall.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided observation. / stretch: explain how a cyclist reduces air resistance.
- **Materials:** paper, card sheets, sycamore seeds (or paper spinners).
- **Joy:** Paper spinner (helicopter) making.

### Lesson 41 — Parachute investigation: planning
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can plan a fair test to find how canopy size affects the time a parachute takes to fall." (UK NC Y5 air resistance — plan a fair test; NGSS 3-5-ETS1-3)
- **Hook:** "Egg drop mission briefing: land the payload safely."
- **Key activity:** Choose the independent variable (canopy area); control variables (mass, drop height, string length, material); measure fall time with stopwatches; plan three repeats.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must every parachute be dropped from the same height?" → Height affects fall time; keeping it the same means only the canopy size affects the result.
- **Differentiation:** support: planning frame with variables listed. / stretch: plan how to reduce timing errors (e.g. average of repeats, same timer).
- **Materials:** planning frames, plastic bags or tissue for canopies, string, plasticine or washers (payload).
- **Joy:** Mission patches for parachute teams.

### Lesson 42 — Parachute investigation: testing and concluding
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can carry out the test, calculate averages and draw a conclusion with an explanation." (UK NC Y5 working scientifically — conclusions)
- **Hook:** "3, 2, 1 — drop!"
- **Key activity:** Drop parachutes from a safe height (adult on a secure platform or a stairwell); record times; calculate mean of three repeats; plot a bar or line graph; conclude.
- **Check for understanding:** "What does your graph show, and why?" → Larger canopies fall more slowly because there is more air resistance acting upwards.
- **Differentiation:** support: results table and graph axes provided. / stretch: identify an anomalous result and explain it.
- **Materials:** parachutes, stopwatches, metre sticks, graph paper, a safe drop point. Safety: adults do any dropping from height; children never climb.
- **Joy:** The final egg (or hard-boiled egg) drop with the best design.

### Lesson 43 — Water resistance and streamlining
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can investigate how shape affects water resistance." (UK NC Y5 water resistance)
- **Hook:** Photos of a dolphin, a penguin and a submarine: "What do their shapes have in common?"
- **Key activity:** Shape equal masses of plasticine differently; time how long each takes to sink through a tall cylinder of water (or pull shapes through water with a newton meter).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is a streamlined shape faster through water?" → It pushes less water out of the way, so there's less water resistance.
- **Differentiation:** support: three shapes given. / stretch: link to biomimicry (e.g. the Japanese Shinkansen bullet train nose inspired by a kingfisher's beak).
- **Materials:** plasticine (same mass for each shape), tall clear cylinders or bottles of water, stopwatches.
- **Joy:** Design a "fastest fish" shape.

### Lesson 44 — Friction
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can investigate how the weight of an object affects the friction on it, measuring in newtons and presenting a line graph." (UK NC Y5 friction; working scientifically — line graphs)
- **Hook:** "Why is a sledge full of friends so much harder to pull than an empty one — even on the same snow?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 2's surface test in one minute. New question: does a heavier object need more force to drag? Pairs drag a shoe (or a tray) across the same surface at a steady speed with a newton meter, adding 100 g masses one at a time; repeat each reading three times; plot force against mass on a line graph and describe the pattern.
- **Check for understanding:** "Use your graph to predict the force needed with one more mass than you tested, and explain why a loaded sledge is harder to pull than an empty one." → A prediction that continues the pattern; more weight presses the surfaces together harder, so there is more friction to overcome
- **Differentiation:** support: two surfaces and three masses only, with axes pre-drawn. / stretch: explain why the reading must be taken while the shoe is moving steadily, not as it starts.
- **Materials:** a shoe or tray, newton meters (0–10 N), 100 g masses, one surface, results table, graph paper.
- **Joy:** "Sledge-pull challenge" — predict then measure the force to drag a friend-loaded play mat.

### Lesson 45 — Levers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show how a lever lets a small force lift a large load." (UK NC Y5 mechanisms — levers)
- **Hook:** "Archimedes is said to have claimed: 'Give me a place to stand and I will move the Earth.' What did he mean?"
- **Key activity:** Ruler-and-pencil seesaw; move the fulcrum; measure force needed to lift a load with a newton meter at different distances; find that a longer effort arm needs less force.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is it easier to open a paint tin with a long screwdriver than a short coin?" → The longer lever means your force is further from the fulcrum, so a smaller effort lifts the lid.
- **Differentiation:** support: seesaw demo with coins. / stretch: find a pattern in the results (distance × force).
- **Materials:** rulers, pencils, coins or masses, newton meters, a paint tin and screwdriver.
- **Joy:** Lift the teacher on a playground seesaw lever.

### Lesson 46 — Pulleys
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can build a pulley system and explain how more pulleys reduce the effort needed." (UK NC Y5 mechanisms — pulleys)
- **Hook:** Photo of a construction crane: "How can it lift a steel beam?"
- **Key activity:** Build a single fixed pulley (it changes the direction of the pull but not its size) and then a movable pulley where two lengths of string hold the load; measure effort with newton meters; compare and discuss why the readings are not exactly half (friction and the pulley's own weight).
- **Check for understanding:** "When two strings hold the load, the effort is about half. What is the trade-off?" → You have to pull twice as much string (twice the distance) to lift the load the same height.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-built pulley frames. / stretch: predict the force needed with four supporting ropes.
- **Materials:** cotton reels or pulley kits, string, frames, masses, newton meters.
- **Joy:** "Castle drawbridge" pulley model.

### Lesson 47 — Gears
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how gears change the speed and direction of turning." (UK NC Y5 mechanisms — gears)
- **Hook:** Turn a bicycle pedal in different gears: "Why is one easier for hills?"
- **Key activity:** Gear kits: meshed gears turn in opposite directions; a small gear driving a large gear turns the large one more slowly with more force.
- **Check for understanding:** "A 10-tooth gear turns a 20-tooth gear. How many times does the small gear turn for each turn of the big gear?" → Twice.
- **Differentiation:** support: two-gear trains. / stretch: design a three-gear train so the last gear turns the same direction as the first.
- **Materials:** gear kits, a bicycle (on a stand), egg whisk, old clock mechanism.
- **Joy:** Take apart an old clock or toy (safe, battery removed) to find gears.

### Lesson 48 — Design challenge: a machine to lift a load
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can design and test a mechanism that lifts a load using a lever, pulley or gears." (UK NC Y5 mechanisms; NGSS 3-5-ETS1-1)
- **Hook:** "A village well needs a better bucket lifter."
- **Key activity:** Design, build, test and improve; measure effort force with and without the mechanism.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does your mechanism reduce the effort needed? Show a measurement." → Newton-meter readings before and after.
- **Differentiation:** support: choose one mechanism from a template. / stretch: combine two mechanisms.
- **Materials:** construction kits, cotton reels, dowel, string, card, masses, newton meters.
- **Joy:** Showcase of working lifters.

### Lesson 49 — Retrieval: forces
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain forces and mechanisms using correct vocabulary." (UK NC Y5 — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "Force detectives: label the forces in 10 photos."
- **Key activity:** Force-arrow labelling; mechanism matching; misconception hunt.
- **Check for understanding:** "Ava says heavier objects always fall faster. Use evidence to reply." → Without air resistance they fall at the same rate (hammer and feather); differences on Earth are due to air resistance.
- **Differentiation:** support: word bank. / stretch: explain a skydiver's fall.
- **Materials:** photo cards.
- **Joy:** Detective scoring.

### Lesson 50 — Unit 4 assessment
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain forces and interpret results from a forces investigation." (UK NC Y5 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Engineer's licence."
- **Key activity:** Assessment: force diagrams, weight calculations, interpreting a results table, mechanism explanations.
- **Check for understanding:** "From the friction results table, which surface had the most friction and how do you know?" → The one needing the largest force in newtons.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided questions. / stretch: evaluation of the investigation.
- **Materials:** assessment sheet.
- **Joy:** Engineer's licence.

**End-of-unit check:** Draw force arrows on a falling parachute; convert a 3 kg mass to its approximate weight on Earth (30 N); explain how a lever, pulley or gear gives mechanical advantage; interpret parachute results.

## Unit 5 — Life cycles and reproduction

**Essential question:** How do living things make new life, and why are life cycles so different?

**Key vocabulary:** life cycle, reproduction, sexual, asexual, metamorphosis, complete, incomplete, larva, pupa, nymph, embryo, gestation, pollination, fertilisation, seed dispersal, runner, bulb, tuber, cutting, stamen, anther, stigma, ovary

### Lesson 51 — Life cycles: what do all living things share?
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that all living things have a life cycle of birth, growth, reproduction and death." (UK NC Y5 living things — life cycles; NGSS 3-LS1-1)
- **Hook:** Photos of a sunflower, a salmon, a butterfly and a baby elephant: "What do all these life stories have in common?"
- **Key activity:** Build a general life-cycle wheel; match organisms to stages; discuss why reproduction matters for species survival.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is reproduction essential for a species even though individuals die?" → New individuals replace those that die, so the species continues.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture wheel. / stretch: compare lifespans (mayfly adult hours to a day; Greenland shark centuries).
- **Materials:** photo cards, wheel templates.
- **Joy:** "Life cycle mix-up" matching game.

### Lesson 52 — Mammal life cycles
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the life cycle of mammals and compare gestation periods." (UK NC Y5 life cycles — mammals)
- **Hook:** "Which mammals lay eggs?" (platypus and echidnas)
- **Key activity:** Mammal features (most give birth to live young, all feed young on milk); compare gestation (mouse about 3 weeks, human about 9 months, African elephant about 22 months); discuss the egg-laying monotremes as an exception.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is a platypus a mammal if it lays eggs? Explain." → Yes; it has fur and feeds its young on milk — the features that define mammals; it is one of the few egg-laying mammals.
- **Differentiation:** support: gestation matching cards. / stretch: explain the pattern between size and gestation length.
- **Materials:** fact cards, gestation cards, a bar chart template.
- **Joy:** Plot a class bar chart of gestation periods.

### Lesson 53 — Amphibian life cycles: frogs (observation)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the stages of a frog's life cycle and how the body changes (metamorphosis)." (UK NC Y5 life cycles — amphibians)
- **Hook:** Tadpoles in a classroom tank (sourced responsibly and returned to the pond where legally allowed) or time-lapse video.
- **Key activity:** Observe and draw; stages: frogspawn → tadpole (gills, tail) → tadpole with legs → froglet → adult frog (lungs); explain metamorphosis; begin an observation diary.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a tadpole have gills but an adult frog doesn't need them?" → The tadpole lives in water and breathes through gills; the adult develops lungs to breathe air on land.
- **Differentiation:** support: stage cards to order. / stretch: compare with a different amphibian (e.g. axolotls, which usually keep their gills into adulthood).
- **Materials:** tadpole tank (if appropriate) or time-lapse video, hand lenses, observation diaries. Ethics: follow local wildlife rules; return animals to their original habitat.
- **Joy:** A class tadpole diary with drawings.

### Lesson 54 — Insect life cycles: complete and incomplete metamorphosis
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare complete and incomplete metamorphosis in insects." (UK NC Y5 life cycles — insects)
- **Hook:** "A caterpillar and a butterfly — same animal?"
- **Key activity:** Complete metamorphosis: egg → larva → pupa → adult (butterfly, beetle); incomplete: egg → nymph → adult (grasshopper, dragonfly). Maria Sibylla Merian (German naturalist and artist) painted insect life cycles from her observations in Suriname (1699–1701), helping show that butterflies come from caterpillars.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between a grasshopper's and a butterfly's life cycle?" → The butterfly has a pupa stage and changes completely; the grasshopper nymph looks like a small adult and has no pupa stage.
- **Differentiation:** support: two cycles with picture cards. / stretch: explain an advantage of larva and adult eating different foods.
- **Materials:** life-cycle cards, Merian's illustrations (printed reproductions), butterfly-rearing kit (if available).
- **Joy:** Raise butterflies from caterpillars and release them (where permitted).

### Lesson 55 — Bird life cycles
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe a bird's life cycle and compare it with other animals." (UK NC Y5 life cycles — birds)
- **Hook:** Watch a live nest camera (many conservation groups run them): "What will happen next?"
- **Key activity:** Stages: egg → hatchling → fledgling → adult; nest-building and parental care; compare with amphibians (many eggs, little care) and mammals.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might birds lay fewer eggs than frogs?" → Birds care for their young, so more survive; frogs lay many eggs because most are eaten.
- **Differentiation:** support: order bird-stage cards. / stretch: explain the survival trade-off between number of young and care.
- **Materials:** nest-cam stream, bird life-cycle cards, an empty (cleaned, legally collected) nest if available.
- **Joy:** Build a nest from natural materials.

### Lesson 56 — Comparing life cycles and naturalists
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare the life cycles of different animal groups and describe the work of a naturalist." (UK NC Y5 life cycles — compare; naturalists)
- **Hook:** Jane Goodall watched chimpanzees in Gombe, Tanzania, for decades: "What can you learn by patient watching?"
- **Key activity:** Comparison table (mammal, amphibian, insect, bird); read about Jane Goodall and David Attenborough; children practise a 10-minute naturalist observation of a local animal (birds, snails, ants).
- **Check for understanding:** "Name one similarity and one difference between the frog and bird life cycles." → Both begin as eggs; frogs change body form completely (metamorphosis) and grow up in water, while birds grow into adults without metamorphosis and are usually fed and protected by their parents.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided comparison table. / stretch: evaluate which life cycle gives the best chance of survival in a changing environment.
- **Materials:** comparison tables, field notebooks, clipboards, hand lenses.
- **Joy:** Outdoor naturalist observation.

### Lesson 57 — Parts of a flower and sexual reproduction in plants
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify the parts of a flower and describe pollination and fertilisation." (UK NC Y5 reproduction in plants; NGSS 4-LS1-1)
- **Hook:** Dissect a lily or tulip: "Where does a seed come from?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 2's flower parts quickly; the new work is inside the carpel. Careful dissection; label petals, stamens (anther and filament), carpel (stigma, style, ovary), then cut the ovary open with a hand lens to find the ovules; pollen transfer by insects or wind (pollination), then pollen joins the egg cell (fertilisation) to make a seed.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between pollination and fertilisation?" → Pollination is pollen moving to the stigma; fertilisation is when the pollen's cell joins the egg in the ovary to make a seed.
- **Differentiation:** support: labelled diagram to match. / stretch: explain why flowers pollinated by wind (e.g. grasses) have no bright petals.
- **Materials:** large flowers (lilies/tulips), tweezers, hand lenses, sticky tape, card. Safety: lily pollen stains and is toxic to cats; wash hands.
- **Joy:** A flower-dissection collage.

### Lesson 58 — Seed dispersal investigation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can investigate how seed shape affects dispersal distance." (UK NC Y5 reproduction in plants; working scientifically)
- **Hook:** Sycamore spinners, dandelion clocks, burrs: "Why do plants want their seeds to travel?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 2's dispersal types (wind, animals, water, explosion). New: a fair test — change the wing length of paper 'sycamore' models, keep the paper, paper-clip mass, fan setting and release height the same; measure distance travelled, repeat three times and find the mean; record and conclude.
- **Check for understanding:** "Your longer-winged models travelled further. Explain why, and name two things you kept the same so you can trust the result." → Longer wings meet more air, so the seed falls more slowly and the fan carries it further; e.g. same paper-clip mass and same release height/fan distance
- **Differentiation:** support: test two designs only. / stretch: control the fan distance and repeat for reliability.
- **Materials:** seeds/fruit examples, paper, paper clips, fan, measuring tapes.
- **Joy:** "Seed flight" competition.

### Lesson 59 — Asexual reproduction in plants
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe asexual reproduction in plants and grow new plants from cuttings, bulbs, runners or tubers." (UK NC Y5 reproduction in plants — asexual)
- **Hook:** "Can you grow a new plant without any seeds?"
- **Key activity:** Plant spider-plant runners, potato pieces with eyes (tubers), onion bulbs; take mint or geranium cuttings; begin a growth record. Asexual reproduction makes plants identical to the parent.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why are plants grown from cuttings identical to the parent, but plants from seeds are not?" → A cutting is part of one parent, so it has exactly the same features; a seed forms when a pollen cell joins an egg cell (usually from two different plants), so the new plant is a mix and varies.
- **Differentiation:** support: step picture guide. / stretch: explain one advantage and one disadvantage for farmers of growing crops asexually (e.g. bananas).
- **Materials:** spider plant, potatoes, onions, mint, compost, pots, water.
- **Joy:** A classroom "plant nursery" to take home.

### Lesson 60 — Plant growth record and conclusions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can record and compare the growth of plants from different reproduction methods." (UK NC Y5 working scientifically — record and report)
- **Hook:** "Which new plant is winning the race?"
- **Key activity:** Measure and record growth (height, leaves) over several weeks; plot a line graph; compare methods; write a conclusion.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which method produced the fastest growth, and can you be sure why?" → A reasoned answer that notes other variables (light, water, plant type) also affect growth.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-drawn graph axes. / stretch: identify variables that weren't controlled and suggest improvements.
- **Materials:** rulers, growth records, graph paper.
- **Joy:** Photos of each plant's progress.

### Lesson 61 — Retrieval: life cycles and reproduction
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall and explain life cycles and reproduction in plants and animals." (UK NC Y5 — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "Life-cycle bingo."
- **Key activity:** Bingo with definition clues; one misconception-correction round.
- **Check for understanding:** "Sam says plants only reproduce from seeds. Give two examples to correct him." → Potato tubers and strawberry runners (or bulbs, cuttings).
- **Differentiation:** support: word bank. / stretch: bonus explain round.
- **Materials:** bingo cards.
- **Joy:** Bingo.

### Lesson 62 — Unit 5 assessment
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain and compare life cycles and plant reproduction." (UK NC Y5 — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Naturalist's notebook challenge."
- **Key activity:** Label life cycles; explain pollination vs fertilisation; interpret growth data.
- **Check for understanding:** "Compare complete and incomplete metamorphosis with examples." → Complete has a pupa (butterfly); incomplete has nymphs (grasshopper).
- **Differentiation:** support: partly completed diagrams. / stretch: explain why life cycles differ between habitats.
- **Materials:** assessment sheet.
- **Joy:** Naturalist's badge.

**End-of-unit check:** Draw and label the life cycles of a frog and a butterfly; label a flower's reproductive parts and explain pollination vs fertilisation; give two methods of asexual reproduction with examples.

## Unit 6 — Growing up: human development

**Essential question:** How do humans change from birth to old age?

**Key vocabulary:** baby, toddler, child, adolescent, adult, elderly, puberty, gestation, growth spurt, lifespan, healthy ageing

### Lesson 63 — Human life stages
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the stages of human development and what changes at each." (UK NC Y5 animals including humans — changes from birth to old age)
- **Hook:** Baby photos (with permission) of staff: "Who is who?"
- **Key activity:** Order life stages; list what people can do at each; compare human gestation (about 40 weeks) with other mammals.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is one big change between childhood and adulthood?" → Physical growth and maturity, including puberty; greater independence.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture cards. / stretch: compare how life expectancy differs around the world and suggest reasons (health care, clean water, nutrition).
- **Materials:** life-stage cards, staff baby photos (with permission).
- **Joy:** "Guess the baby" game.

### Lesson 64 — Growth data investigation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can interpret growth data and describe the pattern of human growth." (UK NC Y5 changes as humans develop; working scientifically — interpret data)
- **Hook:** "Do you grow the same amount every year?"
- **Key activity:** Interpret an anonymous height-for-age line graph; identify growth spurts (fastest in infancy and at puberty); measure class heights (optional and sensitive — anonymous data only).
- **Check for understanding:** "At which ages does the graph show the fastest growth, and how can you tell?" → Babyhood and the early teens; the line is steepest there.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided graph reading questions. / stretch: explain why the graph may differ between individuals.
- **Materials:** growth-chart line graphs (e.g. WHO growth charts, simplified), rulers.
- **Joy:** Predict what height a sunflower and a child reach in a year and compare.

### Lesson 65 — Changes at puberty (sensitive, in line with family and school policy)
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe some of the physical and emotional changes during puberty and know who to ask for help." (UK NC Y5 changes as humans develop — puberty, with health education)
- **Hook:** Anonymous question box: "It's normal to have questions."
- **Key activity:** Age-appropriate, factual explanation of body changes (growth, voice, hair, hygiene) and emotional changes; ground rules for respectful discussion; answer anonymous questions. Coordinate with PE & Health and share materials with families first.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does everyone go through puberty at slightly different times?" → Bodies are different; puberty usually starts between about 8 and 14, and every timing is normal.
- **Differentiation:** support: picture-based materials. / stretch: explain how hormones act as chemical messengers.
- **Materials:** agreed, age-appropriate health-education materials, question box. Families informed in advance.
- **Joy:** A calm, respectful space where every question is welcome.

### Lesson 66 — Healthy ageing
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how lifestyle choices affect health through life." (UK NC Y5 animals including humans — health)
- **Hook:** Interview a grandparent or older community member (in class or recorded at home).
- **Key activity:** Discuss changes in old age; habits that support health (exercise, food, sleep, social connection); record interviews.
- **Check for understanding:** "Name two habits that help people stay healthy as they age and explain why." → e.g. regular exercise keeps the heart and muscles strong; staying socially connected supports mental health.
- **Differentiation:** support: interview question sheet. / stretch: compare the questions with a Blue Zones-style comparison of long-lived communities (e.g. Okinawa, Japan).
- **Materials:** interview sheets, recording device (optional).

## Unit 7 — Independent investigation and science fair

**Essential question:** Can I plan, carry out and explain my own scientific investigation?

**Key vocabulary:** question, hypothesis, prediction, independent variable, dependent variable, controlled variable, repeat, mean, anomalous, conclusion, evaluation

### Lesson 67 — Choosing a question and planning
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose a testable question and plan a fair investigation." (UK NC Y5 working scientifically — plan)
- **Hook:** "What have you wondered about all year?"
- **Key activity:** Sort questions into testable and not testable; choose one linked to the year's units (e.g. does salt affect how fast ice melts; does canopy shape affect parachute fall time; does the length of a lever change the effort needed); complete a planning sheet.
- **Check for understanding:** "What will you change, measure and keep the same?" → All three variable types identified correctly.
- **Differentiation:** support: choose from 5 ready-made questions. / stretch: add repeats and plan a graph.
- **Materials:** planning sheets, question cards.
- **Joy:** Freedom to choose the question.

### Lesson 68 — Carrying out the investigation
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can carry out my investigation safely and record results accurately." (UK NC Y5 working scientifically — measure and record)
- **Hook:** "Scientists at work: quiet, careful, curious."
- **Key activity:** Carry out tests; record in tables with units; repeat.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me a result you repeated. Were the readings close? What does that tell you?" → Close repeats suggest reliable results.
- **Differentiation:** support: partner support and a results table template. / stretch: calculate means.
- **Materials:** equipment per investigation, results tables. Safety: teacher checks every plan for safety.
- **Joy:** Real independent experiments.

### Lesson 69 — Analysing, concluding and making a poster
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can present results in a graph, write a conclusion that explains why, and evaluate my method." (UK NC Y5 working scientifically — report and evaluate)
- **Hook:** "Your results tell a story — what is it?"
- **Key activity:** Plot graphs; write conclusion (what happened + scientific explanation); evaluation (what would improve reliability?); make a science-fair poster.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between your result and your explanation?" → The result says what happened; the explanation says why, using science.
- **Differentiation:** support: conclusion frame. / stretch: suggest a follow-up question.
- **Materials:** graph paper, poster paper.
- **Joy:** Poster making.

### Lesson 70 — Science fair
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain my investigation to visitors and answer their questions." (UK NC Y5 working scientifically — communicate)
- **Hook:** "Doors open — families and other classes arrive."
- **Key activity:** Present at stalls; visitors leave question cards; reflect on the year's science.
- **Check for understanding:** "What would you do differently next time and why?" → A sensible evaluation point.
- **Differentiation:** support: presentation prompt card. / stretch: act as a judge for another class's work.
- **Materials:** posters, equipment for demonstrations.
- **Joy:** The science fair itself.

**End-of-unit check:** A completed independent investigation with plan, results table, graph, conclusion and evaluation, presented at the science fair.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Plans a controlled investigation** — identifies independent, dependent and controlled variables and plans repeats. *See it:* the child's independent investigation plan in Unit 7.
2. **Records and interprets data** — tables with units, bar and line graphs, and identifies anomalous results. *See it:* a line graph of cooling or growth with a correct description of the pattern.
3. **Writes explained conclusions** — states what happened *and* why using scientific ideas, and evaluates. *See it:* parachute conclusion referring to air resistance.
4. **Materials** — compares and groups materials by several properties and justifies uses with evidence. *See it:* chooses and justifies materials for a lunchbox using test results.
5. **Changes** — separates mixtures by sieving, filtering, evaporating and magnets; classifies reversible and irreversible changes with evidence of new materials. *See it:* separates sand, salt and iron filings and explains each step.
6. **Earth and space** — explains day and night, years, seasons (tilt) and Moon phases with models; describes the Solar System. *See it:* uses a globe and lamp to explain why it is night in Japan when it is midday in Europe.
7. **Forces** — explains gravity (weight in newtons), air and water resistance, friction, and how levers, pulleys and gears help. *See it:* measures effort with and without a pulley and explains the difference.
8. **Life cycles and reproduction** — compares life cycles of mammals, amphibians, insects and birds; describes sexual and asexual reproduction in plants. *See it:* labels a flower and explains pollination vs fertilisation.
9. **Human development** — describes stages from birth to old age and reads a growth graph. *See it:* identifies growth spurts on a graph.

## Teacher guidance

**Common misconceptions (and fixes).**
- *"When sugar dissolves it disappears."* — Weigh before and after (mass is conserved) and evaporate to recover it.
- *"Dissolving is the same as melting."* — Melting needs heat and involves one substance; dissolving needs a solvent (water) and two substances.
- *"All metals are magnetic."* — Test aluminium and copper.
- *"Heavier objects fall faster."* — Show the Apollo 15 hammer and feather; air resistance explains differences on Earth.
- *"Weight and mass are the same."* — Mass in kg (amount of matter), weight in N (pull of gravity); use newton meters.
- *"The Sun moves around the Earth"* and *"seasons are caused by distance from the Sun"* — use globe-and-lamp models and the opposite seasons of the hemispheres.
- *"The Moon makes its own light"* and *"phases are Earth's shadow"* — model with a lamp and ball; eclipses and phases are different things.
- *"Plants only grow from seeds"* — grow runners, tubers and cuttings.
- *"Pollination and fertilisation are the same."* — separate the two steps clearly.
- *"All mammals give birth to live young."* — platypus and echidnas lay eggs.

**Working scientifically.** In every investigation, insist on the three variable types, a results table drawn *before* testing, repeats, and a conclusion that includes "because". Praise good evaluation ("our timing was hard because…") as much as "right" answers.

**Safety.** Low-voltage batteries only; warm (not hot) water; adults only for flames and heights; goggles for reactions and scratch tests; never look at the Sun; follow local rules for handling wildlife and return animals to their habitats; check allergies before food and plant work.

**International notes.** Seasons and the night sky differ by hemisphere and latitude — use your local observations (Southern Hemisphere schools see the Moon's phases "flipped" left–right). Swap in local plants, animals and materials.

**Sensitive content.** Human development and puberty (Unit 6) should follow the school's relationships and health education policy, with families informed and materials shared in advance.

**Vertical links.** Year 2 taught magnetic materials, friction on different surfaces, shadows through the day, day and night, and flower parts, pollination and dispersal; Year 3 taught states of matter, circuits, conductors and insulators, and classification. Year 4 only *retrieves* those (a few minutes each) and spends its lessons on what is new: strength and absorbency, cooling curves, dissolving, separating, reversible and irreversible change, which way Earth spins, the Sun as a star, weight in newtons, friction and mass, fertilisation, and controlled investigations. Year 5 builds on life cycles (inheritance and evolution), forces and measurement, and the full independent investigation.

**Cross-curricular links.** Maths (line graphs, averages, measures and time zones), English (explanation texts, *Cosmic* and *The Boy Who Harnessed the Wind*), geography (salt production, time zones, climate), computing (data loggers), art (Merian's illustrations, botanical drawing).
