> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Written to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Standards reference:

> Codes: **UK NC Y5** (England National Curriculum, Year 5 statements) and **CCSS** (US Common Core, Grades 4–5).

## Year overview

Year 4 is the year a child stops being a "calculator of small numbers" and becomes a **reasoner with large numbers, parts of wholes, and space**. It follows the Singapore / Maths-No-Problem mastery tradition: every new idea is met **concretely** (base-10, place-value counters, fraction strips, cubes, protractors), then **pictorially** (place-value charts, bar models, number lines, area models), then **abstractly**, and no idea gets only one lesson — each is introduced, practised, applied to a problem and retrieved later in the year.

**What Year 4 assumes (from Year 3):** place value to 10,000; all times tables to 12 × 12 recalled fluently with related division facts; column addition and subtraction of 4-digit numbers; column multiplication of 2–3-digit × 1-digit and short division with no or simple remainders; tenths and hundredths; equivalent fractions and fractions of amounts; area by counting squares; right, acute and obtuse angles; first-quadrant coordinates; bar charts and simple line graphs.

**How the year sequences.**
- **Term 1 — Number sense for big numbers.** Place value to 1,000,000 (with negatives and Roman numerals), written and mental addition/subtraction, then the *structure* of multiplication — multiples, factors, primes, squares, cubes — and the key idea that ×/÷ by 10, 100 and 1000 moves digits, not the decimal point. Fractions A (equivalence, comparing, mixed numbers) closes the term so fractions are revisited in Term 2 rather than crammed.
- **Term 2 — Calculation power.** Long multiplication (up to 4-digit × 2-digit), short division with remainders interpreted in context, then Fractions B (adding and subtracting with related denominators, multiplying a fraction by a whole number, fractions of amounts) and decimals to three places, including ×/÷ by 10/100/1000 with decimals. Statistics (line graphs, tables, timetables) applies the number work.
- **Term 3 — Connecting and measuring.** Percentages as a third way of writing a fraction or decimal; perimeter, area and volume; converting metric units and time; angles measured and drawn with a protractor and angle facts; properties of shape, 3-D shapes from nets, reflection and translation; a year-end consolidation and a maths festival.

Every unit contains **fluency** (quick recall and procedures), **reasoning** ("explain why", "which is wrong?") and **problem-solving** (bar-model word problems, NRICH-style investigations). Each term closes with **retrieval and assessment** lessons that mix earlier units (spaced practice), and a daily 5-minute "fluency starter" (times tables, number bonds to 1 and 100, doubling/halving) opens every lesson. Lessons run **30–40 minutes**.

**What Year 5 relies on from Year 4:** fluent place value to 1,000,000 and ×/÷ by powers of 10 (for numbers to 10,000,000 and decimal calculation); long multiplication with a secure placeholder (for long division by 2-digit divisors); equivalence and adding fractions with related denominators (for all four operations with any fractions); the decimal–fraction–percentage links (for converting FDP and ratio); protractor skill and angle facts (for angles in triangles and polygons); reflection/translation in the first quadrant (for all four quadrants).

## Unit 1 — Place value to 1,000,000

**Essential question:** How does our place-value system let ten digits write any number, however large — and what happens below zero?

**Key vocabulary:** digit, place value, ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten thousands, hundred thousands, million, partition, compare, order, round, estimate, negative number, interval, Roman numerals

### Lesson 1 — Counting in powers of ten: the ×10 place-value pattern
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain that each place is worth ten times the place to its right, up to a million." (UK NC Y5 place value; CCSS 4.NBT.1)
- **Hook:** Show a single centimetre cube: "If this is 1, how big would one million of them be?" Build 1 → 10 (rod) → 100 (flat) → 1000 (big cube) and imagine the next three.
- **Key activity:** Concrete: exchange 10 ones for a ten, 10 tens for a hundred, 10 hundreds for a thousand with base-10. Pictorial: extend a place-value chart to six columns grouped as thousands | ones. Abstract: write 1, 10, 100 … 1,000,000 and name each. I do the exchanges, we do the chart, you do: "What is 10 times bigger than 10,000? 10 times smaller than 100,000?"
- **Check for understanding:** "Kai says the 5 in 50,000 is worth 5 ten-thousands and also 50 thousands. Is he right? Explain." → Yes; 50,000 = 5 × 10,000 = 50 × 1000 — both describe the same value.
- **Differentiation:** support: keep the base-10 blocks in front of them and use a 4-column chart first, then add columns. / stretch: "How many hundreds are in one million? How many thousands?" (10,000; 1000) and explain using the ×10 pattern.
- **Materials:** base-10 blocks (units, rods, flats, 1000-cube), place-value chart to millions, mini-whiteboards.
- **Joy:** "Million estimation" — estimate how many rice grains fill a cup, then calculate how many cups would make a million (a class guess board).

### Lesson 2 — Reading and writing numbers to 1,000,000
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read, write and build any number up to 1,000,000 in digits and words." (UK NC Y5 place value; CCSS 4.NBT.2)
- **Hook:** Real populations: Reykjavík (about 140,000), Wellington (about 215,000) — "How do you say these out loud?"
- **Key activity:** Place-value counters on a 6-column chart; read the thousands group first, then the ones group ("two hundred and fifteen thousand, four hundred and six"). We do: dictation of numbers with zeros (305,040). You do: match digit cards to word cards, including traps like 30,500 vs 300,050.
- **Check for understanding:** "Write four hundred and six thousand and twenty. Why do you need zeros?" → 406,020; zeros hold the empty ten-thousands, hundreds and ones places.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters on the chart before writing digits; a comma card to place after the thousands group. / stretch: write numbers from clues ("6 digits, 7 hundred thousands, no tens, digit sum 20…").
- **Materials:** place-value counters (1, 10, 100, 1000, 10,000, 100,000), laminated chart, digit cards, world-city population cards.
- **Joy:** "Number dictation relay" — one child reads a big number, a partner builds it, then swap.

### Lesson 3 — Partitioning six-digit numbers flexibly
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can partition a six-digit number in more than one way." (UK NC Y5 place value; CCSS 4.NBT.2)
- **Hook:** "Is 245,000 made of 245 thousands or 2,450 hundreds? Could both be true?"
- **Key activity:** Standard partition with counters (200,000 + 40,000 + 5,000 + …), then non-standard (245,000 = 240,000 + 5,000 = 2,450 hundreds). Part-whole models and bar models. You do: complete missing-part problems (___ + 60,000 + 700 = 368,700).
- **Check for understanding:** "368,700 = 300,000 + ___ + 700. What goes in the gap, and why is it not 6?" → 68,000; the 6 and 8 are in the ten-thousands and thousands places.
- **Differentiation:** support: 4-digit numbers first with base-10, then move to counters. / stretch: find three different partitions of 500,500 using exactly three parts.
- **Materials:** place-value counters, part-whole mats, bar-model strips.
- **Joy:** "Partition puzzle cards" — race a partner to fill every gap in a mystery number.

### Lesson 4 — Numbers on a number line to 1,000,000
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can place and estimate large numbers on a number line." (UK NC Y5 place value; CCSS 4.NBT.2)
- **Hook:** A long rope across the room labelled 0 and 1,000,000: "Where would 750,000 go? Where is your town's population?"
- **Key activity:** Work out intervals (a 0–100,000 line split into 10 → steps of 10,000). Place numbers, then estimate unmarked points. You do: lines with different start/end values (340,000–350,000).
- **Check for understanding:** "On a line from 400,000 to 500,000 with 10 equal steps, an arrow is on the 6th mark. What is the number and how do you know?" → 460,000; each step is 10,000.
- **Differentiation:** support: lines with every interval labelled; count the jumps aloud. / stretch: lines with 4 or 5 intervals (steps of 25,000 or 20,000) and non-zero starts.
- **Materials:** skipping rope or masking-tape line, pegs and cards, printed number lines, population cards for five world cities.
- **Joy:** "Human number line" — each child holds a big-number card and the class sorts itself in silence.

### Lesson 5 — Comparing and ordering numbers to 1,000,000
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare and order numbers to a million and explain which place decides." (UK NC Y5 place value; CCSS 4.NBT.2)
- **Hook:** "Which country is bigger: one with 357,592 km² or one with 377,975 km²?" (Germany and Japan) — order the areas of five countries from an atlas.
- **Key activity:** Line numbers up by place value on a chart; compare from the greatest place; use < > =. Order sets including same-digit traps (120,400; 102,400; 120,040).
- **Check for understanding:** "Mia says 99,999 > 100,000 because 9 is the biggest digit. What would you tell her?" → 100,000 has more digits (a hundred-thousands digit), so it is greater; compare places, not digits.
- **Differentiation:** support: write numbers in the place-value chart before comparing. / stretch: "Use the digits 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9 once each to make the number closest to 500,000."
- **Materials:** place-value chart, digit cards 0–9, country-area fact cards (from an atlas).
- **Joy:** A trumps-style card game with country populations and areas — the bigger number wins the trick.

### Lesson 6 — Rounding to the nearest 10, 100, 1000
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can round any number to the nearest 10, 100 or 1000 and explain using a number line." (UK NC Y5 place value; CCSS 4.NBT.3)
- **Hook:** A newspaper says "5,000 fans"; the ticket office says 4,862. "Who is right?"
- **Key activity:** Find the two multiples the number sits between on a number line, find the halfway point, decide which is nearer; then the digit-to-the-right shortcut (5 or more rounds up — a halfway number is rounded up by convention).
- **Check for understanding:** "Round 4,950 to the nearest 100 and to the nearest 1000. Why do both answers make sense?" → 5,000 both times; 4,950 is halfway between 4,900 and 5,000 (rounds up) and nearer 5,000 than 4,000.
- **Differentiation:** support: always draw the number line with both multiples and the halfway mark. / stretch: "Which whole numbers round to 3,000 to the nearest 1000?" → 2,500 to 3,499.
- **Materials:** blank number lines, place-value chart, 0–9 dice.
- **Joy:** "Roll and round" — roll a 4-digit number, round it three ways, score points for the biggest rounded value.

### Lesson 7 — Rounding to 10,000 and 100,000
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can round numbers to the nearest 10,000 and 100,000 and choose a sensible degree of rounding." (UK NC Y5 place value; CCSS 4.NBT.3)
- **Hook:** Crowd at a football final: 83,746. "What would a reporter say — 80,000? 84,000? 83,700?"
- **Key activity:** Number lines with 10,000 and 100,000 intervals; identify the deciding digit; discuss which rounding fits which purpose (newspaper headline vs ticket count).
- **Check for understanding:** "Round 249,999 to the nearest 100,000. Leo says 300,000 because it has lots of 9s. Is he right?" → No — 200,000; the ten-thousands digit is 4, so it is below the halfway point 250,000.
- **Differentiation:** support: number line first, then circle the deciding digit. / stretch: "A number rounds to 350,000 to the nearest 10,000 and to 300,000 to the nearest 100,000. Give the smallest and largest possibilities." → 345,000 and 349,999.
- **Materials:** number lines, place-value charts, real headline cards (stadium crowds, city populations).
- **Joy:** Write a "newspaper headline" rounding a real statistic, then trade headlines to guess the exact figure.

### Lesson 8 — Negative numbers in context
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read, order and count through zero with negative numbers." (UK NC Y5 place value; CCSS 6.NS.5 preview)
- **Hook:** Temperatures on one January morning: Oslo −6 °C, Toronto −11 °C, Singapore 31 °C. "Which is colder, Oslo or Toronto — and how do you know?"
- **Key activity:** A vertical thermometer and a lift with basement floors (−1, −2, −3). Count forwards and backwards through zero in 1s, 2s, 5s. Order a set of temperatures.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is −8 smaller than −3, even though 8 is bigger than 3?" → −8 is further below zero; on the number line it is further left.
- **Differentiation:** support: a large vertical thermometer to point along. / stretch: sequences crossing zero with non-unit steps (15, 9, 3, −3, …) and finding the rule.
- **Materials:** demonstration thermometer (or printed thermometer scale), vertical number line −20 to 20, world weather cards.
- **Joy:** "Lift operator" — children move a paper lift up and down floors from −4 to 10 following instructions.

### Lesson 9 — Intervals across zero
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find the difference between a negative and a positive number." (UK NC Y5 place value)
- **Hook:** "At dawn in the Sahara it was −2 °C; by noon it was 34 °C. How much warmer?" (desert nights can drop below freezing in winter)
- **Key activity:** Count up to zero, then from zero: −2 → 0 is 2, 0 → 34 is 34, total 36. Model with hops on a number line. Apply to depths below sea level and heights above it.
- **Check for understanding:** "The temperature went from −5 °C to 3 °C. Sam says it rose 2 degrees. What mistake did he make?" → He subtracted 5 − 3; it rose 8 (5 to reach zero + 3 more).
- **Differentiation:** support: number line −10 to 10 with hops drawn. / stretch: a diver at −35 m rises 12 m, then 30 m; where is she and how far above/below sea level?
- **Materials:** number lines, thermometer cards, a cross-section picture of a harbour (sea level at 0).
- **Joy:** "Sea-level adventure" — plot a submarine, a diver, a seagull and a plane on one vertical line and ask partners distance questions.

### Lesson 10 — Roman numerals to 1000 (M)
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read and write Roman numerals to 1000 and years in Roman numerals." (UK NC Y5 place value)
- **Hook:** A photograph of a building's cornerstone or film credits with MCMXCVIII: "What year is this?"
- **Key activity:** Recap I, V, X, L, C; add D (500) and M (1000). Rules: add when a symbol is followed by an equal or smaller one; subtract when a smaller one comes before a larger (only IV, IX, XL, XC, CD, CM). Convert place by place: 1998 = 1000 + 900 + 90 + 8 = M CM XC VIII.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 99 written XCIX and not IC?" → Roman numerals are written place by place: 90 = XC and 9 = IX; I may only be subtracted from V or X.
- **Differentiation:** support: a symbol card strip and converting 3-digit numbers only. / stretch: find the year between 1000 and 2025 that needs the most Roman symbols. (1888 = MDCCCLXXXVIII, 13 symbols)
- **Materials:** Roman-numeral cards, clock face with Roman numerals, photos of dated buildings.
- **Joy:** "Code-breaker" — decode a secret message where each letter is labelled with a Roman numeral.

### Lesson 11 — Place value problem-solving
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve place value puzzles by reasoning systematically." (UK NC Y5 place value — solve problems)
- **Hook:** "I am a 6-digit number. My digits add to 9, I round to 300,000, and I have no tens. What could I be?"
- **Key activity:** Model a systematic list for a digit puzzle; pairs solve three NRICH-style puzzles with place-value counters, recording all solutions and explaining how they know they have them all.
- **Check for understanding:** "How do you know you have found every number that fits?" → By working systematically (e.g. fixing the largest digit first and listing in order).
- **Differentiation:** support: 4-digit puzzle version with counters. / stretch: create a puzzle with exactly one solution and test it on a partner.
- **Materials:** place-value counters, digit cards, puzzle cards (e.g. adapted from NRICH).
- **Joy:** "Mystery number" — write your own riddle and the class tries to crack it.

### Lesson 12 — Retrieval: place value, rounding and negatives
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show what I remember about place value, rounding, negative numbers and Roman numerals." (UK NC Y5 place value — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "Quiz show: four rounds, one lifeline."
- **Key activity:** Four short rounds (read/write, compare/round, negatives, Roman numerals); after each round, children self-mark and fix one error with a concrete model.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain the difference between rounding 45,500 to the nearest 1000 and to the nearest 10,000." → 46,000 (hundreds digit 5, round up) and 50,000 (thousands digit 5, round up); a different place decides each time.
- **Differentiation:** support: keep the place-value chart and a number line available. / stretch: a bonus "explain the error" question for each round.
- **Materials:** quiz cards, mini-whiteboards, place-value charts.
- **Joy:** Team quiz show with a "phone a friend" lifeline.

### Lesson 13 — Unit 1 assessment and review
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show mastery of place value to a million and set my next target." (UK NC Y5 place value — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Show what you know, then choose your own challenge."
- **Key activity:** A short low-stakes assessment (8 items including 2 reasoning questions), marked together; children then choose a "fix-it" or "deepen-it" task.
- **Check for understanding:** "Order −12, 5, −3, 0 and explain where −12 goes." → −12, −3, 0, 5; −12 is furthest below zero.
- **Differentiation:** support: fix-it tasks with base-10 and number lines. / stretch: deepen-it task — how many different 6-digit numbers round to 500,000 to the nearest 100,000? (100,000: from 450,000 to 549,999)
- **Materials:** assessment sheet, counters, number lines.
- **Joy:** Each child adds a "big number fact" (e.g. distance to the Moon, about 384,000 km) to a class wall.

**End-of-unit check:** Write 507,030 in words; round 384,650 to the nearest 10,000 (380,000) and 100,000 (400,000) and explain the deciding digit; find the difference between −7 °C and 12 °C (19 °C); write 1964 in Roman numerals (MCMLXIV).

## Unit 2 — Addition and subtraction

**Essential question:** How do I choose the best way to add or subtract — in my head, with jottings, or in columns — and how do I know my answer is sensible?

**Key vocabulary:** column method, exchange, regroup, inverse, estimate, round, mental strategy, compensation, bar model, multi-step, checking

### Lesson 14 — Mental addition and subtraction with large numbers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add and subtract large numbers mentally when only one or two places change." (UK NC Y5 addition & subtraction)
- **Hook:** "Can you do 345,000 + 30,000 faster than a calculator?" Race someone typing it.
- **Key activity:** Place-value counters show that only one column changes (345,000 + 30,000 = 375,000). Progress to crossing a boundary (375,000 + 30,000 = 405,000). You do: "Which of these can you do in your head?" sorting, then solve.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 482,500 − 200,000 easy to do in your head?" → Only the hundred-thousands digit changes (4 → 2): 282,500.
- **Differentiation:** support: model each with counters on the chart. / stretch: calculations that cross two boundaries (599,800 + 300).
- **Materials:** place-value counters and chart, mini-whiteboards, a calculator for the race.
- **Joy:** "Beat the calculator" race.

### Lesson 15 — Mental strategies: compensation and near multiples
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use rounding and adjusting to add and subtract near multiples mentally." (UK NC Y5 addition & subtraction)
- **Hook:** "12,450 + 9,999 — is there a shortcut?"
- **Key activity:** Show on a number line: +10,000 then −1. Practise with 99, 999, 1,999; subtraction: −2,998 = −3,000 then +2. Children explain why the adjustment direction differs for + and −.
- **Check for understanding:** "To work out 6,400 − 1,998, Ana did 6,400 − 2,000 = 4,400, then took away 2 more. What went wrong?" → She subtracted too much, so she must add 2 back: 4,402.
- **Differentiation:** support: 3-digit examples (345 + 99) with a number line. / stretch: invent three calculations where compensation is the best method and one where it is not.
- **Materials:** empty number lines, place-value counters, strategy sorting cards.
- **Joy:** "Strategy detective" — spot which method a mystery child used from their jottings.

### Lesson 16 — Column addition of numbers with more than 4 digits
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add numbers with up to 6 digits using the column method with exchanges." (UK NC Y5 addition & subtraction; CCSS 4.NBT.4)
- **Hook:** "Two stadiums: 54,786 and 38,947 fans. How many altogether?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: build both on a place-value grid with counters, combine, exchange 10 of any counter. Pictorial: draw the counters. Abstract: column method, recording exchanges below the line. Estimate first by rounding.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we write a small 1 under the tens column when the ones make 13?" → 13 ones = 1 ten and 3 ones; the ten is exchanged into the tens column.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters alongside every column calculation; squared paper to line up digits. / stretch: adding three numbers with different numbers of digits (245,680 + 9,875 + 67,009).
- **Materials:** place-value counters and grid, squared paper, stadium capacity cards.
- **Joy:** "Missing digit mysteries" — columns with smudged digits to reconstruct.

### Lesson 17 — Column subtraction with exchanges
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can subtract numbers with up to 6 digits using the column method, exchanging across several places." (UK NC Y5 addition & subtraction; CCSS 4.NBT.4)
- **Hook:** "Mount Everest is 8,849 m; Aconcagua is 6,961 m. How much taller is Everest?"
- **Key activity:** Build the first number only with counters; take away, exchanging one of a higher place for ten of the next. Record the exchange in columns. Special focus: exchanging across zeros (40,006 − 18,329).
- **Check for understanding:** "In 40,006 − 18,329 you need more ones. Where do you exchange from, and what does the number look like after?" → From the ten-thousands: 40,006 becomes 3 ten-thousands, 9 thousands, 9 hundreds, 9 tens, 16 ones; answer 21,677.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters for every exchange; start with 4-digit numbers. / stretch: "Make a subtraction with exactly three exchanges and an answer between 10,000 and 20,000."
- **Materials:** place-value counters and grid, squared paper, world-mountain height cards (Everest 8,849 m, Aconcagua 6,961 m, Kilimanjaro 5,895 m, Denali 6,190 m).
- **Joy:** "Mountain race" — calculate height differences between the highest peaks of each continent.

### Lesson 18 — Checking with inverse operations and estimation
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can check answers using estimation and the inverse operation." (UK NC Y5 addition & subtraction)
- **Hook:** Three "calculator" answers, one badly wrong. "Which is impossible — without calculating exactly?"
- **Key activity:** Round to estimate before calculating; after calculating, check subtraction with addition (and vice versa) using a bar model to show the relationship.
- **Check for understanding:** "Zara worked out 62,315 − 28,907 = 43,408. Use an estimate and an inverse to decide if she is right." → Estimate 62,000 − 29,000 = 33,000, so 43,408 is too big; 33,408 + 28,907 = 62,315, so the correct answer is 33,408.
- **Differentiation:** support: round to the nearest 1000 only; bar model provided. / stretch: find the digit error in a list of calculations and explain the likely cause.
- **Materials:** bar-model strips, mini-whiteboards, "suspicious calculator" cards.
- **Joy:** "Spot the fake" — be the checker who catches the broken robot calculator.

### Lesson 19 — Bar models for addition and subtraction problems
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw a bar model to decide whether to add or subtract in a word problem." (UK NC Y5 addition & subtraction — solve problems; CCSS 4.OA.3)
- **Hook:** "A library had some books. It gave away 1,450 and now has 6,275. How many did it start with?" Most children subtract — why is that wrong?
- **Key activity:** Part–whole and comparison bar models; label the known and unknown; decide the operation from the model, not keywords.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'gave away' not always a subtraction clue?" → The unknown is the whole (the start), so you add the parts: 6,275 + 1,450 = 7,725.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-drawn bars to label. / stretch: write a word problem that matches a given bar model.
- **Materials:** paper strips, coloured pencils, bar-model templates, Cuisenaire rods to model parts.
- **Joy:** "Model match" — pairs race to match six word problems to six bar models.

### Lesson 20 — Multi-step addition and subtraction problems
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve two- and three-step problems and explain each step." (UK NC Y5 addition & subtraction — multi-step problems)
- **Hook:** A charity bake sale fundraising thermometer: target 25,000, three days of takings — "Did they make it?"
- **Key activity:** Draw one bar model for the whole story; plan steps before calculating; choose mental or written methods for each step.
- **Check for understanding:** "A school needs 25,000 for a playground. It raised 8,750 and 11,395. How much more is needed? Explain the order of your steps." → Add the two amounts (20,145), then subtract from 25,000: 4,855.
- **Differentiation:** support: 2-step problems with the bar partly drawn. / stretch: problems with surplus information to spot and ignore.
- **Materials:** bar-model templates, play money or coin/note cards in a local currency, fundraising thermometer poster.
- **Joy:** Design your own fundraising thermometer for a real class cause and track pretend takings.

### Lesson 21 — Missing numbers and balancing equations
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find missing numbers in addition and subtraction equations, including balanced equations." (UK NC Y5 addition & subtraction)
- **Hook:** A pan balance: 45 + ☐ = 30 + 50. "What must be true about both sides?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: a balance scale with cubes. Pictorial: bar models for ☐ + 3,450 = 9,000. Abstract: equations with the unknown in different positions and on both sides (5,000 − ☐ = 2,000 + 1,500).
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 36 + 27 = 40 + 23 true? Explain without working out both sides." → Yes; 40 is 4 more than 36, and 23 is 4 less than 27, so the totals balance.
- **Differentiation:** support: balance scale and cubes for small numbers. / stretch: create balanced equations using compensation that a partner must verify.
- **Materials:** pan balance, interlocking cubes, bar-model templates.
- **Joy:** "Balance or topple?" — predict whether the equation balances, then test it on the scale.

### Lesson 22 — Choosing efficient methods
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose between mental, jotting and column methods and justify my choice." (UK NC Y5 addition & subtraction)
- **Hook:** "Would you use a column method for 10,000 − 1? Why do people sometimes do that?"
- **Key activity:** Sort 12 calculations into "in my head", "jotting", "columns"; discuss disagreements; solve 6 with the chosen method. Highlight counting-on for subtractions near a multiple (10,000 − 9,986).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the most efficient way to do 10,000 − 9,986, and why?" → Count on 14 from 9,986; the numbers are close together so a column method is slow and error-prone.
- **Differentiation:** support: work with 3- and 4-digit numbers. / stretch: design calculations that tempt people into the wrong method.
- **Materials:** calculation sorting cards, number lines, mini-whiteboards.
- **Joy:** "Method auction" — bid tokens on the method you think is quickest, then race to test it.

### Lesson 23 — Problem-solving: number puzzles with addition and subtraction
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve an addition/subtraction investigation by working systematically." (UK NC Y5 solve problems)
- **Hook:** Magic squares: "Every row, column and diagonal adds to the same total. Can you finish this one?"
- **Key activity:** Complete a 3×3 magic square with 4-digit numbers; then an investigation — arrange digits 1–9 into a 4-digit + 5-digit addition to get the largest or smallest total; record strategies.

- **Differentiation:** support: 3×3 magic square with 2-digit numbers; digit cards to move. / stretch: investigate why the magic total of a 3×3 magic square is always three times its centre number.
- **Materials:** digit cards 1–9, magic square templates, counters.
- **Joy:** Create a magic square for the class "puzzle corner".

### Lesson 24 — Unit 2 assessment and review
- **Duration:** 35 min

## Unit 3 — Multiplication and division A: number structure and powers of 10

**Essential question:** What are numbers made of when we multiply — and why does multiplying by 10, 100 or 1000 shift the digits?

**Key vocabulary:** multiple, factor, factor pair, common factor, common multiple, prime, composite, square number, cube number, squared (²), cubed (³), place value shift, inverse

### Lesson 25 — Multiples and common multiples
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find multiples of a number and common multiples of two numbers." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division; CCSS 4.OA.4)
- **Hook:** "One lighthouse flashes every 4 seconds, another every 6. When do they flash together?"
- **Key activity:** Cuisenaire rods: lay trains of 4 and 6 side by side until the ends meet (12, 24…). Shade multiples on a 100 square in two colours; find overlaps.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 36 a common multiple of 4 and 6? Is it the lowest? Explain." → Yes, 36 = 9 × 4 = 6 × 6, but the lowest is 12.
- **Differentiation:** support: rods and a 100 square for 2 and 3 first. / stretch: find the lowest common multiple of 3, 4 and 5 (60) and explain why.
- **Materials:** Cuisenaire rods, 100 squares, two colours of counters or pencils.
- **Joy:** "Lighthouse flashes" — two children clap on different beats; the class predicts when they clap together.

### Lesson 26 — Factors and factor pairs
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find all the factor pairs of a number systematically using arrays." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division; CCSS 4.OA.4)
- **Hook:** "A baker has 24 cupcakes. How many different rectangular boxes could she use?"
- **Key activity:** Build every rectangle array of 24 counters (1×24, 2×12, 3×8, 4×6). Record factor pairs in a "factor rainbow" in order; notice when to stop (when the pairs meet).
- **Check for understanding:** "How do you know you've found all the factors of 36?" → Test 1, 2, 3 … in order; after 6 × 6 the pairs repeat, so all are found: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters for numbers up to 24. / stretch: find a number under 100 with exactly 12 factors (60, 72, 84, 90 or 96).
- **Materials:** counters, squared paper, cupcake-box cards.
- **Joy:** "Factor rainbows" drawn with arcs linking each pair.

### Lesson 27 — Common factors
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find common factors of two numbers and use them to solve a sharing problem." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division)
- **Hook:** "18 apples and 24 oranges packed into identical bags with nothing left over. What is the largest number of bags?"
- **Key activity:** List factors of each number; use a Venn diagram to show common factors; choose the highest common factor for the bag problem.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 6 the answer and not 3?" → Both 3 and 6 are common factors, but 6 is the largest, so it makes the most bags (6 bags of 3 apples and 4 oranges).
- **Differentiation:** support: counters to test sharing. / stretch: find two numbers whose only common factor is 1 and explain.
- **Materials:** Venn diagram mats, counters, real or pictured fruit.
- **Joy:** "Party bag planner" — plan goody bags for a class party with no leftovers.

### Lesson 28 — Prime numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what a prime number is and find primes up to 100." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division; CCSS 4.OA.4)
- **Hook:** "Some numbers can only make one rectangle out of counters. Why are they special?"
- **Key activity:** Try arrays for 2–20 with counters; numbers with only a 1-row array are prime. Then the Sieve of Eratosthenes on a 100 square (named after the Greek mathematician in Alexandria). Stress: a prime has exactly two factors, so 1 is not prime; 2 is the only even prime.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 1 a prime number? Is 51? Explain both." → 1 is not prime (only one factor); 51 is not prime (3 × 17).
- **Differentiation:** support: counters and arrays to 30. / stretch: why must every prime greater than 3 be one more or one less than a multiple of 6?
- **Materials:** counters, 100 squares, coloured pencils for the sieve.
- **Joy:** "Prime sieve" — watch the primes appear as multiples are crossed out; secret-code messages using primes.

### Lesson 29 — Prime and composite numbers; prime factors
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can tell whether a number up to 100 is prime or composite and break a composite number into prime factors." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division — prime factors)
- **Hook:** "Every whole number above 1 is either a prime or built from primes, like Lego from bricks."
- **Key activity:** Factor trees for 12, 30, 36, 60; compare trees that start differently but end with the same primes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Two children draw different factor trees for 36. Can they end with different primes? Why?" → No; both end with 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 — every number has only one set of prime factors.
- **Differentiation:** support: numbers under 40 with a prime list on the desk. / stretch: find the prime factors of 360 and use them to list factors.
- **Materials:** 100 square with primes marked, factor-tree templates, interlocking cubes (coloured by prime).
- **Joy:** "Prime Lego" — build numbers from coloured cubes where each colour is a prime.

### Lesson 30 — Square numbers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise square numbers and use the notation ²." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division)
- **Hook:** "Which numbers of counters can make a perfect square?"
- **Key activity:** Build 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 … as squares with counters; see how each grows by the next odd number (1, +3, +5, +7). Notation: 5² = 5 × 5.
- **Check for understanding:** "Tom says 6² = 12. What mistake has he made?" → He did 6 × 2; 6² means 6 × 6 = 36.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters and squared paper up to 10². / stretch: explain why square numbers have an odd number of factors (one factor pairs with itself).
- **Materials:** counters, squared paper, square tiles.
- **Joy:** "Square garden" — design square vegetable plots and work out how many plants fit.

### Lesson 31 — Cube numbers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recognise cube numbers and use the notation ³." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division)
- **Hook:** "How many small cubes to build a cube twice as long in each direction?"
- **Key activity:** Build 1, 8, 27, 64 with multilink cubes; record n³ = n × n × n; compare 2³ with 3² and 2 × 3.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which is bigger, 3² or 2³? Show how you know." → 3² = 9, 2³ = 8, so 3² is bigger.
- **Differentiation:** support: build each cube physically. / stretch: "Is there a number that is both a square and a cube, apart from 1?" → 64 (8² and 4³).
- **Materials:** multilink or centimetre cubes (at least 64 per group), squared paper.
- **Joy:** Build the biggest cube the group's cubes allow and calculate how many more would make the next one.

### Lesson 32 — Multiplying by 10, 100 and 1000
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply whole numbers by 10, 100 and 1000 and explain the digit shift." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division; CCSS 5.NBT.2)
- **Hook:** "A bag holds 45 marbles. How many in 10 bags? 100 bags? 1000 bags?"
- **Key activity:** Place-value counters on a Gattegno chart and a place-value grid: multiplying by 10 moves every digit one place to the left; zero fills the empty place. Contrast with "adding a zero" (which fails for decimals, preview).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'just add a zero' a risky rule?" → It only works for whole numbers; the real reason is that each digit moves one place left and a zero holds the empty ones place.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters physically moved on the grid. / stretch: find the missing number: ☐ × 100 = 45,000; 360 × ☐ = 360,000.
- **Materials:** place-value grid with arrows, place-value counters, Gattegno chart.
- **Joy:** "Digit slide" — children stand as digits on a floor place-value grid and slide left when the teacher calls "×10".

### Lesson 33 — Dividing by 10, 100 and 1000
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide whole numbers by 10, 100 and 1000 and link it to multiplying." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division; CCSS 5.NBT.2)
- **Hook:** "A factory made 45,000 pencils packed in boxes of 1000. How many boxes?"
- **Key activity:** Digits move right on the place-value grid; use inverse to check. Include results with remainders ignored for now (only multiples of the divisor).
- **Check for understanding:** "What is 70,000 ÷ 100? How can you check with multiplication?" → 700; 700 × 100 = 70,000.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters on the grid for every example. / stretch: "What do you multiply 3,600 by to get 36? What do you divide by?" (show the relationship ×/÷ inverse).
- **Materials:** place-value grid, counters, factory packing cards.
- **Joy:** "Packing factory" — pack pretend items into boxes of 10, 100, 1000 and label the crates.

### Lesson 34 — Multiples of 10, 100 and 1000 in calculations
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use known facts and place value to calculate with multiples of 10, 100 and 1000 (e.g. 40 × 300)." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division)
- **Hook:** "If 4 × 3 = 12, what could 40 × 300 be?"
- **Key activity:** Break into known fact × powers of ten: 40 × 300 = 4 × 3 × 10 × 100 = 12,000. Use arrays of place-value counters to show. Also division: 2,400 ÷ 60 = 240 ÷ 6 = 40.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 50 × 400 = 20,000 and not 2,000?" → 5 × 4 = 20, then × 10 × 100 = × 1000; 20 × 1000 = 20,000 (the 0 from 20 is part of the fact).
- **Differentiation:** support: one multiple of 10 only (7 × 300). / stretch: find all pairs of multiples of 10 that multiply to 12,000.
- **Materials:** place-value counters, known-fact cards, place-value grid.
- **Joy:** "Fact family trees" — one fact grows 10 related big-number facts on a paper tree.

### Lesson 35 — Mental multiplication and division strategies
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply and divide mentally using doubling, halving and partitioning." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division — mental methods)
- **Hook:** "25 × 16 in your head in 5 seconds? There's a trick."
- **Key activity:** Double and halve (25 × 16 = 50 × 8 = 100 × 4 = 400); partition (7 × 36 = 7 × 30 + 7 × 6); ×5 as ×10 ÷ 2; ×9 as ×10 − 1. Area models show why partitioning works.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does doubling one number and halving the other keep the product the same?" → The array is rearranged (twice as many rows, half as many in each row), so the total stays the same.
- **Differentiation:** support: arrays on squared paper for doubling/halving. / stretch: use factors to multiply: 15 × 24 = 15 × 2 × 12 = 30 × 12 = 360.
- **Materials:** squared paper, counters, strategy cards.
- **Joy:** "Mental maths magician" — learn one trick and perform it for the class.

### Lesson 36 — Multiplication and division word problems with scaling
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve problems involving 'times as many' and scaling, using a bar model." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division — scaling; CCSS 4.OA.2)
- **Hook:** "The Burj Khalifa in Dubai is 828 m tall. About how many times taller is it than a 100 m tower?" Introduce comparison bar models.
- **Key activity:** Comparison bar models: "The lorry carries 4 times as much as the van, which carries 350 kg." Draw and solve forwards and backwards (given the bigger value, find the smaller).
- **Check for understanding:** "A tree is 6 times as tall as a sapling. The tree is 18 m. Priya says the sapling is 108 m. Explain her error." → She multiplied instead of dividing; the sapling is 18 ÷ 6 = 3 m.
- **Differentiation:** support: simple 'times as many' with pre-drawn bars. / stretch: two-step scaling (A is 3 times B, C is twice A; total 450 — find each).
- **Materials:** paper strips, Cuisenaire rods for comparison bars, bar-model templates.
- **Joy:** "Giant and tiny" — scale up a toy animal to find how big a giant version would be.

### Lesson 37 — Rates and correspondence problems
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve correspondence problems (how many combinations?) and simple rates." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division — solve problems)
- **Hook:** "A café offers 4 breads and 3 fillings. How many different sandwiches?"
- **Key activity:** Systematic listing, then tables/tree diagrams, then multiplication (4 × 3 = 12). Simple rates: "3 pens cost 45 — what do 7 cost?" via the cost of one.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do 4 breads and 3 fillings make 12 sandwiches, not 7?" → Each of the 4 breads can go with each of the 3 fillings — that's 4 groups of 3.
- **Differentiation:** support: cut-out bread and filling cards to physically pair. / stretch: add a third choice (2 drinks) — how many meals? (24)
- **Materials:** picture cards for breads/fillings, table templates, play money.
- **Joy:** "Design a café menu" and count every possible meal.

### Lesson 38 — Problem-solving: multiplication investigations
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use multiples, factors and primes to solve an investigation and explain my reasoning." (UK NC Y5 solve problems)
- **Hook:** "Can every even number between 4 and 50 be made by adding two primes?" (Goldbach's conjecture — unsolved for all numbers!)
- **Key activity:** Pairs split the even numbers, test with the prime list, record, and pool results. Discuss "we've checked many, but have we proved it for all?"
- **Check for understanding:** "Find two ways to make 30 from two primes. Why is 2 rarely used?" → 7 + 23, 11 + 19, 13 + 17; 2 + another prime would be even + odd = odd, not 30.
- **Differentiation:** support: numbers 4–20 with a prime list. / stretch: which even numbers under 50 have the most prime-pair ways?
- **Materials:** 100 square with primes highlighted, recording grid.
- **Joy:** Working on a real unsolved problem that famous mathematicians still study.

### Lesson 39 — Retrieval: multiplication facts and number structure
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall tables facts and number-structure vocabulary quickly and accurately." (UK NC Y5 multiplication & division — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "Times-tables bingo — but the calling numbers are clues like 'a square number bigger than 50'."
- **Key activity:** Tables fluency sprint (personal best), then clue bingo (primes, squares, multiples of 7 …), then a sorting task in a Carroll diagram.
- **Check for understanding:** "Place 49 in a Carroll diagram with 'square / not square' and 'odd / even'. Explain." → Square and odd (7 × 7).
- **Differentiation:** support: a multiplication grid for the sprint on the first attempt. / stretch: create your own Carroll diagram with a region that must stay empty and explain why.

### Lesson 40 — Unit 3 assessment and review

- **Hook:** "Number detective files — solve the case."
- **Key activity:** Mini-assessment (fluency + two reasoning items), partner marking, then fix-it/deepen-it tasks.

## Unit 4 — Fractions A: equivalence, comparing, mixed numbers

**Essential question:** How can the same amount have many different fraction names — and how do we compare fractions fairly?

**Key vocabulary:** numerator, denominator, unit fraction, non-unit fraction, equivalent, simplify, common denominator, proper fraction, improper fraction, mixed number, compare, order

### Lesson 41 — What a fraction means: recap with fraction walls
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what the numerator and denominator tell us and show fractions on strips and number lines." (UK NC Y5 fractions)
- **Hook:** "Is ¼ of a big pizza the same as ¼ of a small pizza?"
- **Key activity:** Fraction strips and a fraction wall; each strip represents the same whole; label unit and non-unit fractions; place them on a 0–1 number line.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is ⅕ smaller than ⅓, even though 5 is bigger than 3?" → The whole is split into more parts, so each part is smaller.
- **Differentiation:** support: fold paper strips to make halves, quarters, eighths. / stretch: place ⅗, ⅝ and ⅞ on the same number line and justify their order.
- **Materials:** fraction strips/wall, paper strips for folding, number lines 0–1.
- **Joy:** "Fraction wall build" — each group builds and colours a section of a giant class wall.

### Lesson 42 — Equivalent fractions with models
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find equivalent fractions using fraction walls and area models." (UK NC Y5 fractions — equivalence; CCSS 4.NF.1)
- **Hook:** Fold a paper strip in half, then half again and again: "The shaded amount never changed — but its name did!"
- **Key activity:** Paper folding and fraction walls to find ½ = 2/4 = 4/8; area model grids splitting both ways. Record families of equivalent fractions.
- **Check for understanding:** "Using a model, show why ⅔ = 6/9." → Split each third into 3 equal parts; the 2 shaded thirds become 6 of 9 parts.
- **Differentiation:** support: halves/quarters/eighths families with physical folding. / stretch: find a fraction equivalent to ¾ with a denominator of 100 and explain the link to hundredths.
- **Materials:** paper strips, fraction walls, squared paper for area models.
- **Joy:** "Fraction family trees" — grow as many branches of equivalent fractions as possible in 5 minutes.

### Lesson 43 — Equivalent fractions by multiplying and dividing
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find equivalent fractions by multiplying or dividing numerator and denominator by the same number." (UK NC Y5 fractions — equivalence)
- **Hook:** "Is there a quicker way than drawing every time?"
- **Key activity:** Link the model to the rule: ×3 on both parts = splitting each part into 3. Show why adding the same number does not work (1/2 → 2/3 is not equal).
- **Check for understanding:** "Jin says 2/5 = 3/6 because he added 1 to both. Show with a model why he's wrong." → 2/5 = 0.4 of a strip, 3/6 = ½; the strips don't match — only multiplying/dividing by the same number keeps the value.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction wall stays in view to check each answer. / stretch: missing-number equivalences (3/☐ = 12/20; ☐/7 = 15/35).
- **Materials:** fraction wall, multiplication grid, fraction strips.
- **Joy:** "Equivalence snap" — a card game snapping pairs of equal fractions.

### Lesson 44 — Simplifying fractions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can simplify fractions using common factors." (UK NC Y5 fractions — equivalence)
- **Hook:** "18/24 of the class brought lunch. Can we say it in a simpler way?"
- **Key activity:** Use common factors (from Unit 3) to divide numerator and denominator; the highest common factor simplifies in one step. Check with a fraction wall.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 12/18 fully simplified if you divide by 2 to get 6/9? Why or why not?" → No; 6 and 9 share the factor 3, so 6/9 = 2/3. Using the HCF 6 goes straight there.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction wall and factor lists provided. / stretch: find all fractions with a denominator of 24 that simplify to a unit fraction.
- **Materials:** fraction walls, factor-pair cards, counters.
- **Joy:** "Fraction shrink ray" — "zap" fractions to their simplest form on a game board.

### Lesson 45 — Fractions greater than 1: improper fractions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can represent improper fractions with models and on a number line." (UK NC Y5 fractions — improper fractions)
- **Hook:** "I ate 5 quarters of pizza. Is that possible?"
- **Key activity:** Paper circles cut into quarters; collect 5 quarters, rebuild one whole plus a quarter. Number line 0–3 in quarters. Name improper fractions (numerator ≥ denominator).
- **Check for understanding:** "Where does 7/4 go on a number line? Explain using wholes." → Between 1 and 2; 4/4 is one whole and 3/4 more takes you to 1¾.
- **Differentiation:** support: physical fraction circles for halves and quarters. / stretch: place 11/6, 7/3 and 9/4 on one number line.
- **Materials:** fraction circles, number lines 0–3, paper plates to cut.
- **Joy:** "Pizza party maths" — share paper pizzas to build improper fractions.

### Lesson 46 — Mixed numbers and converting both ways
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can convert between mixed numbers and improper fractions and explain why." (UK NC Y5 fractions — mixed numbers; CCSS 4.NF.3)
- **Hook:** "2¾ bottles of juice — how many quarter-bottles is that?"
- **Key activity:** Model 2¾ with fraction circles: 2 wholes = 8 quarters, + 3 = 11/4. Reverse: 17/5 → how many fives in 17? 3 r2 → 3⅖. Connect to division.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain why 3⅖ = 17/5 using what you know about division." → 3 wholes = 15 fifths, plus 2 fifths = 17 fifths; 17 ÷ 5 = 3 remainder 2.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction circles and number lines for each conversion. / stretch: which is greater: 23/6 or 3⅚? (equal — explain why)
- **Materials:** fraction circles, fraction strips, number lines.
- **Joy:** "Juice bar" — fill paper bottles with quarter-bottle cut-outs to match orders.

### Lesson 47 — Comparing fractions with the same denominator or numerator
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare fractions with the same denominator or the same numerator and explain why." (UK NC Y5 fractions — compare; CCSS 4.NF.2)
- **Hook:** "Would you rather have 3/8 or 3/5 of a chocolate bar?"
- **Key activity:** Same denominator: compare numerators (same-size parts). Same numerator: compare denominators (fewer parts = bigger parts). Prove with strips.
- **Check for understanding:** "Without drawing, which is greater: 4/9 or 4/7? Explain." → 4/7, because sevenths are bigger than ninths and there are 4 of each.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction strips for every comparison. / stretch: order 5/6, 5/8, 5/12, 5/5 and explain.
- **Materials:** fraction strips, chocolate-bar images divided into parts.
- **Joy:** "Would you rather?" fraction debates with votes.

### Lesson 48 — Comparing and ordering fractions with related denominators
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare and order fractions whose denominators are multiples of the same number." (UK NC Y5 fractions — compare and order)
- **Hook:** Recipe card: ⅔ cup of rice or 5/6 cup of water — which is more?
- **Key activity:** Convert to a common denominator (⅔ = 4/6); fraction walls to confirm. Order sets like ½, 3/8, 5/16, ¾ using a common denominator.
- **Check for understanding:** "Order 3/4, 5/8, 7/16 and explain the method." → 7/16, 5/8 (10/16), 3/4 (12/16); convert to sixteenths.

- **Materials:** fraction walls, measuring cups, recipe cards.
- **Joy:** "Recipe detective" — decide which of two cake recipes uses more sugar.

### Lesson 49 — Fractions on a number line including mixed numbers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can place fractions and mixed numbers on number lines and count in fractions beyond 1." (UK NC Y5 fractions — count forwards/backwards)
- **Hook:** A running track 0–3 km with markers every ¼ km: "Where is 2¼? 9/4?"
- **Key activity:** Count forwards and backwards in fractions (⅓, ⅔, 1, 1⅓ …) aloud and on lines; place mixed numbers and improper fractions on the same line.

### Lesson 50 — Fractions problem-solving and reasoning
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use equivalence and comparison to solve fraction puzzles." (UK NC Y5 fractions — solve problems)

### Lesson 51 — Retrieval: fractions
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall and use fraction facts from this unit fluently." (UK NC Y5 fractions — retrieval)
- **Hook:** "Fraction dominoes — every match must be exactly equal."
- **Key activity:** Dominoes (equivalences, mixed/improper), a quick-fire 10, and one worked-error analysis.

## Unit 6 — Multiplication and division B: long multiplication and short division

### Lesson 57 — Multiplying 4-digit by 1-digit numbers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply a 4-digit number by a 1-digit number using the column method." (UK NC Y5 multiplication — formal written method; CCSS 4.NBT.5)
- **Hook:** "A cruise ship carries 2,347 passengers on each trip. How many in 6 trips?"
- **Key activity:** Place-value counters in rows (6 rows of 2,347), exchange groups of ten; then the column method with exchanges recorded; estimate first (2,000 × 6 = 12,000 and 2,500 × 6 = 15,000).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 2,347 × 6 = 1,482 impossible without calculating?" → An estimate says it must be between 12,000 and 15,000; answer 14,082.
- **Differentiation:** support: 3-digit × 1-digit with counters. / stretch: missing digits in a 4-digit × 1-digit column multiplication.
- **Materials:** place-value counters, place-value grid, squared paper.
- **Joy:** "Cruise planner" — work out passengers across a season of trips.

### Lesson 58 — Area model for 2-digit × 2-digit
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply two 2-digit numbers by splitting into an area model." (UK NC Y5 multiplication; CCSS 4.NBT.5)
- **Hook:** "A garden is 23 m by 14 m. How many 1 m² turf squares?"
- **Key activity:** Build 23 × 14 with base-10 (rods and units) as a rectangle; split into 20 × 10, 20 × 4, 3 × 10, 3 × 4; record in a grid; add the four partial products (200 + 80 + 30 + 12 = 322).
- **Check for understanding:** "Mo got 23 × 14 = 92 by doing 20 × 4 + 3 × 4. What did he forget?" → The 10 part: 20 × 10 and 3 × 10 are missing; the full answer is 322.
- **Differentiation:** support: base-10 rectangle for every calculation. / stretch: find the missing side: an area model shows 400, 120, 60 and 18 — what are the two numbers? (23 × 26 = 598)
- **Materials:** base-10 blocks, squared paper, area-model templates.
- **Joy:** "Garden designer" — design a rectangular garden and calculate the turf needed.

### Lesson 59 — From area model to long multiplication (2-digit × 2-digit)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply two 2-digit numbers using long multiplication, explaining the placeholder zero." (UK NC Y5 multiplication — long multiplication)
- **Hook:** Put the area model and the column layout side by side: "Where is each part hiding?"
- **Key activity:** 24 × 16: row 1 = 24 × 6 = 144; row 2 = 24 × 10 = 240 (zero placeholder because we multiply by 1 ten); add = 384. Colour-code rows to match area-model parts.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we write a 0 in the ones column before multiplying by the tens digit?" → We are multiplying by 10 (a ten), so every digit moves one place left; the 0 holds the ones place.
- **Differentiation:** support: grid method next to each column calculation; place-value headings on paper. / stretch: 2-digit × 2-digit where both rows need carrying (78 × 69 = 5,382).
- **Materials:** squared paper with place-value headings, coloured pencils, base-10.
- **Joy:** "Spot the twin" — match each long multiplication row to its area-model rectangle.

### Lesson 60 — Long multiplication: 3-digit × 2-digit
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply a 3-digit number by a 2-digit number using long multiplication." (UK NC Y5 multiplication — long multiplication)
- **Hook:** "A theatre has 245 seats. It sells out 32 nights in a row. How many tickets?"
- **Key activity:** Estimate (250 × 30 = 7,500); 245 × 2 = 490; 245 × 30 = 7,350; total 7,840. Practise with increasing carries.
- **Check for understanding:** "Lena's second row for 245 × 32 is 735. What went wrong?" → She multiplied by 3 not 30 (forgot the placeholder); it should be 7,350.
- **Differentiation:** support: grid method as a bridge; 3-digit × multiples of 10 first. / stretch: "Choose digits for ☐☐☐ × ☐☐ to make a product as close to 10,000 as possible."
- **Materials:** squared paper, base-10 for reference, theatre seating plan.
- **Joy:** "Box office manager" — calculate ticket sales for a real-looking theatre programme.

### Lesson 61 — Long multiplication: 4-digit × 2-digit
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply a 4-digit number by a 2-digit number and check with an estimate." (UK NC Y5 multiplication — long multiplication)
- **Hook:** "A bakery makes 1,265 loaves a day. How many in the 28 days of February (in a non-leap year)?"

- **Check for understanding:** "Why is your estimate 39,000 bigger than the real answer 35,420?" → Both numbers were rounded up (1,265 → 1,300 and 28 → 30).

### Lesson 62 — Long multiplication in problems

### Lesson 64 — Division: sharing and grouping with place-value counters

### Lesson 65 — Short division of 4-digit numbers by 1-digit
- **Duration:** 40 min

- **Hook:** "A marathon's 5,628 runners are split equally into 4 start waves. How many per wave?"

## Unit 7 — Fractions B: add, subtract, multiply, fractions of amounts

### Lesson 74 — Adding and subtracting fractions with the same denominator
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add and subtract fractions with the same denominator, including answers greater than 1." (UK NC Y5 fractions — add/subtract; CCSS 4.NF.3)
- **Hook:** "3/5 of a cake plus 4/5 of another cake — do we have more than one cake?"
- **Key activity:** Fraction strips: add numerators, keep the denominator because the size of the parts does not change; convert results above 1 to mixed numbers (7/5 = 1⅖).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 3/5 + 4/5 not 7/10?" → The parts are still fifths; you have 7 fifths, not tenths. 7/5 = 1⅖.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction strips and circles. / stretch: find three pairs of fifths that add to 1⅕.

### Lesson 75 — Adding fractions with related denominators
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add fractions where one denominator is a multiple of the other." (UK NC Y5 fractions — add with related denominators; CCSS 5.NF.1)
- **Hook:** "½ litre of juice + ¼ litre of juice — how much in the jug?"
- **Key activity:** Concrete: measuring jug and fraction strips — ½ = 2/4 — "make the pieces the same size". Pictorial: bar split and re-split. Abstract: convert to the larger denominator, then add.
- **Check for understanding:** "Kofi says ½ + ¼ = 2/6. Use a model to show why that's impossible." → 2/6 = ⅓, which is less than ½ on its own; the answer is 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4.
- **Differentiation:** support: halves, quarters and eighths with strips. / stretch: 2/3 + 5/12 + 1/6 (8/12 + 5/12 + 2/12 = 15/12 = 1¼).

### Lesson 76 — Subtracting fractions with related denominators
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can subtract fractions where one denominator is a multiple of the other." (UK NC Y5 fractions — subtract; CCSS 5.NF.1)
- **Hook:** "A ribbon is ⅚ m long. I cut off ⅓ m. How much is left?"
- **Key activity:** Convert ⅓ = 2/6; subtract; simplify (3/6 = ½). Bar models for take-away and difference.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do you need to change ⅓ into sixths before subtracting?" → You can only subtract same-sized parts; ⅓ = 2/6, so ⅚ − 2/6 = 3/6 = ½.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction wall to find equivalents. / stretch: 7/8 − ☐ = 5/16 (9/16).
- **Materials:** ribbon and metre stick, fraction wall, bar templates.
- **Joy:** "Ribbon workshop" — cut real ribbon lengths to match calculations.

### Lesson 77 — Adding fractions to make mixed numbers
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add fractions that total more than 1 and write the answer as a mixed number." (UK NC Y5 fractions)
- **Hook:** "¾ of an hour of football plus ⅝ of an hour of swimming — was that more than an hour of sport?"
- **Key activity:** Convert, add, then re-group wholes: ¾ + ⅝ = 6/8 + 5/8 = 11/8 = 1⅜. Clock faces for quarter-hours; strips for eighths.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is ¾ + ⅝ more or less than 1? Explain before calculating." → More; ¾ needs only ¼ (2/8) to make 1, and ⅝ is more than 2/8.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction strips placed end to end against a 'one whole' strip. / stretch: "Find two fractions with denominators 3 and 6 that add to exactly 1½."
- **Materials:** fraction strips, clock faces, number lines 0–2.
- **Joy:** "Sports diary" — total a week's activity in fractions of hours.

### Lesson 78 — Adding mixed numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add mixed numbers by adding wholes and fractions." (UK NC Y5 fractions — mixed numbers; CCSS 4.NF.3c)
- **Hook:** "A plant grew 2⅓ cm one week and 1⅚ cm the next. How much altogether?"
- **Key activity:** Partition: wholes (2 + 1 = 3), fractions (⅓ + ⅚ = 2/6 + 5/6 = 7/6 = 1⅙), recombine = 4⅙. Model with fraction circles; alternative: convert to improper fractions and compare methods.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is the answer 4⅙ and not 3 7/6?" → 7/6 contains a whole, so it must be regrouped: 3 + 1⅙ = 4⅙ (3 7/6 is the same value but not a proper mixed number).
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction circles and a number line. / stretch: add three mixed numbers with denominators 2, 4 and 8.
- **Materials:** fraction circles, number lines, growth chart.
- **Joy:** "Beanstalk race" — record and add weekly growth of paper beanstalks.

### Lesson 79 — Subtracting mixed numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can subtract mixed numbers, exchanging a whole when needed." (UK NC Y5 fractions — mixed numbers; CCSS 4.NF.3c)
- **Hook:** "3¼ pizzas at the party; 1¾ were eaten. How much is left?"
- **Key activity:** Model with fraction circles: can't take ¾ from ¼, so exchange one whole for 4/4 (3¼ = 2 5/4); subtract = 1 2/4 = 1½. Compare with counting on along a number line.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do you need to exchange a whole in 3¼ − 1¾?" → ¼ is less than ¾; breaking a whole into quarters gives 5/4 to subtract from.
- **Differentiation:** support: fraction circles for every step. / stretch: 5 − 2⅜ solved two ways (exchange and counting on).
- **Materials:** fraction circles, paper pizzas, number lines.
- **Joy:** "Leftover pizza" — work out what's left after each friend's order.

### Lesson 80 — Multiplying a unit fraction by a whole number
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply a unit fraction by a whole number and link it to repeated addition." (UK NC Y5 fractions — multiply by whole numbers; CCSS 4.NF.4a)
- **Hook:** "Each child drinks ⅕ of a litre of milk. How much do 7 children drink?"
- **Key activity:** Repeated addition on strips (⅕ + ⅕ + … = 7/5), number line jumps, then the rule: multiply the numerator by the whole number, keep the denominator.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does ⅕ × 7 = 7/5 and not 7/35?" → It is 7 lots of one fifth; the parts are still fifths.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters as fifths on a strip. / stretch: which whole number × ⅛ gives 2¼? (18)
- **Materials:** fraction strips, number lines, measuring jug.
- **Joy:** "Milk-bottle maths" — fill paper bottles with fifths.

### Lesson 81 — Multiplying a non-unit fraction by a whole number
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply a non-unit fraction by a whole number and write the answer as a mixed number." (UK NC Y5 fractions — multiply by whole numbers; CCSS 4.NF.4b)
- **Hook:** "A recipe needs ⅔ cup of flour. We're making 4 batches. How much flour?"
- **Key activity:** Model 4 × ⅔ as four bars of ⅔ = 8/3 = 2⅔; connect with 4 × 2 thirds = 8 thirds.
- **Check for understanding:** "Liv says ⅔ × 4 = 8/12. What did she do wrong?" → She multiplied the denominator too; 4 lots of 2 thirds is 8 thirds = 2⅔.
- **Differentiation:** support: bar models for every question. / stretch: find the missing whole number: ⅗ × ☐ = 4⅕ (7).
- **Materials:** fraction strips, measuring cups, recipe cards.
- **Joy:** Scale up a real recipe (flatbread or pancakes) for a class tasting.

### Lesson 82 — Multiplying mixed numbers by whole numbers
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply a mixed number by a whole number by partitioning." (UK NC Y5 fractions — multiply mixed numbers)
- **Hook:** "Each lap of the park is 1¾ km. How far are 3 laps?"
- **Key activity:** Partition: 3 × 1 = 3 and 3 × ¾ = 9/4 = 2¼; total 5¼. Alternative: 1¾ = 7/4, × 3 = 21/4 = 5¼. Compare.
- **Check for understanding:** "Show two ways to calculate 3 × 1¾ and prove they give the same answer." → 3 + 2¼ = 5¼ and 21/4 = 5¼.
- **Differentiation:** support: number line with ¼ steps. / stretch: 4 × 2⅝ (10½).
- **Materials:** number lines, fraction strips, park map.
- **Joy:** "Fun run" — plan laps to reach a distance target.

### Lesson 83 — Fractions of amounts: unit fractions
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find a unit fraction of an amount using division and a bar model." (UK NC Y5 fractions of amounts)
- **Hook:** "⅙ of the 348 people at the fair won a prize. How many winners?"
- **Key activity:** Bar model split into 6; divide 348 ÷ 6 = 58. Link with short division for larger amounts; counters for small ones.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do we divide by the denominator to find a unit fraction?" → The denominator tells how many equal parts the whole is split into; one part is the whole ÷ that number.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters and amounts under 60. / stretch: ⅛ of a length in cm giving a decimal answer (⅛ of 100 cm = 12.5 cm).
- **Materials:** counters, bar templates, squared paper.
- **Joy:** "Prize draw" — work out prizes for different fairs.

### Lesson 84 — Fractions of amounts: non-unit fractions
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find a non-unit fraction of an amount." (UK NC Y5 fractions of amounts)
- **Hook:** "⅗ of a 2,400 m race has been run. How far to go?"
- **Key activity:** Bar model in 5 parts: one part 480; three parts 1,440; remaining 960. Practise ÷ by denominator, × by numerator.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is ⅗ of 2,400 not 2,400 ÷ 3 × 5?" → That finds 5 thirds (bigger than the whole); ⅗ means split into 5, take 3: 2,400 ÷ 5 × 3 = 1,440.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters and totals that are multiples of 10. / stretch: compare ⅗ of 450 and ⅝ of 400 (270 vs 250).
- **Materials:** bar templates, counters, running-track picture.
- **Joy:** "Race commentator" — call out distances run at different fractions of a race.

### Lesson 85 — Finding the whole from a part
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can find the whole when I know a fraction of it." (UK NC Y5 fractions — solve problems)
- **Hook:** "¾ of the class — that's 21 children — went on the trip. How many children in the class?"
- **Key activity:** Bar model: 3 parts = 21, so 1 part = 7, 4 parts = 28. Practise backwards reasoning with several fractions.
- **Check for understanding:** "⅖ of a number is 30. What is the number? Explain using a bar." → 2 parts = 30, 1 part = 15, 5 parts = 75.
- **Differentiation:** support: unit-fraction versions (⅕ is 12). / stretch: "After spending ⅝ of her money, Ada had 45 left. How much did she start with?" (120)

### Lesson 86 — Fraction word problems

- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve multi-step fraction problems using bar models." (UK NC Y5 fractions — solve problems)

- **Check for understanding:** "Why is ¼ of the rest not ¼ of 360?" → After Monday only 240 remain; ¼ of 240 = 60, leaving 180.

### Lesson 87 — Retrieval: fraction calculations

## Unit 8 — Decimals to three places

**Essential question:** What happens to place value to the right of the decimal point — and how do decimals connect to fractions and measures?

**Key vocabulary:** decimal point, tenth, hundredth, thousandth, decimal place, equivalent, round, compare, order, exchange, place-value shift

### Lesson 89 — Tenths and hundredths recap
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can represent tenths and hundredths as fractions and decimals on place-value charts." (UK NC Y5 decimals; CCSS 4.NF.6)
- **Hook:** "A 100 m sprint was won in 9.58 seconds (Usain Bolt's 2009 world record). What do the 5 and the 8 mean?"
- **Key activity:** Hundred squares and decimal place-value counters (0.1, 0.01); write 0.58 = 5 tenths + 8 hundredths = 58/100.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is 0.58 made of 58 hundredths or 5 tenths and 8 hundredths? Explain." → Both — 5 tenths = 50 hundredths, plus 8 = 58 hundredths.
- **Differentiation:** support: hundred squares shaded for each number. / stretch: represent 1.07 three different ways.
- **Materials:** hundred squares, decimal place-value counters, place-value chart with decimal columns.
- **Joy:** "Sprint timing" — time 5-second challenges with a stopwatch reading hundredths.

### Lesson 90 — Thousandths
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain thousandths and read and write decimals with three decimal places." (UK NC Y5 decimals — thousandths; CCSS 5.NBT.3)
- **Hook:** "A litre bottle and a millilitre spoon — how many spoonfuls fill the bottle?" (1000 — so 1 ml is 0.001 l)
- **Key activity:** Divide one hundredth of a hundred square into 10 (a thousand grid); place-value counters 0.001; build 0.125 and 2.307. Link 1 m = 1000 mm.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the value of the 7 in 2.307? How do you know?" → 7 thousandths (0.007); it is in the third place after the decimal point.
- **Differentiation:** support: link to money and metres first, then thousandths. / stretch: write 0.305 as a fraction and simplify it (305/1000 = 61/200).
- **Materials:** thousand grid (or base-10 with the big cube as 1), decimal counters, measuring jug, metre stick with mm.
- **Joy:** "Spoon challenge" — how many millilitre spoons in a cup? Estimate, then calculate.

### Lesson 91 — Decimals as fractions with denominators 10, 100, 1000
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can convert between decimals and fractions with denominators 10, 100 and 1000." (UK NC Y5 decimals; CCSS 4.NF.6)
- **Hook:** "Which is more: 3/10 or 0.03? Guess, then prove."
- **Key activity:** Place-value chart matching: 0.3 = 3/10 = 30/100 = 300/1000; 0.03 = 3/100; 0.003 = 3/1000. Fraction–decimal matching cards.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 0.3 bigger than 0.03?" → 0.3 is 3 tenths (30 hundredths); 0.03 is 3 hundredths.
- **Differentiation:** support: hundred squares for tenths and hundredths. / stretch: convert 7/20, 3/25 and 9/50 to decimals by making denominators 100 (0.35, 0.12, 0.18).
- **Materials:** matching cards, place-value chart, hundred squares.
- **Joy:** "Decimal pairs" memory game.

### Lesson 92 — Common fraction–decimal equivalents
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall and explain decimal equivalents of ½, ¼, ¾, ⅕ and ⅛." (UK NC Y5 decimals — recall equivalents)
- **Hook:** "Why do shops write 0.25 kg and 0.5 kg instead of ¼ kg and ½ kg?"
- **Key activity:** Derive on a hundred square: ½ = 50/100 = 0.5; ¼ = 0.25; ¾ = 0.75; ⅕ = 0.2; ⅛ = 0.125 (half of 0.25). Learn with a matching game.
- **Check for understanding:** "How can you use ¼ = 0.25 to find ⅛ as a decimal?" → ⅛ is half of ¼, so half of 0.25 = 0.125.
- **Differentiation:** support: halves, quarters, fifths only with hundred squares. / stretch: find ⅜ and ⅝ as decimals (0.375 and 0.625).
- **Materials:** hundred squares, kitchen scales with decimal kg readout, packaging labels.
- **Joy:** "Supermarket labels" — convert real packaging masses between fractions and decimals.

### Lesson 93 — Comparing and ordering decimals
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can compare and order numbers with up to 3 decimal places." (UK NC Y5 decimals — compare; CCSS 5.NBT.3b)
- **Hook:** Long-jump results: 4.5 m, 4.48 m, 4.506 m. "Who won?"
- **Key activity:** Line up decimal points on a place-value chart; add placeholder zeros to equalise places (4.500, 4.480, 4.506); compare from the left.
- **Check for understanding:** "Ella says 0.45 is bigger than 0.5 because 45 is bigger than 5. Explain her mistake." → 0.5 = 0.50 = 50 hundredths, which is more than 45 hundredths.
- **Differentiation:** support: place-value chart and decimal counters. / stretch: find a decimal between 0.34 and 0.35 (e.g. 0.345) and explain.
- **Materials:** place-value chart, decimal counters, measuring tape, results cards.
- **Joy:** Real standing-long-jump contest measured to the nearest centimetre, then ordered.

### Lesson 94 — Rounding decimals
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can round decimals with two places to the nearest whole number and to one decimal place." (UK NC Y5 decimals — rounding; CCSS 5.NBT.4)
- **Hook:** "A parcel weighs 2.64 kg. The post office charges by whole kilograms. What does it round to?"
- **Key activity:** Number lines between whole numbers and between tenths; the deciding digit; model on scales and rulers.
- **Check for understanding:** "Round 3.95 to one decimal place. Why is the answer 4.0 and not 3.10?" → 3.95 is halfway between 3.9 and 4.0, so it rounds up to 4.0; 3.10 is smaller than 3.95.
- **Differentiation:** support: number lines always drawn. / stretch: what numbers with 2 decimal places round to 7.3? (7.25 to 7.34)
- **Materials:** number lines, kitchen scales, rulers with mm.
- **Joy:** "Parcel post" — weigh real objects and round for the 'postage'.

### Lesson 95 — Decimal sequences and counting
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can count forwards and backwards in decimal steps, crossing the whole-number boundary." (UK NC Y5 decimals)
- **Hook:** "Count up in 0.1s from 2.7. Most people say '2.10' next — are they right?"
- **Key activity:** Count with decimal counters, exchanging 10 tenths for 1; sequences with 0.01 and 0.05 steps; complete sequences.
- **Check for understanding:** "What comes after 2.9 when counting in tenths? Why not 2.10?" → 3.0; 10 tenths make 1 whole, so 2.9 + 0.1 = 3; 2.10 is the same as 2.1.
- **Differentiation:** support: counting with money in tenths/hundredths (coins of 10 and 1 cents). / stretch: find the missing terms in 1.47, ☐, 1.61, ☐, 1.75.
- **Materials:** decimal counters, number lines, coins.
- **Joy:** "Decimal countdown" — rocket launch counting backwards in 0.25 steps.

### Lesson 96 — Adding decimals with the same number of places
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add decimals with up to 2 decimal places using a column method." (UK NC Y5 decimals — add; CCSS 5.NBT.7)
- **Hook:** "A shopping basket: 3.45 + 2.80 + 1.25. Can we afford it with 8.00?"
- **Key activity:** Decimal counters on a place-value grid; line up decimal points; exchange 10 hundredths for a tenth. Mental: bonds to 1 (0.35 + 0.65).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must the decimal points line up in column addition?" → So digits of the same value (ones with ones, tenths with tenths) are added together.
- **Differentiation:** support: money context with coins. / stretch: find three prices that total exactly 10.00 with no zeros in the hundredths.
- **Materials:** decimal place-value counters, coins, price tags.
- **Joy:** "Class shop" with real price labels and a budget.

### Lesson 97 — Subtracting decimals with the same number of places
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can subtract decimals with up to 2 decimal places, exchanging where needed." (UK NC Y5 decimals — subtract; CCSS 5.NBT.7)
- **Hook:** "You pay 10.00 for something costing 6.37. What change should you get?"
- **Key activity:** Counting on (6.37 → 6.40 → 7 → 10 = 3.63) and column subtraction with exchanges across zeros; compare methods.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is counting on often easier for 10.00 − 6.37?" → The numbers need several exchanges across zeros in columns; counting on uses easy steps to the next whole numbers.
- **Differentiation:** support: coins for counting on. / stretch: missing-digit decimal subtractions.
- **Materials:** coins and notes (any currency), decimal counters, number lines.
- **Joy:** "Shopkeeper" role-play giving change.

### Lesson 98 — Adding and subtracting decimals with different numbers of places
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can add and subtract decimals with different numbers of places, using placeholder zeros." (UK NC Y5 decimals; CCSS 5.NBT.7)
- **Hook:** "Is 5.3 − 2.47 the same as 5.30 − 2.47?"
- **Key activity:** Add placeholder zeros to line up; mixed measurements (1.5 kg + 0.375 kg = 1.875 kg); calculate and check with estimates.
- **Check for understanding:** "Raj calculates 5.3 − 2.47 = 3.17. Explain his mistake." → He subtracted the smaller digit from the larger in each column (4 − 3, 7 − 0) instead of exchanging; writing 5.30 − 2.47 and exchanging gives 2.83.
- **Differentiation:** support: one-decimal-place and two-decimal-place pairs only. / stretch: 3-decimal-place measurements (2.4 l − 0.875 l = 1.525 l).
- **Materials:** decimal counters, kitchen scales, measuring jugs.
- **Joy:** "Potion lab" — mix measured amounts of coloured water and calculate totals.

### Lesson 99 — Multiplying and dividing decimals by 10, 100 and 1000
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply and divide decimals by 10, 100 and 1000 by moving digits." (UK NC Y5 decimals — ×/÷ by 10, 100, 1000; CCSS 5.NBT.2)
- **Hook:** "A 2.5 kg bag of rice. How heavy are 10 bags? 100? And what is 1/1000 of it in kg?"
- **Key activity:** Place-value grid with a fixed decimal point; digits move left for ×, right for ÷; placeholder zeros. Reinforce: the decimal point does not move — the digits do.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 'add a zero' wrong for 2.5 × 10?" → It would give 2.50, which equals 2.5; really each digit moves one place left: 25.
- **Differentiation:** support: counters physically moved on the grid. / stretch: 0.07 × 1000 and 45 ÷ 1000, then invent a missing-number chain.
- **Materials:** place-value grid with fixed decimal point, decimal counters, rice bags.
- **Joy:** "Shrink and grow machine" — feed numbers into a cardboard machine that ×/÷ by 10, 100, 1000.

### Lesson 100 — Decimals in measures
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use decimals to read scales and convert measures such as m to cm, kg to g, l to ml." (UK NC Y5 decimals & measures; CCSS 5.MD.1)
- **Hook:** "A baby weighs 3.45 kg at birth. How many grams is that?"
- **Key activity:** Read scales with decimal intervals (kitchen scales, measuring jugs, metre sticks); convert by ×/÷ 1000 or 100.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is 3.45 kg = 3,450 g and not 345 g?" → There are 1000 g in 1 kg, so multiply by 1000 — every digit moves three places left.
- **Differentiation:** support: whole and half measures first. / stretch: order 1.5 kg, 1,050 g, 1.505 kg, 1 kg 55 g (1,050 g; 1 kg 55 g; 1.5 kg; 1.505 kg).
- **Materials:** kitchen scales, measuring jugs, metre sticks, baby-scale images.
- **Joy:** "Class measuring museum" — measure and label objects in two units.

### Lesson 101 — Decimal problem-solving
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve problems involving decimals in money and measures." (UK NC Y5 decimals — solve problems)
- **Hook:** "Plan a picnic for 4.00 per person — can you make it healthy and within budget?"
- **Key activity:** Real price lists; add, subtract, compare; justify choices; check with estimation.
- **Check for understanding:** "You have 20.00 and buy items for 7.85, 4.60 and 3.99. Estimate first, then calculate the change." → Estimate 8 + 4.5 + 4 = 16.5 so about 3.50; exact change 3.56.
- **Differentiation:** support: prices to 1 decimal place. / stretch: find the maximum number of items within the budget and prove it.
- **Materials:** price lists, coins/notes, calculators for checking.
- **Joy:** Plan and 'buy' a picnic; the healthiest in-budget picnic wins.

### Lesson 102 — Unit 8 assessment and review
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can show mastery of decimals to three places." (UK NC Y5 decimals — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Decimal Olympics — five events."
- **Key activity:** Mini-assessment (read/write, compare, round, calculate, ×/÷ by 10/100/1000); partner marking; fix-it/deepen-it.
- **Check for understanding:** "Order 0.7, 0.07, 0.707, 0.77 and explain." → 0.07, 0.7, 0.707, 0.77; line up places (0.070, 0.700, 0.707, 0.770).
- **Differentiation:** support: place-value chart for fix-it. / stretch: "Is 3.4 × 100 ÷ 1000 bigger or smaller than 3.4, and by how many times?" (0.34 — ten times smaller).
- **Materials:** assessment sheet, place-value charts.
- **Joy:** Medal podium for "events" completed.

**End-of-unit check:** Write 0.625 as a fraction in simplest form (⅝); order 3.405, 3.45, 3.045 (3.045, 3.405, 3.45); calculate 12.5 − 4.37 (8.13); 0.36 × 100 (36) and 4.2 ÷ 100 (0.042).

## Unit 9 — Statistics: line graphs, tables and timetables

**Essential question:** How do graphs and tables tell stories about change — and how can they mislead?

**Key vocabulary:** line graph, axis, scale, interval, continuous data, trend, two-way table, timetable, 24-hour clock, duration, interpret

### Lesson 103 — Reading and interpreting line graphs
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read values, including between points, from a line graph and describe the trend." (UK NC Y5 statistics)
- **Hook:** A graph of a day's temperature in Nairobi: "When was it warmest? What was it at 10:30?"
- **Key activity:** Read axes and scales; read plotted and intermediate values; describe trends (rising, falling, steady).
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can you read a value at 10:30 on a temperature line graph but not on a bar chart of favourite fruits?" → Temperature changes continuously over time; favourite fruits are separate categories with nothing 'in between'.
- **Differentiation:** support: graphs with scales in 1s or 2s. / stretch: scales in 5s or 25s, reading values between gridlines.
- **Materials:** printed line graphs, rulers, graph paper.
- **Joy:** "Graph storyteller" — narrate what happened in the graph like a sports commentator.

### Lesson 104 — Drawing line graphs from real data
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw a line graph with a suitable scale from data I collect." (UK NC Y5 statistics)
- **Hook:** "Does a cup of hot water cool at the same speed all the time?" (links to science)
- **Key activity:** Measure temperature of warm (not hot) water every 2 minutes with a thermometer; choose scales; plot and join points; describe the curve.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did you choose that scale for your temperature axis?" → A good answer: it covers the range of data and uses the space well, with equal intervals.
- **Differentiation:** support: axes pre-drawn. / stretch: two lines on one graph (insulated vs uninsulated cup) and compare.
- **Materials:** thermometers, warm water (adult-supervised — warm tap water only), stopwatch, graph paper, rulers.
- **Joy:** Running a real mini-experiment and seeing the curve appear.

### Lesson 105 — Two-way tables
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read, complete and interpret two-way tables." (UK NC Y5 statistics — complete and interpret tables)
- **Hook:** "Do more children in our class prefer cats or dogs — and does that differ between people with and without siblings?"
- **Key activity:** Collect class data; complete a two-way table with totals; use row/column totals to find missing values.
- **Check for understanding:** "A table shows 14 girls; 9 walk to school. How many girls don't walk, and where is that in the table?" → 5; in the 'girls' row, 'don't walk' column.
- **Differentiation:** support: tables with only one missing value per row. / stretch: tables with only totals and a few values given.
- **Materials:** tally sheets, blank two-way tables, sticky notes.
- **Joy:** Live class survey building a table on the board.

### Lesson 106 — Reading timetables
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can read a timetable and calculate journey times, including across the hour." (UK NC Y5 statistics — timetables)
- **Hook:** A real-looking train timetable: "Can we get from the city to the coast by 12:00?"
- **Key activity:** Read rows (stops) and columns (services); calculate durations by counting on in steps via the next hour on a number line (09:50 → 10:00 → 10:20).
- **Check for understanding:** "A train leaves at 14:35 and arrives at 15:10. Why is 75 minutes wrong?" → Treating times as ordinary numbers (1510 − 1435 = 75) forgets an hour has 60 minutes, not 100; count on: 25 min to 15:00, then 10 more = 35 min.
- **Differentiation:** support: analogue clocks and a time number line. / stretch: plan a journey with a change, leaving at least 5 minutes to switch trains.
- **Materials:** printed timetables, analogue clocks, time number lines.
- **Joy:** "Travel agent" challenge: plan a day trip itinerary.

### Lesson 107 — Misleading graphs and problem-solving with data
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can spot how a graph can mislead and solve problems using data." (UK NC Y5 statistics — solve problems)
- **Hook:** Two graphs of the same data — one with a y-axis starting at 90 — "Did sales explode or barely move?"
- **Key activity:** Compare truncated vs full axes; redraw fairly; answer comparison and sum/difference questions.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain how the first graph makes a small change look big." → The vertical axis doesn't start at 0, so small differences look huge.
- **Differentiation:** support: two graphs only with guiding questions. / stretch: make a deliberately misleading graph and challenge a partner to find the trick.
- **Materials:** printed graphs, graph paper, rulers.
- **Joy:** "Fake news detectives" — find the sneaky graph.

### Lesson 108 — Unit 9 assessment and review
- **Duration:** 30 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can interpret line graphs, tables and timetables to answer questions." (UK NC Y5 statistics — assessment)
- **Hook:** "Mission debrief: read the data, save the day."
- **Key activity:** Short assessment with a line graph, a two-way table and a timetable; partner marking.
- **Check for understanding:** "From the timetable, which is the latest bus you can take to arrive by 09:00, and how long is the journey?" → Answer depends on the given timetable; child identifies the service and calculates duration correctly.
- **Differentiation:** support: questions read aloud. / stretch: add a 'which graph would best show…' reasoning question.
- **Materials:** assessment sheet.
- **Joy:** Unlock a 'mission complete' certificate.

**End-of-unit check:** Read a temperature at an intermediate time from a line graph; complete a two-way table with 3 missing values; calculate a journey 11:48 → 13:15 (1 h 27 min).

## Unit 12 — Perimeter, area and volume

**Essential question:** How do we measure the edge, the surface and the space of a shape — and why do they need different units?

### Lesson 121 — Perimeter of rectangles and rectilinear shapes
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate the perimeter of rectangles and rectilinear shapes, finding missing lengths." (UK NC Y5 measurement — perimeter; CCSS 4.MD.3)
- **Hook:** "A farmer needs fencing for an L-shaped field. Some side lengths are missing!"

### Lesson 122 — Area of rectangles by formula

- **Hook:** "How many square tiles to cover this table without counting them all?"

### Lesson 123 — Area and perimeter: same area, different perimeter

- **Key activity:** Step-by-step: draw arm, mark vertex, align protractor, mark the degree, join. Check by measuring a partner's angle.
- **Check for understanding:** "How can you check your 35° angle is right without a protractor?" → Estimate: it should be a bit more than a third of a right angle; then measure to confirm.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-drawn arms with vertex marked. / stretch: draw a triangle with sides and angles given (e.g. 6 cm base, angles 50° and 70°).
- **Materials:** protractors, sharp pencils, rulers, toy cars and card ramps.
- **Joy:** Test ramps at different angles — which angle sends the car furthest?

### Lesson 145 — Measuring reflex angles
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can measure and draw reflex angles using 360°." (UK NC Y5 geometry — reflex angles)
- **Hook:** "A 180° protractor can't reach a 250° angle. What can we do?"
- **Key activity:** Measure the smaller angle and subtract from 360°, or measure 180° plus the extra; 360° protractors if available.
- **Check for understanding:** "The small angle is 75°. What is the reflex angle, and why?" → 285°, because the two angles make a full turn: 360 − 75.
- **Differentiation:** support: reflex angles with the small angle already labelled. / stretch: draw a 310° angle two ways.
- **Materials:** 180° and 360° protractors, printed angles.
- **Joy:** "Pac-Man angles" — draw Pac-Man with the mouth angle given, measure the reflex body angle.

### Lesson 146 — Angles on a straight line and around a point
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate missing angles on a straight line (180°) and around a point (360°)." (UK NC Y5 geometry — angle facts; CCSS 4.MD.7)
- **Hook:** Tear corners off paper shapes and line them up: "Why do angles on a straight line always make 180°?"
- **Key activity:** Card angle fans; calculate missing angles with bar models (180 split into parts); around a point (360).
- **Check for understanding:** "One angle on a straight line is 118°. Eli says the other is 242°. What mistake did he make?" → He used 360 instead of 180; the answer is 62°.
- **Differentiation:** support: two-part straight lines only with a bar model. / stretch: three or four unknowns expressed with the same letter (a + a + 60 = 180).
- **Materials:** paper angle cut-outs, protractors, bar templates.
- **Joy:** "Angle pizza" — cut paper pizzas into angle slices that must total 360°.

### Lesson 147 — Vertically opposite angles and multiples of 90°
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify vertically opposite angles and angles that are multiples of 90°." (UK NC Y5 geometry — angle facts)
- **Hook:** "Cross two sticks. Which angles are always equal?"
- **Key activity:** Two rulers crossed; measure all four angles; reason using straight-line facts; find angles made by compass turns (multiples of 90°).
- **Check for understanding:** "Two lines cross. One angle is 40°. Find the other three and explain." → 140°, 40°, 140°; neighbours make 180° on a straight line, opposites are equal.
- **Differentiation:** support: protractor to measure and confirm. / stretch: prove vertically opposite angles are equal using straight-line facts.
- **Materials:** rulers or sticks, split-pin strips, protractors.
- **Joy:** "Scissors angles" — open scissors and predict the opposite angle.

### Lesson 148 — Angles problem-solving
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can solve multi-step angle problems and explain my reasoning." (UK NC Y5 geometry — solve problems)
- **Hook:** "An escape map where every door code is a missing angle."
- **Key activity:** Chains of angle facts; explain each step using "because" sentences.
- **Check for understanding:** "Around a point: 90°, 115°, x and x. Find x and explain." → 360 − 90 − 115 = 155; x = 77.5°.
- **Differentiation:** support: one-step puzzles. / stretch: puzzles combining straight lines and points.
- **Materials:** puzzle maps, protractors for checking.
- **Joy:** Angle escape map.

### Lesson 149 — Retrieval: angles
- **Duration:** 30 min

- **Hook:** "Estimate-off: who gets closest?"
- **Key activity:** Estimate then measure 6 angles; quick-fire missing angle facts.
- **Check for understanding:** "Estimate this angle, then measure. Explain how your estimate helped." → Estimate used benchmarks (90°, 45°) and caught a scale error.
- **Differentiation:** support: benchmark angle cards. / stretch: reflex estimates.
- **Materials:** protractors, printed angles.
- **Joy:** Estimate-off league.

### Lesson 150 — Unit 14 assessment and review
- **Duration:** 35 min

- **Hook:** "Navigator's exam: guide the ship with angles."

## Unit 15 — Shape, position and direction

**Essential question:** What makes a shape the shape it is — and what changes and stays the same when we move it?

**Key vocabulary:** polygon, regular, irregular, parallel, perpendicular, diagonal, quadrilateral, rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram, trapezium (trapezoid), 3-D shape, face, edge, vertex, net, prism, pyramid, reflection, mirror line, translation, coordinate, axis

### Lesson 151 — Regular and irregular polygons
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can decide if a polygon is regular, using both sides and angles." (UK NC Y5 geometry — regular and irregular polygons; CCSS 5.G.3)
- **Hook:** "A stop sign is a regular octagon. Is every 8-sided shape a stop sign shape?"
- **Key activity:** Geoboards and polygon sets; measure sides and angles; sort in a Venn diagram (equal sides / equal angles).
- **Check for understanding:** "Is a rhombus regular? Is a rectangle? Explain." → Neither: a rhombus has equal sides but not always equal angles; a rectangle has equal angles but not always equal sides. Only the square is both.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-made shapes to measure. / stretch: can a polygon have equal angles but unequal sides? Draw one.
- **Materials:** geoboards, polygon shape sets, rulers, protractors.
- **Joy:** "Polygon zoo" — build and name polygons on geoboards.

### Lesson 152 — Properties of rectangles and other quadrilaterals
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use properties of rectangles to find missing lengths and angles." (UK NC Y5 geometry — properties of rectangles)
- **Hook:** "A shape detective's notebook: what do we know for sure about every rectangle?"
- **Key activity:** Fold and measure: opposite sides equal and parallel, all angles 90°, diagonals equal and bisect each other. Compare with parallelogram, rhombus, trapezium (called trapezoid in the US).
- **Check for understanding:** "Is a square a rectangle? Explain using properties." → Yes; it has four right angles and opposite sides equal, so it meets every rectangle property (it's a special rectangle).
- **Differentiation:** support: shape cards with property checklists. / stretch: a quadrilateral family tree diagram.
- **Materials:** quadrilateral cut-outs, rulers, protractors, geostrips.
- **Joy:** "Guess my shape" — 20 questions using properties only.

### Lesson 153 — Parallel and perpendicular lines
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify and draw parallel and perpendicular lines." (consolidates UK NC Y3 geometry; CCSS 4.G.1)
- **Hook:** "Railway tracks and crossroads — which lines never meet?"
- **Key activity:** Find examples in the room and in street maps; draw with a ruler and set square (or the corner of a card); mark with arrows (parallel) and a small square (perpendicular).
- **Check for understanding:** "Two lines look parallel but will they ever meet? How can you check?" → Measure the gap at several points — parallel lines stay the same distance apart.
- **Differentiation:** support: squared paper for drawing. / stretch: draw on plain paper using only a ruler and set square.
- **Materials:** rulers, set squares, street map extracts, squared paper.
- **Joy:** "Design a city grid" with parallel and perpendicular streets.

### Lesson 154 — 3-D shapes and their properties
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe 3-D shapes by faces, edges and vertices and name prisms and pyramids." (UK NC Y5 geometry — 3-D shapes)
- **Hook:** "Why is a Toblerone box a prism and the Great Pyramid of Giza a pyramid?"
- **Key activity:** Handle solids; count F, E, V; classify prisms (same cross-section) vs pyramids (faces meet at an apex). Look at Euler's rule F + V − E = 2 for these shapes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Is a cylinder a prism? Explain." → Not in the usual (polygon) sense — its faces aren't all polygons — though it has a constant circular cross-section; accept a reasoned answer that mentions the curved surface.
- **Differentiation:** support: count with sticky dots on each vertex. / stretch: check F + V − E = 2 for 5 shapes.
- **Materials:** 3-D solids set, sticky dots, packaging boxes.
- **Joy:** "Mystery bag" — identify a shape by touch alone.

### Lesson 155 — Nets and building 3-D shapes
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can identify and draw nets of cubes, cuboids, prisms and pyramids." (UK NC Y5 geometry — 3-D from 2-D representations)
- **Hook:** "Here are 6 squares joined in different ways. Which fold into a cube?"
- **Key activity:** Unfold packaging; build with Polydron/card; find all 11 cube nets; draw nets on squared paper.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a cross of 6 squares fold into a cube, but a 2 × 3 rectangle of 6 squares does not?" → Folding the rectangle makes squares land on top of each other and leaves two faces of the cube uncovered; in the cross every face has its own place.
- **Differentiation:** support: interlocking plastic shapes (e.g. Polydron). / stretch: draw the net of a triangular prism and a square-based pyramid to exact measurements.
- **Materials:** interlocking polygon kits, card, scissors, glue, squared paper, packaging.
- **Joy:** Design and build a gift box net for a real present.

### Lesson 156 — Isometric drawing and plans
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw simple cube models on isometric paper and match 3-D models to plans and elevations." (UK NC Y5 geometry — 3-D from 2-D representations)
- **Hook:** "An architect sends you a drawing — can you build it from cubes?"
- **Key activity:** Build models from 4–6 cubes, draw them on isometric paper; match to top/front views.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might two different models look the same from the front?" → Cubes can be hidden behind others; you need more views.
- **Differentiation:** support: 3–4 cubes only. / stretch: build a model from three views (plan, front, side).
- **Materials:** interlocking cubes, isometric dot paper.
- **Joy:** "Builder and architect" pairs — one draws, one builds, compare.

### Lesson 157 — Reflection in a mirror line
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can reflect shapes in horizontal and vertical mirror lines and explain that the shape is unchanged." (UK NC Y5 geometry — reflection)
- **Hook:** "Rangoli and kolam designs from India use reflection. How do the artists make both halves match?"
- **Key activity:** Mirrors and tracing paper; each vertex is the same distance from the mirror on the other side; reflect on squared paper with horizontal and vertical lines.
- **Check for understanding:** "A vertex is 3 squares left of a vertical mirror line. Where is its image and why?" → 3 squares to the right, on the same row — reflected points are the same perpendicular distance from the line.
- **Differentiation:** support: small mirrors and shapes touching the line. / stretch: reflect in a diagonal mirror line.
- **Materials:** mirrors, tracing paper, squared paper, rangoli pattern examples.
- **Joy:** Design a symmetric rangoli-inspired pattern.

### Lesson 158 — Reflection on coordinate grids
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can reflect a shape in a line parallel to an axis and write the new coordinates." (UK NC Y5 geometry — position)
- **Hook:** "A treasure map is only half drawn — the other half is its reflection."
- **Key activity:** First-quadrant grids with mirror lines like x = 5 or y = 4; plot, reflect, record coordinates in tables; notice which coordinate changes.
- **Check for understanding:** "Reflect (2, 3) in the line x = 5. What is the image and which coordinate stayed the same?" → (8, 3); the y-coordinate stays the same because the mirror is vertical.
- **Differentiation:** support: mirror lines on gridlines with small shapes. / stretch: find the mirror line given a point and its image.

### Lesson 159 — Translation
- **Duration:** 35 min

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Place value to 1,000,000 and negatives** — read, write, order and round six-digit numbers and find intervals across zero. *See it:* child explains the deciding digit when rounding 649,500 to the nearest 10,000 (650,000) and calculates −8 °C to 5 °C (13 °C).
2. **Number structure** — recall squares to 12² and cubes to 5³, find factor pairs, identify primes to 100. *See it:* child lists factors of 60 systematically and explains why 1 is not prime.
3. **×/÷ by 10, 100, 1000** — including decimals, explained as digits moving. *See it:* child moves counters on a place-value grid for 3.7 × 100 and 45 ÷ 1000.
4. **Written methods** — long multiplication (4-digit × 2-digit) and short division with remainders interpreted. *See it:* child explains the placeholder zero and chooses to round up for "how many coaches".
5. **Fractions** — equivalence, compare/order, convert mixed ↔ improper, add/subtract with related denominators, multiply by a whole number, find fractions of amounts. *See it:* child uses a bar model to solve "⅗ of the pupils is 42 — how many pupils?"
6. **Decimals and percentages** — read, order and round to 3 dp; add/subtract decimals; link %, fraction, decimal; find percentages of amounts. *See it:* child orders ⅜, 0.35, 38% and finds 35% of 240 using 10% and 5%.

8. **Angles** — measure and draw to ±2°, calculate missing angles on a line and around a point. *See it:* child draws 145° and explains which protractor scale they read.

## Teacher guidance

**How to teach each lesson.** Start with a 5-minute fluency starter (tables, bonds, doubling). Introduce new ideas concretely, then draw them, then write them — and keep the concrete materials out for any child who needs them; manipulatives are a thinking tool, not a crutch. Use "I do, we do, you do", and finish with the check for understanding before moving on. Where many children miss the check, reteach the next day using a different representation.

**Common misconceptions (and fixes).**
- *"Add a zero" when multiplying by 10* — breaks for decimals (2.5 × 10 ≠ 2.50). Always show digits moving on a place-value grid with a fixed decimal point.
- *Longer decimals are bigger* (0.45 > 0.5) — equalise places with placeholder zeros; use hundred squares.
- *Rounding always goes up* — use number lines to show which multiple is nearer.
- *Adding fractions across* (½ + ¼ = 2/6) — insist on same-size pieces with fraction strips; show 2/6 is smaller than ½.
- *Multiplying both parts of a fraction by the whole number* (⅔ × 4 = 8/12) — model as repeated addition of the fraction.
- *1 is prime; 5² = 10* — prime = exactly two factors; the index means repeated multiplication.
- *Missing the placeholder zero in long multiplication* — colour-code the area model and match each row.
- *Reading the wrong protractor scale* — estimate first; an acute angle must read under 90°.
- *Time as decimals* (1 h 35 min for 135 minutes; 1510 − 1435 = 75) — count on using a time number line.
- *Bigger area means bigger perimeter* — build rectangles with tiles to disprove it.

**Differentiation.** Keep support concrete (same idea, smaller numbers, more models) rather than giving different content; stretch goes deeper (reasoning, generalising, open problems), not faster into Year 5 content.

**International notes.** Use local currency in money lessons (all examples use "cents"/decimal amounts deliberately); swap local cities, temperatures and landmarks into the hooks. Where your country uses "trapezoid" or "decimal comma", say so explicitly and show both forms.

**Vertical links.** Year 3 built tables to 12 × 12, tenths/hundredths and fractions of amounts; do not re-teach from scratch — retrieve and extend. Year 5 needs long multiplication, fraction equivalence, decimals to 3 dp, protractor skills and first-quadrant reflection to be secure: use the end-of-year checks to flag children who need a summer boost.

**Cross-curricular links.** Science (line graphs of cooling in Lesson 104; forces measured in newtons; Earth and space distances), geography (populations, time zones, map scales), art (rangoli symmetry, tessellation), computing (angles in turtle programming), life skills (budgets and percentages in money sense).
