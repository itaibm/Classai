# Year 4 Mathematics — Tutor Scripts (Lessons 1–30)

Complete, minute-by-minute scripts for [Year 4 Maths](../maths-year-4.md), built to the [scripting guide](../../../SCRIPTING-GUIDE.md) and grounded in `knowledge-base/subjects/maths/year-4.md`. Year 4 (ages 8–9) works fluently in the abstract while still proving new ideas concretely (100-square for decimals, cubes for area). Lessons run the upper band (24–30 min) with reasoning probes ("how do you know?"). Every beat ⏱ sums to the duration, with verbatim Say / Show / Do / Ask, KB-based wrong-answer fixes, the "if the child is stuck" protocol, differentiation and assessment evidence.

## Lesson 1 — Place value to 10,000
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Place value, rounding, number systems)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can read, build and partition four-digit numbers."
**You will need:** a Th|H|T|O place-value chart, base-10/counters, numeral cards.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Year 3 place value to 1000 (hundreds, tens, ones).
**Key vocabulary:** **thousands**, **ten-thousands**, **partition**, **placeholder zero**, **digit value**.
**What to emphasize:** each digit's **place gives its value**; a **placeholder zero** holds an empty column (3,047). Compare from the **biggest place first**.
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "A stadium holds 3,482 fans. Each digit is doing a different job — what does the 3 mean? The 4? Let's crack the code of big numbers."
**Show:** the number 3,482 on the chart.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "In 482, what does the 4 mean?" → *listen for:* "400 / four hundreds."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Four columns: **Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones**. 3,482 = 3000 + 400 + 80 + 2 — that's **partitioning**. Watch a tricky one: 3,047 — the **0 is a placeholder**, holding the empty tens so the 3 stays in thousands. To compare, start at the **biggest place**."
**Do:** build 3,482 and 3,047 on the chart; partition aloud.
**Emphasize:** "**Place gives value**; a 0 holds an empty column."
### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Partition 5,260 with me — thousands? hundreds? tens? ones?"
**Do:** child partitions 5,260 = 5000 + 200 + 60 + 0.
**Ask:** "Partition 6,209." → *listen for:* "6000 + 200 + 0 + 9."
### ⏱ 14–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Partition 6,209 — and which digit is the placeholder?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 6000 + 200 + 0 + 9; the 0 in tens. — probe: *"What would happen without that 0?"* (it'd become 629).
- ❌ **If writes 6,209 as 6000+200+9 (drops the 0):** → **fix:** "Show the tens column — it's empty, so 0 holds the place."
- ❌ **If misreads digit values:** → **fix:** "Put it on the chart — which column is the 2 in?"
### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child partitions four-digit numbers (incl. ones with placeholder zeros) and builds them on the chart. Once secure, interleave: "Which is bigger, 7,108 or 7,180?"
**Watch for:** dropping placeholder zeros; comparing from the wrong end.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — place/zero; 3) build it on the chart together; 4) hint: "biggest place first"; 5) re-ask a 3-digit one; 6) "Each digit has its own job."
### ⏱ 22–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "How do you compare two big numbers?" → *listen for:* "start at the biggest place."
**Process praise:** "You used the placeholder zero correctly — that's precise place-value thinking."
**Revisit later:** rounding next builds straight on this.
---
**Differentiation** — support: Th/H/T/O chart, empty columns get a 0. / stretch: compare 7,108 vs 7,180 and justify.
**Extension (if time allows):** make the biggest and smallest 4-digit number from four digit cards.
**Assessment evidence:** child reads, builds and partitions four-digit numbers including placeholder zeros.

## Lesson 2 — Rounding to 10, 100 and 1000
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Place value, rounding, number systems)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can round a number to the nearest 10, 100 or 1000."
**You will need:** number lines, a place-value chart.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** place value to 10,000 (L1); number lines.
**Key vocabulary:** **round**, **nearest**, **rounding digit**, **the digit to the right**, **halfway**.
**What to emphasize:** look at the digit **to the right** of the rounding place — **5 or more rounds up, 4 or less stays**; the digits after become 0. Pre-empt "rounding always goes up."
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "'About how many people came?' Sometimes 'about 3,000' is more useful than the exact number. That's **rounding** — and there's a clever rule."
**Show:** a number line 2,700–2,800.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's 2,749 to the nearest ten — between which tens does it sit?" → *listen for:* "2,740 and 2,750."
### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "To round, I find the two round numbers it sits between and pick the **nearer**. The rule: look at the digit **just to the right** of the place I'm rounding to. **5 or more → round up; 4 or less → stay**; the rest become 0. Round 2,749 to the nearest 100: the tens digit is 4 → stays → 2,700. Not always up!"
**Do:** show 2,749 on the line nearer 2,700; apply the rule.
**Emphasize:** "Check the **digit to the right** — 5+ up, 4 or less stays."
### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Round 2,749 to the nearest 100 with me — which digit do we look at?"
**Do:** child identifies the tens digit (4) and rounds to 2,700.
**Ask:** "Round 2,749 to the nearest 100." → *listen for:* "2,700."
### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Round 4,350 to the nearest 1000 — and does rounding always go up?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 4,000; no — depends on the digit to the right. — probe: *"Which digit decided it?"* (the hundreds, 3).
- ❌ **If "rounds up to 5,000 always":** → **fix (KB):** "Put it on the line — is 4,350 nearer 4,000 or 5,000? The 3 says stay."
- ❌ **If looks at the wrong digit:** → **fix:** "Look just to the *right* of the thousands — that's the hundreds."
### ⏱ 17–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child rounds numbers to 10/100/1000, using a number line where unsure. Once secure, interleave: "Which numbers round to 500 to the nearest 100?"
**Watch for:** always-up; wrong rounding digit.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — which digit / nearer end; 3) draw the number line together; 4) hint: "nearer the top or bottom of the hill?"; 5) re-ask nearest 10; 6) "Check the neighbour digit."
### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What's the rounding rule?" → *listen for:* "look at the digit to the right — 5+ up, else stay."
**Process praise:** "You proved rounding doesn't always go up — you read the deciding digit."
**Revisit later:** rounding helps estimate answers all year.
---
**Differentiation** — support: a number line to see the nearer end. / stretch: list numbers that round to 500 to the nearest 100.
**Extension (if time allows):** round the same number to 10, 100 and 1000 and compare.
**Assessment evidence:** child rounds to the nearest 10/100/1000 using the right-digit rule.

## Lesson 3 — Negative numbers
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Place value, rounding, number systems)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can count through zero and compare negative numbers."
**You will need:** a thermometer model, a negative number line (left of 0).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the number line; ordering numbers.
**Key vocabulary:** **negative**, **below zero**, **minus**, **count through zero**, **lower/colder**.
**What to emphasize:** below zero, a **bigger digit means a LOWER number** (−7 is colder/less than −3). Count **through** zero. Pre-empt "−5 > −2."
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "The weather says it's −2 °C outside. What does that little **minus** mean? Today we go *below zero*."
**Show:** the thermometer model.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Count down: 3, 2, 1, 0… what comes next?" → *listen for:* "−1."
### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "The number line keeps going **left of zero**: 2, 1, 0, −1, −2, −3… On a thermometer, **lower = colder = a smaller number**. So −5 is *colder* (lower) than −2 — even though 5 looks bigger, with the minus it's *less*. Watch me count down through zero."
**Do:** count 3→−3 on the line; point on the thermometer.
**Emphasize:** "Below zero, **bigger digit = lower number**."
### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Which is lower on the thermometer — −2 or −5? Point to each."
**Do:** child locates and compares on the thermometer.
**Ask:** "Which is colder, −3 °C or −7 °C?" → *listen for:* "−7 °C."
### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which is colder, −3 or −7 — and how do you know?"
- ✅ **Expected:** −7; it's lower on the thermometer / further below zero. — probe: *"Is −7 bigger or smaller than −3?"* (smaller).
- ❌ **If "−5 is bigger than −2":** → **fix (KB):** "Read the thermometer — −5 is *lower down*, so it's the smaller, colder number."
- ❌ **If can't count through zero:** → **fix:** "2, 1, 0, then keep going — −1, −2."
### ⏱ 17–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child counts through zero and compares pairs of negatives on the line. Once secure, interleave: find the difference between −3 and 4.
**Watch for:** treating negatives like positives; stopping at zero.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — direction; 3) use the thermometer together; 4) hint: "lower = colder = smaller"; 5) re-ask −1 vs −4; 6) "Below zero flips it."
### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Below zero, which is smaller — −2 or −8?" → *listen for:* "−8."
**Process praise:** "You read the thermometer instead of just the digits — that's the key to negatives."
**Revisit later:** negatives return in temperature and difference problems.
---
**Differentiation** — support: read the thermometer. / stretch: find the difference between −3 and 4.
**Extension (if time allows):** order four negative/positive numbers smallest to largest.
**Assessment evidence:** child counts through zero and compares negative numbers correctly.

## Lesson 4 — Roman numerals
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Place value, rounding, number systems)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can read and write Roman numerals using the subtractive rule."
**You will need:** a Roman-numeral chart, a clock face.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** numbers to 1000; the idea of symbols for numbers.
**Key vocabulary:** **Roman numerals**, **I/V/X/L/C/D/M**, **subtractive rule**, **before = subtract**.
**What to emphasize:** repeating numerals **add** (III=3); a **smaller numeral before a bigger one subtracts** (IV=4, IX=9, XL=40). Pre-empt writing 4 as IIII.
---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Why does a posh clock say **IV** for 4, and a film say **MMXXIV**? The Romans had a clever number system — let's decode it."
**Show:** a clock face with Roman numerals.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What number is on most clocks at the top?" → *listen for:* "12 (XII)."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "The symbols: **I=1, V=5, X=10, L=50, C=100, D=500, M=1000**. Repeating **adds**: III = 3. But a smaller numeral **before** a bigger one **subtracts**: IV = 5−1 = 4, IX = 9, XL = 40. That's the **subtractive rule** — it's why 4 is IV, not IIII. Watch: 24 = XX + IV = XXIV."
**Do:** build III, IV, IX, XL, XXIV on the chart.
**Emphasize:** "Smaller **before** bigger = **subtract** (IV=4, IX=9, XL=40)."
### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Write 24 with me — twenty is…? then four?"
**Do:** child writes XXIV; reads a year like MMXX.
**Ask:** "What is XL?" → *listen for:* "40."
### ⏱ 14–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What is XL, and why isn't 4 written IIII?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 40; the subtractive rule (smaller before bigger subtracts) — probe: *"So what's IX?"* (9).
- ❌ **If writes 4 as IIII:** → **fix (KB):** "Use the subtractive rule — one before five, IV = 4."
- ❌ **If adds a subtractive pair (IX=11):** → **fix:** "I is *before* X, so subtract: 10 − 1 = 9."
### ⏱ 17–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child reads and writes numerals (incl. IV, IX, XL, clock numbers). Once secure, interleave: write a recent year in Roman numerals.
**Watch for:** IIII; adding instead of subtracting.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — subtractive rule; 3) use the chart together; 4) hint: "smaller before bigger = take away"; 5) re-ask IV/IX; 6) "Before means subtract."
### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the subtractive rule." → *listen for:* "a smaller numeral before a bigger one subtracts."
**Process praise:** "You used the subtractive rule to write 4 as IV — proper Roman numeral work."
**Revisit later:** Unit 1 check; Roman numerals appear on clocks and dates.
---
**Differentiation** — support: the subtractive-rule chart. / stretch: write a recent year in Roman numerals.
**Extension (if time allows):** write your birth year in Roman numerals.
**Assessment evidence:** child reads/writes Roman numerals using the subtractive rule.

> **End-of-unit check (Unit 1):** (1) Partition 6,209 → 6000+200+0+9. (2) Round 4,350 to nearest 1000 → 4,000. (3) Colder: −3 or −7 → −7. (4) XL → 40.

## Lesson 5 — Tables to 12 × 12 (building the new tables)
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Multiplication, division and tables fluency)** | **Duration:** 28 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can use known facts to find 6, 7, 9, 11 and 12 table facts."
**You will need:** a 12×12 multiplication grid, pattern cards, mini-whiteboards.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** tables for 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 10; commutativity (7×8 = 8×7).
**Key vocabulary:** **times table**, **fact family**, **commutative**, **double**, **pattern**.
**What to emphasize:** build the hard tables from the easy ones — **6× = double 3×**, **9× = 10× − 1×**, **12× = 10× + 2×**; the **9-table digit pattern** (digits sum to 9). Pre-empt skip-counting from the start each time.

---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "**9 × 7** looks scary — but it's just **10 × 7 take away one 7**. 70 − 7 = 63. Today we turn the tables you *don't* know into tables you *do*."
**Show:** the multiplication grid with 6, 7, 9, 11, 12 columns highlighted.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's 10 × 7?" → *listen for:* "70."
*(If 10× is shaky: count 7, 70 — "just add a zero.")*
### ⏱ 3–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch me build the tricky tables from easy ones. **6× = double the 3×** — 3×7 = 21, double it = 42, so 6×7 = 42. **9× = 10× minus 1×** — 9×6 = 60 − 6 = 54. **12× = 10× plus 2×** — 12×8 = 80 + 16 = 96. And a magic pattern: in the 9 table the **digits always add to 9** — 18 (1+8), 27 (2+7), 36, 45…"
**Show / Do:** model each on the grid; circle the 9-table digit sums.
**Emphasize:** "Use a **fact you know** to reach one you don't."
### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's do 9 × 6 together. Ten sixes is…? Then take away one six?"
**Do:** child says 60, then 60 − 6 = 54; tries 6×8 by doubling 3×8.
**Ask:** "9 × 6 = ?" → *listen for:* "54."
### ⏱ 17–21 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "9 × 6 = ? — and how did you get it?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 54; 10×6 − 6 = 60 − 6. — probe: *"Do the digits 5 and 4 add to 9?"* (yes).
- ❌ **If "63" (counted wrong):** likely skip-counting slips → **fix (KB):** "Use 10×6 = 60, then take one 6 away."
- ❌ **If stalls and counts up the 6s:** no recall → **fix (KB):** "Start from 5×6 = 30, add a 6 → 36… build from a known fact."
### ⏱ 21–25 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Mixed facts from 6, 7, 9, 11, 12 tables; write the helper fact first. Once secure, interleave a division (54 ÷ 9) from the same family.
**Watch for:** skip-counting from 1; forgetting to subtract the 1× in 9s.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — no helper fact; 3) drop to the grid / write the known fact; 4) hint: "10× then adjust"; 5) re-ask 9×5; 6) "Build from one you know."
### ⏱ 25–28 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the trick for any 9× fact." → *listen for:* "ten times it, take away one lot."
**Process praise:** "You built 9×6 from 10×6 instead of guessing — that's a mathematician's move."
**Revisit later:** these facts feed rapid recall (Lesson 6) and column multiplication.
---
**Differentiation** — support: write the helper fact first. / stretch: explain the 9s digit pattern.
**Extension (if time allows):** find two facts that make 72 (8×9, 9×8, 6×12, 12×6).
**Assessment evidence:** child derives 6/9/12 facts from known facts and states the 9-pattern.

## Lesson 6 — MTC-style rapid recall
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Multiplication, division and tables fluency)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can recall any fact to 12 × 12 quickly, both ways and as division."
**You will need:** a timer, fact cards, mini-whiteboards.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the tricks from Lesson 5; commutativity and inverse.
**Key vocabulary:** **recall**, **fact family**, **commutative**, **inverse**, **personal best**.
**What to emphasize:** **speed comes from recall, not working out**; **commutativity halves the work** (7×8 = 8×7); every fact has a division partner (56 ÷ 7 = 8). Pre-empt knowing one direction only.

---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "**Beat the clock!** The national **Multiplication Tables Check** is all about *speed* — answering before you'd have time to count. Let's set a personal best today."
**Show:** the timer and a shuffled fact pack.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If 7 × 8 = 56, what's 8 × 7?" → *listen for:* "56 — same."
### ⏱ 3–8 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Two shortcuts. **Commutativity:** 7×8 and 8×7 are the *same* fact — learn one, get one free. **Inverse:** every fact gives a division — 7×8=56, so 56÷7=8 and 56÷8=7. Watch me flash a card and answer *instantly*, no counting."
**Show / Do:** flash 5 cards, model instant answers and the matching division.
**Emphasize:** "**Recall it, don't reckon it.**"
### ⏱ 8–13 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn on the whiteboard — I flash, you write. Then I'll flip one into a division."
**Do:** child answers a flashed set; converts 56 to 56÷7.
**Ask:** "72 ÷ 12 = ?" → *listen for:* "6."
### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "7 × 8 = ? and 72 ÷ 12 = ?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 56 and 6. — probe: *"What division comes from 7×8?"* (56÷7=8).
- ❌ **If knows 7×8 but not 56÷7:** one direction only → **fix (KB):** "Same fact family — 7×8=56, so 56÷7=8."
- ❌ **If counts up slowly:** no recall yet → **fix (KB):** "Target the tricky one (6,7,8) and drill it little and often."
### ⏱ 17–21 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Timed mixed pack — multiplications and divisions across all tables; record the score, then try to beat it. Support: tricky facts only (6,7,8).
**Watch for:** stalling on 6/7/8; ignoring the division partner.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — that fact isn't automatic; 3) pull it onto its own card; 4) hint with the Lesson 5 trick; 5) re-ask after three easy wins; 6) "Speed grows with practice — note it for tomorrow."
### ⏱ 21–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What's your personal best, and which fact will you target next?" → *listen for:* a score + a named tricky fact.
**Process praise:** "You beat your own time and used commutativity to skip half the work."
**Revisit later:** daily 2-minute recall sprint; revisit before column × and short ÷.
---
**Differentiation** — support: target the tricky facts (6, 7, 8) only. / stretch: full mixed grid, tighter timer.
**Extension (if time allows):** "Hit the Button"-style streak against the personal best.
**Assessment evidence:** child recalls mixed facts (and their divisions) at speed; logs a personal best.

## Lesson 7 — Column multiplication (× 1-digit)
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Multiplication, division and tables fluency)** | **Duration:** 28 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can multiply a 2- or 3-digit number by a 1-digit number in columns."
**You will need:** columned paper, a place-value chart, pencils.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** tables to 12; column addition with carrying; estimation.
**Key vocabulary:** **column multiplication**, **carry**, **estimate**, **place value**.
**What to emphasize:** **estimate first**; multiply **from the right**, **carry the ten** above the next column and **add it in**. Pre-empt dropping the carry.

---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "There are **243 seats in each of 3 sections** — how many seats in all? Too big to count, so we'll use **column multiplication**. First, a guess: about 240 × 3…?"
**Show:** 243 × 3 set out in columns.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Roughly, 240 × 3 ≈ ?" → *listen for:* "about 720."
### ⏱ 3–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "**Estimate first**: 243 × 3 ≈ 720, so our answer should be near 720. Now multiply **from the right**: 3×3 = 9, write 9. 3×4 = 12 — write the 2, **carry the 1** above the hundreds. 3×2 = 6, **plus the carried 1 = 7**. Answer: **729** — and look, that's right by 720. The carry is the part people forget, so I park it above the next column."
**Show / Do:** work the algorithm, writing the small carried 1 clearly.
**Emphasize:** "**Carry the ten** above the next column and **add it in**."
### ⏱ 11–18 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's do **134 × 4** together. Estimate first… now from the right."
**Do:** child: 4×4=16 (write 6 carry 1), 4×3=12 +1=13 (write 3 carry 1), 4×1=4 +1=5 → 536.
**Ask:** "134 × 4 = ?" → *listen for:* "536."
### ⏱ 18–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "134 × 4 = ? — does it match your estimate?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 536 (estimate ~520, close). — probe: *"Where did the carried tens go?"*
- ❌ **If "516" (dropped a carry):** forgot to add the carried ten → **fix (KB):** "Note the carry above the next column and add it in — check against your estimate."
- ❌ **If digits misaligned:** place-value slip → **fix:** "Keep ones under ones, tens under tens on the columned paper."
### ⏱ 22–25 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** 246×3, 158×4 — estimate, then compute, then check against the estimate. Once secure, interleave a 3-digit × 1-digit with two carries.
**Watch for:** lost carries; answer far from estimate (a flag).
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — the carry; 3) drop to a 2-digit ×1; 4) hint: "small 1 above the next column"; 5) re-ask 23×3; 6) "Estimate catches slips — let's check it."
### ⏱ 25–28 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Why do we estimate before we multiply?" → *listen for:* "to check the answer is sensible / catch slips."
**Process praise:** "You carried the ten and your answer landed right by your estimate — careful work."
**Revisit later:** column × feeds Lesson 9 fluency and Unit 8 reasoning.
---
**Differentiation** — support: note the carry above the next column. / stretch: a 3-digit × 1-digit with two carries.
**Extension (if time allows):** make up a multiplication whose answer is between 700 and 800.
**Assessment evidence:** child multiplies 2/3-digit × 1-digit with correct carrying and checks against an estimate.

## Lesson 8 — Short division (bus stop)
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Multiplication, division and tables fluency)** | **Duration:** 28 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide using short division, carrying remainders."
**You will need:** columned paper, pencils.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** tables to 12 and their division facts; the inverse (×/÷).
**Key vocabulary:** **short division**, **bus stop**, **remainder**, **carry**, **estimate**.
**What to emphasize:** divide **left to right**; **carry the remainder** to the next digit; **check by multiplying back**. Pre-empt ignoring the remainder.

---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "**84 cards shared among 6** friends — how many each, fairly? We'll use the **bus stop** method to share it out, digit by digit."
**Show:** 96 ÷ 4 drawn under the bus-stop bracket.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's 8 ÷ 4?" → *listen for:* "2."
### ⏱ 3–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Number goes **under the bus stop**, divide **left to right**. 96 ÷ 4: first **9 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 1** — write 2 on top, **carry the leftover 1** to the next digit, making **16**. Then **16 ÷ 4 = 4** — write 4. Answer **24**. The remainder is the bit people forget — it *travels* to the next digit. Check: 24 × 4 = 96. ✓"
**Show / Do:** model 96 ÷ 4, writing the carried remainder small in front of the 6.
**Emphasize:** "**Carry the remainder** to make the next digit bigger."
### ⏱ 11–18 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's do **84 ÷ 6** together. 8 ÷ 6 = ? remainder?"
**Do:** child: 8÷6 = 1 r2 (carry 2 → 24), 24÷6 = 4 → 14.
**Ask:** "84 ÷ 6 = ?" → *listen for:* "14."
### ⏱ 18–22 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "84 ÷ 6 = ? — prove it by multiplying back."
- ✅ **Expected:** 14; 14 × 6 = 84. — probe: *"What did you do with the leftover 2?"*
- ❌ **If "12" (ignored remainder):** treated digits separately → **fix (KB):** "Carry the leftover 2 to the next digit — 8÷6 leaves 2, so it's 24, not 4."
- ❌ **If can't start (8÷6):** recall gap → **fix:** "How many 6s in 8? One, with 2 left over."
### ⏱ 22–25 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** 96÷4, 132÷6 — estimate first, divide, then check by multiplying back. Once secure, interleave a 3-digit ÷ 1-digit with a remainder in the answer.
**Watch for:** dropped remainders; not checking.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — the remainder; 3) use counters to share one digit; 4) hint: "leftover travels right"; 5) re-ask 48÷4; 6) "Multiply back to check — does it return the start?"

## Lesson 10 — Tenths and hundredths

---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK

**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's a tenth of something — one part out of how many?" → *listen for:* "ten."
### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Past the point, places keep **dividing by ten**: first **tenths**, then **hundredths**. **Show / Do:** shade 7 columns = 0.7; shade 3 squares = 0.03.
**Emphasize:** "**Tenths then hundredths** — 0.1 = 1/10, 0.01 = 1/100."
### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)

**Do:** child shades 7 columns, writes 7/10; then shades 0.5 and names ½.
**Ask:** "Write 7/10 as a decimal." → *listen for:* "0.7."
### ⏱ 16–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Write 7/10 as a decimal, and 3/100 as a decimal."
- ✅ **Expected:** 0.7 and 0.03. — probe: *"Why is 3/100 = 0.03, not 0.3?"* (hundredths → two places).
- ❌ **If writes 3/100 as 0.3:** confused place → **fix (KB):** "Shade 3 little squares — that's hundredths, the *second* place: 0.03."
- ❌ **If thinks 0.5 < 0.25:** whole-number thinking → **fix (KB):** "0.5 = 0.50 = 50 hundredths; that's more than 25."
### ⏱ 20–23 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Convert tenths/hundredths between fraction and decimal; shade to match. Once secure, interleave 0.25 = ¼.
**Watch for:** tenths/hundredths place mix-ups.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — which place; 3) shade the 100-square; 4) hint: "column = tenth, square = hundredth"; 5) re-ask 0.4; 6) "Count the columns — that's the tenths."
### ⏱ 23–26 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "On the 100-square, what's one column worth? One little square?" → *listen for:* "0.1; 0.01."
**Process praise:** "You shaded the hundredths to *prove* 0.03 — that's understanding, not guessing."
**Revisit later:** decimals continue in Lessons 11–12 and link to fractions in Unit 4.

## Lesson 11 — Dividing by 10 and 100
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Decimals: tenths and hundredths)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can divide by 10 and 100 by moving the digits, not the point."
**You will need:** a place-value chart (Th H T O · t h), digit cards.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** tenths/hundredths (Lesson 10); place value.
**Key vocabulary:** **divide by 10/100**, **place**, **shift right**, **the point stays**.
**What to emphasize:** **÷10 moves every digit ONE place right**, **÷100 moves TWO**; the **digits move, the point stays**. Pre-empt "4 ÷ 10 = 0.04".

---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Where does the **4** go when you share it among **10**? It gets *smaller* — but by exactly how much? Watch the digits slide."
**Show:** a place-value chart with the columns labelled.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Is 4 ÷ 10 bigger or smaller than 4?" → *listen for:* "smaller."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "**Dividing by 10 moves every digit ONE place to the right.** Put 4 in the ones; ÷10 slides it into the **tenths** → **0.4**. 36 ÷ 10: the 3 slides to ones, the 6 to tenths → **3.6**. **÷100 moves TWO places**: 36 → 0.36. The **point never moves — the digits do**. The classic mistake is 4 ÷ 10 = 0.04 — that's moving *two* places; ÷10 is only *one*."
**Show / Do:** slide digit cards one column right on the chart.
**Emphasize:** "**Digits move; the point stays. ÷10 = one place.**"
### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Slide **26 ÷ 10** with me — each digit one place right."
**Do:** child slides 2→ones, 6→tenths → 2.6; tries 36 ÷ 100.
**Ask:** "What is 26 ÷ 10?" → *listen for:* "2.6."
### ⏱ 14–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "26 ÷ 10 = ? and 4 ÷ 10 = ?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 2.6 and 0.4. — probe: *"How many places did each digit move?"* (one).
- ❌ **If "0.04" for 4÷10:** moved two places → **fix (KB):** "÷10 is *one* place right: 4 → 0.4. Two places is ÷100."
- ❌ **If moves the point not the digits:** rule confusion → **fix:** "The point stays put — slide the *digits* on the chart."
### ⏱ 18–21 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** ÷10 and ÷100 on a place-value chart (e.g. 7÷10, 80÷10, 45÷100). Once secure, interleave a ÷100 like 36÷100.
**Watch for:** two-place slips for ÷10; moving the point.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — number of places; 3) physically slide a digit card; 4) hint: "÷10 = one hop right"; 5) re-ask 5÷10; 6) "One ten, one hop."
### ⏱ 21–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "How many places does each digit move when I divide by 10? By 100?" → *listen for:* "one; two."
**Process praise:** "You slid the digits, not the point — and kept ÷10 to exactly one place."
**Revisit later:** ÷10/÷100 supports comparing decimals (Lesson 12) and measures.
---
**Differentiation** — support: a place-value chart to slide digits. / stretch: 36 ÷ 100.
**Extension (if time allows):** what is 360 ÷ 100? (3.6) — explain the slide.
**Assessment evidence:** child divides by 10/100 by shifting digits the correct number of places.

## Lesson 12 — Comparing and ordering decimals

### ⏱ 13–17 min · CHECK

- ✅ **Expected:** 0.3; 0.30 = 30 hundredths > 25. — probe: *"Why did you add the zero?"*
- ❌ **If "0.25 (more digits)":** compared length not value → **fix (KB):** "Equalise: 0.3 = 0.30; 30 hundredths beats 25."

**Watch for:** the "longer = bigger" trap.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — places not equal; 3) write trailing zeros; 4) hint: "compare tenths first"; 5) re-ask 0.6 vs 0.45; 6) "Make the places match, then compare."
### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What's the trick when one decimal has more digits than the other?" → *listen for:* "add a trailing zero so the places match."
**Process praise:** "You beat the 0.45-vs-0.5 trap by equalising the places — that's careful place-value thinking."

## Lesson 14 — Fractions of an amount
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 4 (Fractions: equivalence and amounts)** | **Duration:** 24 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can find a fraction of an amount by dividing by the bottom and multiplying by the top."
**You will need:** counters, sharing mats, mini-whiteboards.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** equivalence (Lesson 13); division; numerator/denominator.
**Key vocabulary:** **fraction of an amount**, **divide by the bottom**, **times by the top**, **improper**, **mixed number**.
**What to emphasize:** **divide by the denominator, multiply by the numerator**; deal into the denominator's piles first. Pre-empt dividing by the *numerator*.

---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "**⅗ of a team of 20 players** — how many is that? We need a fraction *of* a number. Let's deal the players into groups."
**Show:** 20 counters and a 5-pile sharing mat.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "The 5 on the bottom — how many equal piles?" → *listen for:* "five."
### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Rule: **divide by the bottom, times by the top.** ⅗ of 20: the **bottom is 5**, so share 20 into 5 piles — **20 ÷ 5 = 4** in each (that's *one* fifth). The **top is 3**, so take **3** piles — **4 × 3 = 12**. So ⅗ of 20 = **12**. The classic slip is dividing by the *top* (the 3) — but the **denominator** tells you how many piles."
**Show / Do:** deal 20 counters into 5 piles of 4; take 3 piles = 12.
**Emphasize:** "**Divide by the bottom, multiply by the top.**"
### ⏱ 10–15 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "⅔ of 12 with me — share 12 into how many piles? Then take how many?"

**Ask:** "⅔ of 12 = ?" → *listen for:* "8."
### ⏱ 15–19 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "⅔ of 12 = ? — talk me through it."
- ✅ **Expected:** 8; 12 ÷ 3 = 4, × 2 = 8. — probe: *"Why divide by 3 first?"* (bottom = piles).
- ❌ **If "6" (divided by 2, the top):** wrong number → **fix (KB):** "Divide by the *bottom* (3) to make the piles, then take the top (2)."
- ❌ **If gives 4 (one third only):** forgot the numerator → **fix:** "Take *two* of the piles — the top is 2."
### ⏱ 19–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Find ¾ of 12, ⅖ of 20, ⅔ of 9 — deal counters first, then abstract. Once secure, interleave ¾ + 2/4 = 5/4 = 1¼ (improper → mixed).
**Watch for:** dividing by the numerator; stopping at one part.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — which number divides; 3) deal counters into piles; 4) hint: "bottom = piles, top = how many you keep"; 5) re-ask ½ of 8; 6) "Bottom first, then top."
### ⏱ 22–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Say the rule for a fraction of an amount." → *listen for:* "divide by the bottom, times by the top."
**Process praise:** "You dealt the counters into the *bottom* number of piles — that's why you didn't fall for dividing by the top."
**Revisit later:** Unit 4 check and Lesson 15 fluency; reused in problem-solving.
---
**Differentiation** — support: deal into the denominator's piles first. / stretch: convert an improper answer to a mixed number.
**Extension (if time allows):** ⅗ of 30 (= 18) — show the piles.
**Assessment evidence:** child finds a fraction of an amount by ÷ denominator, × numerator.

## Lesson 17 — Area = length × width; perimeter together
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 5 (Area and perimeter)** | **Duration:** 26 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can calculate area with length × width and keep it separate from perimeter."
**You will need:** cm grid paper, a ruler, labelled rectangles.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** area by counting (Lesson 16); perimeter; multiplication.
**Key vocabulary:** **area**, **length × width**, **perimeter**, **cm²** vs **cm**, **missing side**.
**What to emphasize:** **area = length × width (cm²)**; **perimeter = add the sides (cm)** — keep them **separate**, with **different units**. Pre-empt confusing the two.

---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "**Fence for the garden, turf for the inside** — which is which? The fence is the **perimeter**; the turf is the **area**. Today we *calculate* both."
**Show:** a 5 cm × 3 cm labelled rectangle on grid paper.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Yesterday we counted squares for area — is there a quicker way for a rectangle?" → *listen for:* "multiply / rows × columns."
### ⏱ 3–10 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Counting squares is slow, so for a rectangle: **area = length × width**. This 5 by 3: 5 × 3 = **15 cm²** — the same as counting all 15 squares, but faster. **Perimeter is different — add all the sides**: 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = **16 cm**. Notice the **units**: area is **cm²** (squares), perimeter is **cm** (a length). They answer *different* questions — turf vs fence — so keep them apart."
**Show / Do:** compute area 15 cm² and perimeter 16 cm side by side.
**Emphasize:** "**Area = length × width (cm²). Perimeter = add the sides (cm).**"
### ⏱ 10–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "A 6 cm × 4 cm rectangle — area first (length × width), then perimeter (add the sides)."
**Do:** child: area 6×4 = 24 cm²; perimeter 6+4+6+4 = 20 cm.
**Ask:** "Area of the 6 × 4? Perimeter?" → *listen for:* "24 cm²; 20 cm."
### ⏱ 16–20 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Area and perimeter of a 6 cm × 4 cm rectangle — with units."
- ✅ **Expected:** 24 cm² and 20 cm. — probe: *"Why is one cm² and the other cm?"*
- ❌ **If adds the sides for area:** confused the two → **fix (KB):** "Area = fill inside (× ); perimeter = walk the edge (+). Different units."
- ❌ **If forgets the ² (says 24 cm):** unit slip → **fix:** "It's *squares* covering the inside → cm²."
### ⏱ 20–23 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Area + perimeter of labelled rectangles, units each time. Once secure, interleave a missing-side problem: area 12 cm², one side 3 → other side 4.
**Watch for:** swapping area/perimeter; dropping the ².
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — which is which; 3) shade inside (area) / trace edge (perimeter); 4) hint: "× for inside, + for around"; 5) re-ask a 2×3; 6) "Turf or fence?"
### ⏱ 23–26 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Area uses which operation and which unit? Perimeter?" → *listen for:* "× and cm²; + and cm."
**Process praise:** "You kept area and perimeter separate *and* used the right units — that's the part most people muddle."
**Revisit later:** Unit 5 check and Lesson 24 (area in a real plan).
---
**Differentiation** — support: perimeter = walk the edge, area = fill inside; different units. / stretch: "area 12 cm², one side 3 — find the other."

**Assessment evidence:** child calculates area (length × width) and perimeter with correct units, distinguishing them.

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK

## Lesson 19 — Translating shapes
**Maths · Year 4 · Unit 6 (Coordinates and translation)** | **Duration:** 22 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can translate a point or shape by a given number of squares."
**You will need:** coordinate grids, cut-out shapes, counters.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** plotting coordinates (Lesson 18, x then y).
**Key vocabulary:** **translation**, **slide**, **right/left**, **up/down**, **same size and turn**.
**What to emphasize:** translation **slides every point the same way** — **no turning, no resizing**. Pre-empt thinking translation rotates or resizes.

---
### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "**Slide the game piece 3 right and 2 up** — where does it land? That sliding move is called a **translation**. No spinning, no growing — just sliding."
**Show:** a shape on a grid and an arrow showing the slide.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Plot (4, 1) — how far across, how far up?" → *listen for:* "4 across, 1 up."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **translation slides a shape** a set number of squares — every point moves **the same direction and distance**. It does **not turn or resize** the shape. Watch: translate **(4, 1) by 2 left and 3 up** — across: 4 − 2 = 2; up: 1 + 3 = 4 → new point **(2, 4)**. For a whole shape, every corner shifts the same way, so it looks identical, just *moved*."
**Show / Do:** slide the cut-out shape 2 left, 3 up; show it's unchanged in size/turn.
**Emphasize:** "Translation = **slide only** — same size, same way up."
### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Translate **(1, 1) by 3 right and 1 up** with me — change across, then up."
**Do:** child: 1+3 = 4 across, 1+1 = 2 up → (4, 2).
**Ask:** "Translate (1, 1) by 3 right, 1 up." → *listen for:* "(4, 2)."
### ⏱ 14–17 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Translate (1, 1) by 3 right, 1 up — and does the shape turn?"
- ✅ **Expected:** (4, 2); no, translation only slides. — probe: *"What changed and what stayed the same?"*
- ❌ **If turns/resizes the shape:** confused with rotation → **fix (KB):** "Translation only *slides* — every point moves the same; no turning."
- ❌ **If adds to the wrong coordinate:** axis mix-up → **fix:** "Right/left changes the *x*; up/down changes the *y*."
### ⏱ 17–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Translate points and a small shape by given amounts; move each vertex one at a time. Once secure, interleave describing the slide between two given shapes.
**Watch for:** rotating the shape; wrong axis.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — slide vs turn; 3) physically slide the cut-out; 4) hint: "every point the same way"; 5) re-ask "1 right" only; 6) "Just slide it — don't spin it."
### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "What does a translation do — and not do?" → *listen for:* "slides it; doesn't turn or resize it."
**Process praise:** "You moved every point the same way and kept the shape the same — proper translation."
**Revisit later:** Unit 6 check and Year-end review (Lesson 25).
---
**Differentiation** — support: move each vertex one at a time. / stretch: describe the translation between two given shapes.
**Extension (if time allows):** translate a shape so it lands in a new corner of the grid; name the new vertices.
**Assessment evidence:** child translates points/shapes by a given slide without rotating or resizing.

### ⏱ 17–20 min · PRACTICE (you do)

### ⏱ 20–22 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "How do you read a value off a line graph?" → *listen for:* "up from the time, across to the value."
**Process praise:** "You traced up-then-across *and* checked the scale — that's how you read graphs accurately."
**Revisit later:** line graphs return in Lesson 22 fluency and Lesson 24 (a plant's growth).

### ⏱ 0–3 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "**Plan and measure a garden, then chart its growth.** One scenario, three skills — area, coordinates and a graph. Let's design it."
**Show:** a 7 × 4 plot drawn on grid paper, plus a plant-height graph.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "For 'how much turf', do I need area or perimeter?" → *listen for:* "area."
### ⏱ 3–9 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Each question needs the **right tool**. 'How big is the plot?' → **area = length × width** = 7 × 4 = **28 cm²**. 'Where are its corners?' → **coordinates** (across then up). 'How fast did the plant grow?' → **read the line graph** (up from the week, across to the height). I match each question to its method before I start — that's the planning."
**Show / Do:** compute area; mark a corner as a coordinate; read a graph value.
**Emphasize:** "**Match each question to the right method** — then combine."
### ⏱ 9–14 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Together: area of this **7 × 4** plot? Then name a corner as a coordinate. Then read the plant's height at week 3."
**Do:** child: 7 × 4 = 28 cm²; gives a corner coordinate; reads a graph value.
**Ask:** "Area of a 7 cm × 4 cm plot?" → *listen for:* "28 cm²."
### ⏱ 14–18 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Area of the 7 × 4 plot, with units?"
- ✅ **Expected:** 28 cm². — probe: *"Which method did this part need, and why?"*
- ❌ **If adds the sides (gets 22):** used perimeter → **fix (KB):** "Area fills inside — *multiply* length × width; perimeter is for the fence."
- ❌ **If drops the ²:** unit slip → **fix:** "Squares cover a surface → cm²."
### ⏱ 18–22 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** A combined garden scenario — area, a coordinate, a graph reading. Support: grid and ruler to hand. Stretch: a problem needing both area and a coordinate to answer.
**Watch for:** using the wrong method for a part; unit slips.
> **If the child is stuck:** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — wrong tool; 3) ask "which skill does this part need?"; 4) one hint; 5) re-ask the part alone; 6) "Match the question to the method."
### ⏱ 22–24 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Name the three skills you combined today." → *listen for:* "area, coordinates, line graph."
**Process praise:** "You matched each part of the problem to the right method — that's real mathematical thinking."
**Revisit later:** these combined skills appear in the Lesson 25 challenge circuit.

## End-of-year mastery checks

> Use these as a final, low-stakes tour of the year. A child secure in Year 4 maths can:

1. **Place value to 10,000** — partition (6,209 → 6000 + 200 + 0 + 9) and compare four-digit numbers (7,180 > 7,108).
2. **Rounding & number systems** — round to 10/100/1000 (4,350 → 4,000 to nearest 1000), compare negatives (−7 colder than −3), read Roman numerals (XL → 40).
3. **Tables to 12 × 12** — recall any fact quickly both ways and as division (7 × 8 → 56; 72 ÷ 12 → 6).
4. **Written × and ÷** — column-multiply (134 × 4 → 536) and short-divide (84 ÷ 6 → 14), estimating first.
5. **Decimals** — read tenths/hundredths (7/10 → 0.7), divide by 10/100 (26 ÷ 10 → 2.6), compare decimals (0.3 > 0.25).
6. **Fractions** — make equivalents (¾ = 6/8) and find a fraction of an amount (⅔ of 12 → 8).
7. **Area & perimeter** — find area (6 × 4 → 24 cm²) and perimeter (→ 20 cm), keeping the units distinct.
8. **Coordinates & data** — plot/translate coordinates ((1,1) → (4,2) by 3 right, 1 up) and read line graphs and two-way tables.

**For each:** ask the sample question; if secure, move on; if not, return to that lesson's CHECK and its KB wrong-answer fixes, then re-ask a smaller version. Record secure / developing / needs revisiting for the next year's starters and spaced review.
