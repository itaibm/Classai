# Year 4 History — Tutor Scripts (Lessons 1–11)

Complete, minute-by-minute scripts for [Year 4 History](../history-year-4.md), built to the [scripting guide](../../../SCRIPTING-GUIDE.md) and grounded in [`knowledge-base/subjects/history-geography/history-ks2.md`](../../../knowledge-base/subjects/history-geography/history-ks2.md). Every beat ⏱ sums to the lesson's duration. History lessons lead with **story + sources** ("how do we know?") and the disciplinary questions of KS2: **cause & consequence** ("what changed, and why?"), **significance** ("why does it matter?"), and now **source reliability and fairness** ("can we trust this — is it fair to both sides?"). Each lesson gives the tutor verbatim Say / Show / Do / Ask, a real check with wrong-answer fixes (pulled from the KB misconception lists), the mandatory "if the child is stuck" protocol, what to emphasize, differentiation, extension and assessment evidence.

**How to use:** read the "What to emphasize" box first, lay out the materials, and follow the timed beats. A Year 4 child (ages 8–9) can handle more abstract reasoning and longer independent practice — push the "how do you know?" probe every lesson.

**Three units, 11 lessons:** Unit 1 Ancient Greece (L1–4) · Unit 2 The Romans and Britain (L5–8) · Unit 3 Chronology (L9–11).

# Unit 1 — Ancient Greece

**Essential question:** How can a civilisation from 2,500 years ago still shape the way we live today?

---

## Lesson 1 — Not one country: the Greek city-states
**History · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Ancient Greece)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what a city-state was and name two famous Greek ones."

**You will need:** the long timeline (left of the Romans, right of Egypt), a map of Ancient Greece (showing scattered cities and islands), city-state cards (Athens, Sparta, Corinth, Thebes…), a globe or world map.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Ancient Egypt (~5,000 years ago, an early civilisation) and the idea of BC dates from Year 3.
**Key vocabulary:** **city-state** ("an independent city that ran like its own little country"), **Athens**, **Sparta**, **BC**, **legacy**, **gods**, **Olympic Games**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Ancient Greece was **not one country with one king** — it was **dozens of independent city-states** (Athens, Sparta…) that often fought each other but **shared** a language, the same gods and the Olympic Games. Pin it on the timeline: ~2,500 years ago (BC), **after** Egypt, **before** the Romans.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's something strange. Ancient Greece wasn't one country with one king — it was made of dozens of tiny separate countries, and they often *fought each other*. Imagine if every big city near you — was its own country, with its own army! How on earth did that work? Let's find out."
**Show:** the map of Ancient Greece, lots of scattered cities and islands.  **Do:** sweep your hand over the many separate dots.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Last year you met Ancient Egypt — was that recent, or thousands of years ago? Was it written with BC or AD?" → *listen for:* "thousands of years ago / BC."
*(If BC is shaky: BC means the years that count *down* toward zero — the bigger the BC number, the further back. We'll master it later this year.)*

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Watch me place Greece on our timeline. Here's Egypt — really old, about 5,000 years ago. Now come forward… here's Ancient Greece, about **2,500 years ago**, written **BC**. And further along — the Romans, who come later. So the order is Egypt, then Greece, then Rome. Now the big idea. Greece was **not one country**. It was lots of **city-states** — a city-state is an independent city that ran like its own little country, with its own rulers and its own army. Two famous ones: **Athens** and **Sparta**. They often quarrelled — but they *shared* three things: the same **language**, the same **gods** (Zeus, Athena, Poseidon), and the **Olympic Games**, a sports festival every four years. Yes — that's where our Olympics began!"
**Show / Do:** pin "Ancient Greece ~2,500 years ago" between Egypt and Rome; lay Athens and Sparta cards on the map; circle Greece on the globe.
**Emphasize:** "Not one country with one king — many **city-states** that fought but shared a language, gods and the Olympics."

### ⏱ 12–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's build the picture together. I'll hand you the city-state cards. Place Athens and Sparta on the map. Now — were they one country, or separate? And tell me one thing all the city-states shared even though they fought."
**Do:** child places the cards, says "separate," and names a shared thing.
**Ask:** "So if Athens and Sparta fought each other but spoke the same language and prayed to the same gods — what's the word for one of those independent cities?" → *listen for:* "a city-state."

### ⏱ 20–28 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What is a *city-state*, and name two famous Greek ones?"
- ✅ **Expected:** an independent city that ran like its own country; **Athens** and **Sparta** — then probe: *"If they were separate countries, how come we still call them all 'Greek'?"* (shared language, gods, Olympics).
- ❌ **If "Greece was one big country with a king" (KB misconception):** today's Greece is one nation, so the past is assumed the same → **fix:** "Look at the map — how many separate cities? Each ran itself. There was no one king of all Greece — they were many city-states."
- ❌ **If names a god or a place that isn't a city-state (e.g. "Zeus", "the Olympics"):** muddling shared culture with the cities → **fix:** "Good — that's something they *shared*. But I want the name of an independent *city*. Point to Athens; now find one more."

### ⏱ 28–35 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child writes/says one sentence: "A city-state is an independent city that ran like its own country, such as Athens and Sparta." Then sorts a small pile of cards into "a city-state" (Athens, Sparta, Corinth) vs "something they shared" (the gods, the Olympics, the language). Once secure, interleave: "Put Egypt, Greece and the Romans in time order."
**Watch for:** the "one country, one king" slip; confusing shared culture with the cities.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — one-country misconception vs city/culture mix-up; 3) point along the map (separate dots) or timeline; 4) give ONE hint: "Each city had its own army — so it was its own…?"; 5) re-ask just "name one famous Greek city"; 6) keep it safe — "Mistakes show us what to practise. Let's take a smaller step."

### ⏱ 35–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me what a city-state was, name two, and place Greece between Egypt and the Romans on the line." → *listen for:* "independent city like its own country; Athens and Sparta; Greece after Egypt, before Rome."
**Process praise:** "You spotted that 'Greek' didn't mean one country — that's exactly how a historian reads a map."
**Revisit later:** tomorrow we go inside two of those city-states and compare them — Athens vs Sparta. Bring back "city-state" in a week.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match the "Athens" and "Sparta" cards to dots on the map; say "city-state" aloud.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain what the city-states *shared* even though they fought — and why that still lets us call them all "Greek."

**Extension (if time allows):** invent a name and one rule for your own imaginary Greek city-state, and mark where on the map it would sit.
**Assessment evidence:** child defines "city-state," names Athens and Sparta, and places Greece correctly (after Egypt, before Rome).

## Lesson 2 — Athens vs Sparta
**History · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Ancient Greece)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name one big difference between Athens and Sparta."

**You will need:** a two-column comparison mat (ATHENS | SPARTA), statement cards ("loved books, art and theatre", "trained boys as soldiers from childhood", "invented democracy", "valued toughness above all", "famous for ideas and learning", "famous for its army"), images of an Athenian theatre and a Spartan soldier.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** city-states; that Athens and Sparta were two famous ones (Lesson 1).
**Key vocabulary:** **Athens**, **Sparta**, **values** ("what a place cares about most"), **trade-off** ("what you give up to get something"), **democracy** (preview).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** this is a **similarity & difference** study. **Athens** prized **learning, art, theatre, ideas and democracy**; **Sparta** prized **war, toughness and soldiering**. Same era, same language, same gods — but they **valued opposite things**, and each gave something up for it (a trade-off).

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Two cities, both Greek. In one, children went to school to learn poetry, music and big ideas, and grown-ups packed into theatres. In the other, boys were taken from home young and trained to be soldiers — toughness mattered more than anything. Which would *you* rather grow up in? Hold that thought."
**Show:** side by side — the Athenian theatre image and the Spartan soldier image.  **Do:** point to each as you describe it.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What's the word for one of those independent Greek cities, and name the two we're comparing?" → *listen for:* "city-states; Athens and Sparta."

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Both Athens and Sparta were Greek city-states — same language, same gods. But they cared about *opposite* things. **Athens** loved **learning, art, theatre and ideas** — it's where clever thinkers asked big questions, and it even invented **democracy**, where people voted. **Sparta** was the opposite: a tough warrior city. Boys started soldier training as young children; being strong and brave in battle mattered most. Here's the historian's idea — every choice has a **trade-off**. Sparta got the best army in Greece, but gave up art and easy living. Athens got brilliant ideas and theatre, but its soldiers weren't as feared as Sparta's."
**Show / Do:** on the comparison mat, model placing two cards — "loved books and theatre → Athens", "trained boys as soldiers → Sparta" — thinking aloud as you decide.
**Emphasize:** "Athens = learning, art, **democracy**. Sparta = war and toughness. Same era — opposite values."

### ⏱ 12–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn to sort with me. Take this card — 'famous for its army.' Athens or Sparta? Why? Now this one — 'invented democracy.' Where does it go?"
**Do:** child places each statement card in the right column and gives a one-word reason.
**Ask:** "What did Athens value that Sparta didn't care much about?" → *listen for:* "learning / art / ideas / democracy."

### ⏱ 20–28 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Name one big difference between Athens and Sparta."
- ✅ **Expected:** Athens valued **learning, art and democracy**; Sparta valued **war and toughness** — then probe: *"What did each city give up to be good at that?"* (the trade-off).
- ❌ **If "they were exactly the same / both just Greek":** missing the difference → **fix:** "Yes, both Greek — but look at the two columns. One trained soldiers; the other built theatres. Read me one card from each side."
- ❌ **If swaps them (e.g. "Sparta invented democracy"):** mixed-up values → **fix:** "Let's check the soldier image — which city trained boys for war? So democracy and theatre belong to…?"

### ⏱ 28–35 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child sorts a fresh set of statements into Athens/Sparta unaided, then writes one full-sentence difference ("Athens valued learning and democracy, but Sparta valued war and toughness"). Once secure, interleave: "Both cities were Greek — name one thing they *shared*" (language / gods / Olympics — links to Lesson 1).
**Watch for:** swapping the two; saying "same" with no difference; forgetting they still shared a culture.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — values swapped or no contrast; 3) put the theatre and soldier images side by side; 4) ONE hint: "Which city is famous for its *army*?"; 5) re-ask "Athens loved ___ ; Sparta loved ___"; 6) "smaller step — just sort two cards."

### ⏱ 35–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the one big difference between Athens and Sparta — and now finish your hook answer: which would you choose, and why?" → *listen for:* a clear difference plus a reasoned choice.
**Process praise:** "You didn't just say 'different' — you said *how* and *why*. That's real comparing."
**Revisit later:** tomorrow we dig into Athens's biggest gift — democracy — and an honest catch about it. Revisit Athens/Sparta in spaced review.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** a two-column sort with picture support and only four obvious cards.
- **Stretch (challenge):** argue which city-state you'd choose to be a citizen of, naming the trade-off you'd accept.

**Extension (if time allows):** write a tiny diary line as an Athenian child *and* a Spartan child on the same day — show how different the two days are.
**Assessment evidence:** child sorts statements correctly and states one clear Athens/Sparta difference in a full sentence.

## Lesson 3 — Democracy is born in Athens
**History · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Ancient Greece)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain what democracy means and that it began in Athens — including its honest limits."

**You will need:** voting slips and a simple ballot box (or a show-of-hands setup), two real choices to vote on (e.g. story A or story B; playtime game), the Athens/Sparta mat from Lesson 2, a "who could vote?" card set (free adult man / woman / enslaved person / foreigner / child).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Athens valued ideas and "invented democracy" (Lesson 2).
**Key vocabulary:** **democracy** ("rule by the people — citizens vote on decisions"), **citizen**, **vote**, **fair / unfair**, **honest limit**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **democracy = rule by the people** (citizens vote), and it **began in Athens**. But be **even-handed and honest**: in Athens **only free adult male citizens** could vote — **women, enslaved people and foreigners could not**. It was a huge idea *and* unfair by today's standards. Both are true.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Imagine instead of one king deciding everything, *you and the people* got to vote on it. That world-changing idea started in Athens about 2,500 years ago. We're going to *do* it — hold a real vote in a minute. But here's the catch we'll uncover: back in Athens, not everyone was allowed to vote."
**Show:** the ballot box and two voting choices on the board.  **Do:** hold up the box invitingly.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Which city-state was famous for ideas and invented democracy — Athens or Sparta?" → *listen for:* "Athens."

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "**Democracy** comes from Greek words meaning **rule by the people**. Instead of one king choosing, the **citizens** voted on decisions — they put up their hands or dropped a token, and the most votes won. That began in **Athens**. Now the honest part, and a real historian tells *both* sides. In Athens, only **free adult male citizens** could vote. **Women** couldn't. **Enslaved people** couldn't. People born in another place — **foreigners** — couldn't. So it was a brilliant new idea *and* unfair compared with today, when nearly all grown-ups can vote. We hold both of those true at once."
**Show / Do:** write "democracy = rule by the people"; sort the "who could vote?" cards into ✅ (free adult man) and ❌ (woman, enslaved person, foreigner, child).
**Emphasize:** "Rule by the people — born in **Athens**. Honest limit: **only free adult men** voted."

### ⏱ 11–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do — the real vote)
> **Say:** "Let's feel democracy. Here are our two choices. We each vote — drop your slip in, and I'll drop mine. Now we count. The choice with the most votes wins — that's the people deciding, not one ruler. *That* is democracy."
**Do:** run the vote together (invite any family members nearby to add their votes), count aloud, announce the result. Then point to the cards: "If this were ancient Athens, who in your family would *not* have been allowed to vote?"
**Ask:** "What does the word democracy mean, and where was it born?" → *listen for:* "rule by the people; Athens."

### ⏱ 20–28 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "What does *democracy* mean, and which city-state began it?"
- ✅ **Expected:** **rule by the people** (citizens vote on decisions); it began in **Athens** — then probe: *"Was Athenian democracy fair to everyone? Who was left out?"*
- ❌ **If "democracy means everyone could vote" (KB misconception):** reading it as today's version → **fix:** "Careful — in Athens only free adult *men* could. Look at the ❌ cards: who's left out?"
- ❌ **If "a king decided / it's the same as one ruler":** opposite of democracy → **fix:** "We just voted — did one person decide, or the most votes? So democracy is rule by…?"

### ⏱ 28–35 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child completes two sentences: "Democracy means ___ and it began in ___." and "It was unfair because ___ could not vote." Once secure, interleave: "Name one way today's voting is fairer than Athens's."
**Watch for:** "everyone could vote"; forgetting it was Athens specifically; missing the honest limit.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — over-claiming fairness or wrong city; 3) point to the ✅/❌ cards; 4) ONE hint: "We voted by hands up — so the *people* decided, not a…?"; 5) re-ask just "democracy means rule by the ___"; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 35–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me what democracy means, where it began, and one honest thing that was unfair about it." → *listen for:* "rule by the people; Athens; women/enslaved people/foreigners couldn't vote."
**Process praise:** "You held *both* truths — a great idea *and* an unfair one. That's grown-up, fair-minded history."
**Revisit later:** tomorrow — all the other gifts Greece left us (the legacy). Revisit "democracy" in spaced review and again before BC/AD in Unit 3.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** vote in a simple show of hands with the tutor and say "democracy means rule by the people."
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain *how* Athenian democracy was unfair compared with today, and why that doesn't cancel out how important the idea was.

**Extension (if time allows):** design a fairer "voting rule" for your family and explain who it includes that Athens left out.
**Assessment evidence:** child defines democracy, names Athens, and names at least one group excluded from the Athenian vote.

## Lesson 4 — The legacy of Greece
**History · Year 4 · Unit 1 (Ancient Greece)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name three things from Ancient Greece we still have today."

**You will need:** legacy cards (Olympic Games, democracy/voting, maths & science [Pythagoras, Archimedes], theatre, myths, Greek words like *democracy*/*politics*), Olympic images then-and-now, a short illustrated Greek myth (e.g. Theseus & the Minotaur, the Trojan Horse), the Athens/democracy work from Lessons 2–3.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** democracy began in Athens (Lesson 3); the Olympics and gods were shared by all city-states (Lesson 1).
**Key vocabulary:** **legacy** ("things from the past we still use today"), **significance** ("why it matters"), **Olympic Games**, **philosophy**, **myth**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** this is the **significance** lesson — a 2,500-year-old civilisation still shapes our world. The **legacy**: the **Olympics**, **democracy/voting**, early **maths & science** (Pythagoras, Archimedes), **theatre**, and **myths** — plus Greek **words** we still use (*democracy*, *politics*). And the honest note: Greek **myths were their real religion**, not "just fairy tales."

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's something amazing. The Olympics you watch on TV, the voting grown-ups do, even the word 'politics' — all of them are gifts from people who lived **2,500 years ago**. How can people that long ago still reach into *your* life today? That's what 'legacy' means — and we're going to collect Greece's gifts."
**Show:** an ancient Olympic image beside a modern one.  **Do:** point from old to new — "same idea, 2,500 years apart."
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What big idea did Athens give the world that we used in our vote?" → *listen for:* "democracy / voting."

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "A **legacy** is something from the past we still use today. Let me list Greece's. One — the **Olympic Games**, started ~2,500 years ago, still held now. Two — **democracy** and voting, from Athens. Three — early **maths and science**: thinkers like **Pythagoras** (the triangle rule) and **Archimedes**. Four — **theatre**, with comedies and tragedies. Five — their **myths**, like Heracles, the Minotaur and the Trojan Horse, which we still tell. We even use their **words** — *democracy* and *politics* are Greek! One honest thing: to the Greeks, those myths weren't just stories — they were their **real religion**, with temples and festivals."
**Show / Do:** lay out the legacy cards as you name each; hold up the myth booklet.
**Emphasize:** "**Legacy** = still here today. Olympics, democracy, maths/science, theatre, myths — and Greek words."

### ⏱ 11–19 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's sort together. I'll hand you cards — tell me which are 'from Ancient Greece, still here today.' Olympics? Yes. Voting? Yes. Now this myth booklet — and here's a treat, let's read a bit of the Minotaur story."
**Do:** child matches three legacy cards to "from Ancient Greece"; tutor reads a 1-minute slice of the myth.
**Ask:** "Pick one legacy and tell me *how* a 2,500-year-old idea still affects us." → *listen for:* e.g. "we still vote / still hold the Olympics / still learn Pythagoras."

### ⏱ 19–26 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Give two things from Ancient Greece we still have today."
- ✅ **Expected:** any two of — the **Olympic Games**, **democracy/voting**, **maths & science** ideas, **theatre**, **myths** — then probe: *"Why does that one count as a 'legacy'?"* (we still use it).
- ❌ **If "the Greek gods/myths were just made-up fairy tales they didn't believe" (KB misconception):** treating myths as our kind of story → **fix:** "To them, those were their **real religion** — temples, festivals, worship. We still *tell* the myths today, which is why they're part of the legacy."
- ❌ **If names something not Greek (e.g. "phones"):** confusing modern with legacy → **fix:** "Check our cards — which of these did *Greece* give us? Point to one and say how we still use it."

### ⏱ 26–31 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child lists **three** Greek legacies and, for one, writes a sentence on *how* it still matters ("We still hold the Olympic Games, an idea from Greece 2,500 years ago"). Once secure, interleave: "Name a Greek *word* we still use."
**Watch for:** the "just fairy tales" slip; listing modern items that aren't Greek; naming only one.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — modern-vs-legacy mix or myth misconception; 3) spread the legacy cards out; 4) ONE hint: "What sporting event began in Greece and we still watch?"; 5) re-ask "name just one Greek legacy"; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me three gifts Ancient Greece left us that we still have today — and why we call them a 'legacy.'" → *listen for:* three valid legacies + "because we still use them."
**Process praise:** "You connected the past to right now — that's understanding *why history matters*."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-Unit-1 check** next (city-states, Athens/Sparta, democracy, legacy). Then we leap forward to a conquering empire — the Romans.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** match three legacy items to "from Ancient Greece" with picture cards.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain how one 2,500-year-old idea still shapes life today, using "legacy" and "significance."

**Extension (if time allows):** make a tiny "Greek Gifts" poster with three legacies drawn and labelled.
**Assessment evidence:** child names three Greek legacies and explains why one counts as a lasting legacy.

---

### ✅ End-of-Unit 1 check — Ancient Greece (run after Lesson 4, ~10 min)
Ask the child to, in their own words:
1. **Explain a city-state and name two.** ✅ independent city like its own country; Athens and Sparta.
2. **Give one Athens/Sparta difference.** ✅ Athens = learning/art/democracy; Sparta = war/toughness.
3. **Define democracy and where it began — with its honest limit.** ✅ rule by the people, citizens vote; Athens; only free adult men voted.
4. **Name three Greek legacies.** ✅ any three of: Olympics, democracy, maths/science, theatre, myths.

**Misconception watch (KB):** Greece was *not* one country with one king · Athenian democracy was *not* for everyone · the myths were their *real religion*, not fairy tales.
**If any part fails:** re-run that lesson's CHECK with the relevant cards/map before moving to Unit 2.

# Unit 2 — The Romans and Their Impact on Britain

**Essential question:** What did the Romans change about Britain — and did everyone welcome them?

---

## Lesson 5 — Rome invades: AD 43
**History · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Romans)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can say when and from where the Romans invaded Britain and what an empire is."

**You will need:** the long timeline, a map showing Rome (Italy), the Mediterranean and Britain (and the wide Roman Empire), Iron Age Britain images (roundhouse, hillfort), a Roman legionary image, an "AD 43" arrow.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** Iron Age Britain from Year 3 (tribes, roundhouses, hillforts); the idea of an empire/legacy from Greece (Unit 1).
**Key vocabulary:** **empire** ("lots of different lands ruled by one power"), **invade**, **AD 43**, **legion / army**, **Roman**, **Italy**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** Rome was a huge **empire** already ruling lands round the Mediterranean; its superbly trained **army (legions)** **invaded Britain in AD 43**. Kill the KB misconception head-on: **Rome is in Italy — the Romans came from abroad**; "Roman Britain" does *not* mean Rome was British.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Picture Britain 2,000 years ago: tribes living in round wooden houses, hiding behind hilltop forts. Then one day, ships land — and out marches the most organised, best-trained army the world had ever seen, in shining armour, marching in perfect lines. They've come from far across the sea to conquer. What happens next?"
**Show:** the roundhouse/hillfort images, then the legionary image.  **Do:** mime a disciplined marching line.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From Year 3 — what was Britain like in the Iron Age, before the Romans? Did people live in cities and write things down?" → *listen for:* "tribes, roundhouses, hillforts; no big cities/writing."

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Rome was a city in **Italy** — across the sea, a long way from Britain. But Rome had grown into a huge **empire**: that means lots of different lands all ruled by one power. By now Rome already ruled lands all around the **Mediterranean** Sea. In **AD 43**, the Roman Empire **invaded** Britain — *invade* means to attack and take over a land. Their secret weapon was the **army**, split into **legions**: thousands of trained soldiers with armour, shields, tactics and iron discipline. Now — really important — Rome is in **Italy**. The Romans **came from abroad**. 'Roman Britain' does *not* mean Rome was British; it means Romans came over and ruled."
**Show / Do:** trace the journey on the map (Rome → across the sea → Britain); pin the "AD 43" arrow on the timeline, *after* Greece.
**Emphasize:** "**AD 43** · from **Rome in Italy** · a huge **empire** · they **invaded** (came from abroad)."

### ⏱ 12–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Show me on the map: point to Rome — what country? Now point to Britain. Did the Romans start in Britain, or come from far away? And what year did they invade?"
**Do:** child points to Italy and Britain, traces the sea crossing, says "AD 43."
**Ask:** "What's the difference between a single kingdom and an *empire*?" → *listen for:* "an empire rules *many* different lands, not just one."

### ⏱ 20–28 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "In what year did the Romans invade Britain, and where did they come from?"
- ✅ **Expected:** **AD 43**; from **Rome / Italy**, as part of the Roman **Empire** — then probe: *"What does 'empire' add that 'one kingdom' doesn't?"*
- ❌ **If "the Romans came from Britain / Rome is in Britain" (KB misconception):** "Roman Britain" makes Rome sound British → **fix:** "Find Rome on the map — what country? Italy! So the Romans came from…? They sailed *to* Britain to conquer it."
- ❌ **If "BC" or a wrong year:** date/era slip → **fix:** "Is the invasion before or after the year zero? It's **AD** — the years counting up. The year is 43, written **AD 43**."

### ⏱ 28–35 min · PRACTICE (you do)

## Lesson 10 — BC and AD: counting time both ways
**History · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Chronology)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can use BC and AD correctly and say which of two dates is older."

**You will need:** a BC/AD number line drawn across the board crossing a clear **0** (BC counting down to the left, AD counting up to the right), date cards (3000 BC, 1500 BC, 500 BC, 200 BC, AD 43, AD 1066), a pointer.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the period order (Lesson 9); that Egypt is "BC" and the Romans are "AD."
**Key vocabulary:** **BC** ("Before Christ — years count *down* toward 0"), **AD** ("the years counting *up* from then"), **0 / zero**, **older / newer**, **further back**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** the rule that trips everyone up — **BC counts *down* toward 0, so a bigger BC number is OLDER** (1500 BC is older than 200 BC). **AD counts *up*** from 0, so a bigger AD number is *newer*. Drill "which is older?" out loud. (Note for later: two peoples can live at the *same time* but far apart in *space* — a Year 5 set-up for the Maya.)

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Brain-twister time. Which is older — **2000 BC** or **500 BC**? Quick, no thinking too hard! …Most people — even grown-ups — get this wrong. By the end of today, you'll get it right *every* time and catch out the adults at home."
**Show:** the two date cards 2000 BC and 500 BC.  **Do:** hold them up like a quiz.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Was Ancient Egypt written BC or AD? Were the Romans BC or AD?" → *listen for:* "Egypt BC; Romans AD."

### ⏱ 4–12 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "Here's the secret. Picture a number line with **0** in the middle. **BC** means 'Before Christ' — those years count **down** toward 0, going *backwards*. So the **bigger** the BC number, the **further back**, the **older**. **3000 BC** is older than **500 BC**, because 3000 is further from 0 going back. Then **AD** — those years count **up** from 0, the normal way. So a bigger AD number is *newer*: AD 1066 is newer than AD 43. The trick to remember: **with BC, big = old; with AD, big = new.** BC goes down, AD goes up."
**Show / Do:** point along the line — start at 0, walk *left* through 500 BC… 1500 BC… 3000 BC ("further back = older"); then *right* through AD 43… AD 1066 ("further on = newer").
**Emphasize:** "**BC: bigger number = older.** AD: bigger number = newer. BC counts down to 0; AD counts up."

### ⏱ 12–22 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's order these together on the line: 3000 BC, 500 BC, AD 43, AD 1066. Put the *oldest* furthest left. Where does 3000 BC go? Where does AD 1066 go?"
**Do:** child places all four cards on the number line in order, saying each.
**Ask:** "Which is older — 1500 BC or 200 BC? Walk it on the line." → *listen for:* "1500 BC — it's a bigger BC number, further back."

### ⏱ 22–30 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Which is older, 1500 BC or 200 BC?"
- ✅ **Expected:** **1500 BC** (bigger BC number = further back = older) — then probe: *"How did you work that out?"* (BC counts down, so bigger = older).
- ❌ **If "200 BC, because 200 is smaller and small numbers come first" (KB misconception):** treating BC like normal counting up → **fix:** "Walk it on the line — BC goes *backwards* toward 0. So 1500 BC is *further back* than 200 BC. Bigger BC = older."
- ❌ **If muddles a BC with an AD (e.g. "AD 200 is older than 1500 BC"):** crossing the zero wrongly → **fix:** "Which side of 0 is BC? The *past* side. Anything BC is older than anything AD. So a BC date beats any AD date for 'older.'"

### ⏱ 30–35 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child answers four "which is older?" cards solo (e.g. 3000 BC vs 1000 BC; AD 43 vs AD 1066; 500 BC vs AD 43), saying the rule each time. Once secure, interleave: order a mixed set (1500 BC, 200 BC, AD 43, AD 1066) oldest → newest.
**Watch for:** treating BC like ordinary counting; crossing 0 the wrong way; forgetting all BC is older than all AD.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — BC-as-counting-up or zero-crossing slip; 3) walk the number line together; 4) ONE hint: "BC counts *down* — so which has the bigger number, and that means…?"; 5) re-ask with two BC dates only; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 35–40 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the BC rule in your own words, and which is older: 1500 BC or 200 BC." → *listen for:* "bigger BC number = older; 1500 BC."
**Process praise:** "You beat the trick that catches grown-ups — and you can *explain* why, not just guess."
**Revisit later:** tomorrow — using the timeline to work out who could have met whom. Revisit the BC rule often; it's easy to lose.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** order two BC dates using the number line, pointing as you go.
- **Stretch (challenge):** order a mixed BC/AD set correctly and explain why every BC date is older than every AD date.

**Extension (if time allows):** make a "Catch a Grown-Up" quiz card — two dates and the right answer hidden underneath.
**Assessment evidence:** child correctly identifies the older of two BC dates and explains the "bigger BC = older" rule.

## Lesson 11 — Who could have met whom?
**History · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Chronology)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can use the timeline to judge whether two peoples could have met."

**You will need:** the timeline (with all periods + BC/AD from Lessons 9–10), "could they meet?" question cards (e.g. *a Roman & a Viking? · a pharaoh & Stonehenge? · the Romans & Egypt's pyramids?*), a world map (for the time-vs-space point).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the period order (Lesson 9) and the BC/AD rule (Lesson 10).
**Key vocabulary:** **overlap** ("alive at the same time"), **centuries apart**, **same time ≠ same place**, **reason / justify**, **how do you know?**
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** use the timeline to **reason** about whether two peoples could meet, separating **time** (did they overlap?) from **space** (were they near enough?). Egypt's pyramids were *already ancient* when the Romans came (yes, much earlier). Tackle the KB misconception "same time = same place": two peoples can live at the same time but be far apart in the world.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Could a Viking ever have met a Roman? Could a mighty pharaoh have popped over to see Stonehenge being built? It *sounds* impossible to know — but our timeline knows the answer for sure. Today you become a history detective who can prove who could, and couldn't, have met."
**Show:** the timeline, all periods pegged.  **Do:** tap two far-apart points dramatically.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Quick rule check — which is older, 3000 BC or AD 43, and how do you know?" → *listen for:* "3000 BC; it's BC, further back."

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do)
> **Say:** "To know if two peoples could meet, a historian checks **two** things. First, **time**: do they **overlap** — were they alive at the same time? If they're **centuries apart** on the timeline, they could *never* meet. Second, **space**: even if they overlapped, were they near enough in the world to actually reach each other? So: could a Roman meet a Viking? Look — the Romans left Britain around AD 410, and the Vikings came *much* later. **Centuries apart — no.** Were Egypt's pyramids already old when the Romans invaded Britain? Egypt is ~3000 BC; Romans AD 43 — **thousands of years later — yes, the pyramids were already ancient.** And remember: **same time doesn't always mean same place** — two peoples can live at once but be oceans apart."
**Show / Do:** point to Romans vs Vikings (big gap → no); point to Egypt vs Romans (huge gap, Egypt first → pyramids already old).
**Emphasize:** "Check **time** (do they overlap?) *and* **space** (near enough?). Centuries apart = couldn't meet."

### ⏱ 11–19 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Detective time, together. Card one: could a Roman meet a Viking? Find both on the timeline — overlap or not? Card two: were the pyramids already old when the Romans came? Use the dates to prove it."
**Do:** child locates each on the timeline and gives a yes/no with a reason.
**Ask:** "How do you *know* a Roman couldn't meet a Viking?" → *listen for:* "they're centuries apart on the timeline."

### ⏱ 19–26 min · CHECK
**Ask:** "Were Egypt's pyramids already old when the Romans invaded Britain? How do you know?"
- ✅ **Expected:** **yes** — Egypt is ~**3000 BC** and the Romans invaded in **AD 43**, **thousands of years later**, so the pyramids were already ancient — then probe: *"Which part of the timeline did you use to be sure?"*
- ❌ **If "they all lived at the same time, long ago" (KB misconception):** time-blur → **fix:** "Use the dates — 3000 BC vs AD 43. Are those close, or thousands of years apart? So one was *already old* when the other arrived."
- ❌ **If "if they're the same time they must be in the same place" (KB misconception):** confusing time with space → **fix:** "Could two peoples be alive at once but live on different sides of the world? Yes! Same *time* doesn't mean same *place*."

### ⏱ 26–31 min · PRACTICE (you do)
**Do:** Child answers two fresh "could they meet?" cards, each with a written reason ("No — they're centuries apart" / "Yes — they overlapped and were close enough"). Once secure, interleave the stretch: a case where two peoples overlap in *time* but not *space*.
**Watch for:** "all the same time"; ignoring the space check; yes/no with no timeline reason.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't reveal; 2) name *why* — time-blur or time/space confusion; 3) put both peoples' pegs side by side; 4) ONE hint: "Are they close together on the line, or far apart?"; 5) re-ask one simple "could they meet?" with the timeline in view; 6) "smaller step."

### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP
> **Say:** "Tell me the two things you check to decide if two peoples could meet — and prove the pyramids were old before the Romans." → *listen for:* "time (overlap) and space (near enough); Egypt ~3000 BC vs Romans AD 43 — thousands of years apart."
**Process praise:** "You *proved* your answers with the timeline and the dates — that's a real historian's reasoning."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-Unit-3 check** and the **end-of-year mastery checks** next. Keep the BC/AD rule and period order in spaced review into Year 5 (Anglo-Saxons, Vikings, the Maya).

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** answer one "could they meet?" with the timeline visible and dates pointed out.
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain a case where two peoples overlapped in *time* but not in *space*, and why both checks matter.

**Extension (if time allows):** invent your own tricky "could they meet?" card and write the proof on the back.
**Assessment evidence:** child judges whether two peoples could meet, using the timeline to justify, and distinguishes time from place.

# End-of-year mastery checks — Year 4 History

   ✅ AD 43, from Rome/Italy (an empire) · any three of roads/towns(London)/baths/heating/coins/writing/Hadrian's Wall.
3. **(Romans — resistance)** Explain it was an **invasion many resisted** (**Boudica**, **Hadrian's Wall**).
   ✅ Boudica, British queen of the Iceni, led a revolt · the Wall defended against northern tribes — Roman rule wasn't all peaceful.

5. **(Chronology — BC/AD)** Use **BC/AD** correctly and say which date is older (**bigger BC number = older**).

### Closing note to the tutor

Year 4 is where the chronological journey reaches the **classical world** and where the disciplinary thinking sharpens from "what happened?" to **"how do we know, what changed and why, and is this fair to both sides?"** Three threads should run through every lesson and be praised whenever you see them:

- **Story and sources first.** Lead with the drama (a warrior queen, a city on fire with ideas, a wall across a country), then ask **"how do we know?"** — the timeline, the dates, the map, the ruins still standing.
- **Even-handed history.** The Romans is the model: they **brought useful things** *and* **conquered by force**. Praise the child when they hold both truths instead of picking the comfortable half — that's the heart of fair-minded history.
- **Chronology as the spine.** Keep returning to the one long timeline and the BC/AD rule in spaced review. They are the single most muddled things at this age, and Unit 3 only sticks if it's revisited — carry it into Year 5 (Anglo-Saxons, Vikings, the Maya), where "same time, different place" pays off directly.

**End every lesson on an earned win** with specific **process praise** ("you held both sides," "you proved it with the timeline," "you beat the BC trick *and* explained why") — never "you're so clever." Hand each idea to spaced review, and the year will compound into a child who can place 5,000 years of human history on one line and reason about it like a historian.
