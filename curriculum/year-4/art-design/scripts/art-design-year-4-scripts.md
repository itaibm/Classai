# Year 4 Art & Design — Tutor Scripts (Lessons 1–12)

Complete tutor scripts for [Year 4 Art & Design](../art-design-year-4.md), built to the [scripting guide](../../../SCRIPTING-GUIDE.md) and grounded in `knowledge-base/subjects/art-music/art-ks1-ks2.md` (topics 2 colour, 3 painting, 8 artists, 10 tone, 11 perspective). Art lessons run **demo → explore → create**, with the motto **make → look → improve**. Each beat ⏱ sums to the duration. Because art "checks" are often a *visible success criterion* rather than one right answer, the CHECK beat names what to look for in the work **plus** a quick question; the mandatory "If the child is stuck" support still applies — step back to a smaller technique, never do it for them. Set up materials before the lesson and protect making time.

Year 4 is the year of **colour theory and the illusion of depth**: the full 12-colour wheel, complementary "pop" and harmonious "calm", painting technique to model form, the completion of tone, and the first real steps into space and a vanishing point. Artists studied: **Claude Monet**, **Paul Cézanne**, **Henri Rousseau** (and Georgia O'Keeffe as an extension touchstone). Builds on Year 3 (observational drawing, a 5-step tone scale, a shaded sphere); hands full one-point perspective to Year 5.

# Unit 1 — The full colour wheel

*Essential question: Why do some colours clash and others calm each other?*

---

## Lesson 1 — Rebuild the colour wheel (with tertiaries)
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 1 (The full colour wheel)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can mix and place tertiary colours between the primaries and secondaries."

**You will need:** red/yellow/blue paint (+ a clean palette), brushes, water pots, a printed 12-segment circle template, sketchbooks, kitchen roll.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** primaries (red, yellow, blue) and secondaries (orange, green, purple) from Years 1–3.
**Key vocabulary:** **colour wheel** (colours arranged in a circle), **primary** (red/yellow/blue — can't be mixed), **secondary** (two primaries mixed), **tertiary** (a primary mixed with its neighbouring secondary), **neighbour**, **palette**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** a **tertiary** colour is made by mixing a primary with **the secondary next to it on the wheel** (e.g. red + orange → red-orange) — so it sits *between* them. Pre-empt the muddy-brown trap: mix only **two neighbours at a time**, and rinse the brush between colours.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "You already know six colours of the rainbow. But the colour wheel isn't six colours — it's twelve. So here's my puzzle: what colour lives *between* red and orange? Not red. Not orange. Something in the middle. Today we're going to mix the six missing colours and complete the whole wheel."
**Show:** a six-colour wheel on the board with gaps between each pair.  **Do:** point to the empty gap between red and orange.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Name the three primaries, then a secondary and what makes it." → *listen for:* "red, yellow, blue… orange = red + yellow."
*(If shaky: quickly re-mix one secondary live — yellow + blue → green — and name it.)*

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "Watch me make the in-between colour. Between red and orange, I take a little **red** and a little **orange** — the two colours either side of the gap — and mix them. Look: **red-orange**. It's named after both its parents. A primary mixed with the secondary next to it is called a **tertiary** colour. The secret is to mix only the **two neighbours**, not everything — if I dip a dirty brush in, I'll get brown mud. So I rinse, then mix the next pair."
**Show / Do:** mix red-orange in the palette, paint it into the gap; rinse; start a second tertiary (yellow-orange) to show the method repeats.
**Emphasize:** "**A tertiary is a primary plus its neighbour secondary — it sits between them.**"

### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Your turn to mix one with me. We need the colour between blue and green. Which two do we mix?"
**Do:** child names blue + green, mixes blue-green, places it in the correct gap on their template; tutor prompts a rinse before the next.
**Ask:** "What two colours did you mix, and where does this new one go?" → *listen for:* "blue and green — between them on the wheel."

### ⏱ 17–21 min · CHECK (look at the work + question)
**Ask:** "What two colours make red-orange — and how do you know where it goes on the wheel?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** a clean tertiary sitting *between* its two parents; "red and orange — it goes between them." — probe: *"Why isn't it next to blue?"*
- ❌ **If "red and yellow":** confusing the recipe with orange itself → **fix:** "Orange is already red + yellow. A tertiary mixes a primary with the *secondary* beside it. Point to the two colours either side of the gap."
- ❌ **If the mix is brown/grey:** dirty brush or three colours mixed → **fix:** "Rinse and dry the brush, then take *only* the two neighbours — just two."

### ⏱ 21–31 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child completes the **12-colour wheel** on the template: confirm the 3 primaries and 3 secondaries are in place, then mix and paint all six tertiaries into their gaps (red-orange, yellow-orange, yellow-green, blue-green, blue-purple, red-purple). Once flowing, interleave: lightly label each tertiary.
**Watch for:** muddy mixes (over-mixing / dirty water); tertiaries placed in the wrong gap.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't mix it for them; 2) name *why* — wrong pair, or muddy brush; 3) shrink the task — "just mix the one between red and orange"; 4) one prompt: "touch the colour on each side of the empty gap — those are your two"; 5) re-mix that single tertiary, then build back up; 6) keep it safe — "Mistakes show us what to practise. Rinse, and take a smaller step with just two colours."

### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Spin a finger round your finished wheel and name one tertiary you mixed and its two parents." → *listen for:* a named tertiary + its two neighbours.
**Process praise:** "You rinsed between every mix and placed each colour between its parents — that's exactly how a painter keeps colours clean."
**Revisit later:** the completed wheel is the map for complementary pairs next lesson; keep it visible.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** a 6-colour wheel with just **one** tertiary gap to fill and label.
- **Stretch (challenge):** name and mix all six tertiaries unaided, and say which primary is "stronger" in each.

**Extension (if time allows):** make a tertiary "leaf" — find the exact yellow-green of a real leaf by adjusting the mix.
**Assessment evidence:** child mixes at least one correct tertiary and places it in the right gap, explaining the recipe (primary + neighbour secondary).

## Lesson 2 — Complementary colours that pop
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 1 (The full colour wheel)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can name complementary pairs and use one pair to make a colour pop."

**You will need:** paint, brushes, water, small paper, the 12-colour wheel from Lesson 1 (on view), sketchbooks; an image of a red poppy in a green field.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the 12-colour wheel and the idea of opposite positions on it.
**Key vocabulary:** **complementary** (colours opposite each other on the wheel), **opposite**, **contrast** (how different two colours are), **pop** (stand out strongly).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **complementary colours sit directly opposite on the wheel** (red/green, blue/orange, yellow/purple) and create the **maximum contrast**, so each makes the other "pop". To make a subject pop, paint it in one colour against its **opposite**.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Look at this red poppy in a green field. Your eye jumps straight to it — it almost glows. The poppy isn't brighter than the grass… so why does it shout so loudly? The colour wheel knows the secret, and by the end you'll be able to make anything pop like this."
**Show:** the poppy image, then the 12-colour wheel.  **Do:** lay a finger on red, then slide straight across to green.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "On our wheel, what colour is directly opposite red?" → *listen for:* "green."

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "Colours that sit **directly opposite** on the wheel are called **complementary**. Red is opposite green; blue is opposite orange; yellow is opposite purple. Because they're as different as colours can be, they have the most **contrast** — and contrast makes them **pop**. Watch: I paint a small orange fish, then fill the water behind it with its opposite, blue. See how the orange leaps forward? If I'd put orange on orange, it would vanish."
**Show / Do:** paint an orange motif, surround it with blue; hold up an orange-on-orange version to compare the weak contrast.
**Emphasize:** "**Opposites on the wheel = maximum contrast = pop.**"

### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's choose your pair. If your subject is going to be yellow, what background colour will make it pop — and how do we find it?"
**Do:** child finds yellow on the wheel, slides across to purple, names the pair; sketches a simple motif to paint.
**Ask:** "What is the complementary colour of blue?" → *listen for:* "orange."

### ⏱ 17–21 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "What is the complementary of blue, and why will it make a blue subject pop?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** correct pair named; a motif planned in one colour on its opposite; "orange — they're opposite, biggest contrast." — probe: *"Point to both on the wheel."*
- ❌ **If a neighbour named (e.g. blue → green):** confusing complementary with harmonious → **fix:** "Neighbours sit *next to* each other. Complementary means *straight across* — run a ruler through the centre."
- ❌ **If subject and background are similar colours:** → **fix:** "They're too alike to pop. Put the subject's *opposite* behind it."

### ⏱ 21–31 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child paints a small motif (a fruit, a fish, a flower) in one colour against its **complementary** background, so it pops. Once secure, interleave: add a few tiny dots of the opposite colour *inside* the subject to make it sing even more.
**Watch for:** background and subject too similar; muddy edges where opposites smear together while wet.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't paint it for them; 2) name *why* — wrong pair or low contrast; 3) shrink — "just colour a square one colour, then frame it with its opposite"; 4) one prompt: "draw a line through the wheel's centre — what lands on the far side?"; 5) rebuild from that single pair; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. Let's take a smaller step — one shape, one opposite."

### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Hold your work at arm's length. Does the subject pop? Name the complementary pair you used." → *listen for:* a correct pair + "yes, it pops because they're opposites."
**Process praise:** "You chose true opposites and kept the edges clean — that's why your subject leaps off the page."
**Revisit later:** contrast with next lesson's *calm* harmonies; both feed the end-of-unit check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** a pre-named pair to use (e.g. "paint it orange on blue").
- **Stretch (challenge):** explain *why* complements pop (they're opposite, so maximum contrast) and try a second pair.

**Extension (if time allows):** make a "before/after" — the same motif once on a similar colour, once on its complement — and label which pops.
**Assessment evidence:** child names a correct complementary pair and produces a motif that pops against its opposite.

## Lesson 3 — Harmonies that calm (Monet)
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 1 (The full colour wheel)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can choose a harmonious (neighbouring) palette to make a calm picture."

**You will need:** paint, brushes, water, paper, sketchbooks, the colour wheel on view; an image of **Claude Monet**'s *Water Lilies*.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** complementary pairs (opposite, bold) from Lesson 2; the wheel positions.
**Key vocabulary:** **harmonious / harmony** (colours next to each other on the wheel), **neighbour**, **palette** (the set of colours you choose), **calm**, **mood**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **harmonious colours are neighbours on the wheel** (e.g. blue, blue-green, green) and feel **calm** together — the opposite of bold complementaries. Choosing a palette of **3 neighbours** sets a peaceful mood.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "**Claude Monet** painted the same lily pond hundreds of times. Look — there's almost no loud colour fight here. The blues, greens and purples melt into one another and the whole picture feels… calm, like a quiet morning. Last lesson we made colours *shout*. Today we'll make them *whisper*."
**Show:** Monet's *Water Lilies*.  **Do:** sweep a hand across the soft blues/greens/purples.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "Last time, opposite colours did what? So what might *neighbouring* colours do instead?" → *listen for:* "opposites pop / clash; neighbours feel calm."

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "Colours that sit **next to each other** on the wheel are **harmonious** — they're family, so they get along quietly. Watch me choose a calm palette: blue, then its neighbour blue-green, then green. Three neighbours. I'll paint a little pond using only these three — no loud opposite anywhere. Because they're so alike, nothing jumps out, and the whole thing feels peaceful, like Monet's."
**Show / Do:** select 3 neighbouring colours on the wheel; paint soft overlapping strokes of a pond using only those three.
**Emphasize:** "**Neighbours on the wheel = harmony = calm.**"

### ⏱ 11–17 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Pick your calm trio with me. Put a finger on one colour, then take the two right beside it. What three did you land on?"
**Do:** child chooses 3 neighbouring colours and dabs a quick swatch of each side by side.
**Ask:** "How is a harmonious palette different from a complementary one?" → *listen for:* "harmonious colours sit next to each other and feel calm; complementary are opposite and feel bold."

### ⏱ 17–21 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "How is harmony different from complementary — and which feels calmer?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** 3 true neighbours chosen; "neighbours = calm; opposites = bold." — probe: *"Why does your trio feel calm?"*
- ❌ **If an opposite sneaks into the trio:** → **fix:** "That one's across the wheel — it'll shout. Swap it for the colour right *beside* your others."
- ❌ **If "harmony just means it looks nice":** vague → **fix:** "Be exact: harmony = colours that are *neighbours*. Show me three touching on the wheel."

### ⏱ 21–31 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child paints a small calm scene (pond, misty hill, evening sky) using a **harmonious palette of 3 neighbouring colours** only. Once secure, interleave: add a tint (a little white) or shade (a little black) *within* the harmony for gentle variety — without breaking the family.
**Watch for:** reaching for a far-off bold colour; muddy mixing of the three.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't choose for them; 2) name *why* — picking colours too far apart; 3) shrink — "use just two neighbours, not three"; 4) one prompt: "keep your finger on the wheel and only use colours it can touch"; 5) rebuild to three; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. Smaller step — two neighbours first."

### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Look at your calm picture and one of your neighbour's *pop* pictures. Say what makes yours feel calm." → *listen for:* "the colours are neighbours / close together."
**Process praise:** "You held your nerve and stayed with the family of colours — that restraint is exactly how Monet made his ponds feel so peaceful."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-unit check** — build the 12-colour wheel, name a complementary pair and a harmonious group, and show one "pop" and one "calm" piece.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** a pre-chosen trio of neighbours to use.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add white/black tints and shades *within* the harmony to model soft form, like Monet's water.

**Extension (if time allows):** paint the same simple scene twice — once harmonious (calm), once complementary (bold) — and write one sentence on the mood of each.
**Assessment evidence:** child selects a true 3-neighbour palette and paints a calm scene, contrasting harmony with complementary in words.

# Unit 2 — Painting technique

*Essential question: How do I control paint to model form and blend smoothly?*

---

## Lesson 4 — Wet-into-wet blending
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Painting technique)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can blend two colours smoothly while the paint is wet."

**You will need:** paint, brushes, water, strips of paper (~10×25 cm), sketchbooks, kitchen roll; an image of a sunset sky.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** mixing two colours; controlling water on the brush (Year 1–3 painting).
**Key vocabulary:** **wet-into-wet** (adding a colour while the first is still wet so they blend), **blend** (mix smoothly with no hard line), **join** (where two colours meet), **dry edge**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** to blend with **no hard line**, both colours must be **wet at the join** — work *into* wet paint, soften the meeting place, and don't let it dry first. Pre-empt the hard-edge problem (waiting too long / too little water).

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Watch a sunset and the orange melts into pink, then into purple — but you can never find the *line* where one colour stops and the next begins. How do painters do that — make colours melt with no edge at all? That magic is called wet-into-wet, and you'll do it today."
**Show:** the sunset image.  **Do:** try (and fail) to point to a hard line between the colours.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If I paint orange, let it *dry*, then paint purple next to it — will they blend?" → *listen for:* "no — there'll be a hard line."

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "Here's the rule: blend while it's **wet**. Watch. I paint a band of orange — nice and juicy, still wet. *Before it dries*, I add purple right against it and gently brush back and forth across the **join**. The two wet paints mix on the paper, so the edge softens away. If I wait and the orange dries, the purple just sits on top with a hard line. Wet meets wet — that's the whole trick."
**Show / Do:** paint orange, immediately add purple at the join, soften with light back-and-forth strokes; then show a *dried* hard-line failure for contrast.
**Emphasize:** "**Both colours wet at the join, or it won't blend.**"

### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's blend together. You lay a band of the first colour, quick — keep it wet — then I'll add the second at the join and you brush across to soften it."
**Do:** child paints band one wet; together they add band two and feather the join.
**Ask:** "Why must we add the second colour *now*, not later?" → *listen for:* "so it's still wet and they mix on the paper."

### ⏱ 16–20 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "Why blend while the paint is still wet?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** a soft graded join, no hard line; "so the colours mix on the paper with no edge." — probe: *"Where exactly is the join? Can you find it?"*
- ❌ **If a hard line appears:** first band dried before the second was added → **fix:** "Work faster, or use a touch more water so it stays wet — add the second colour straight away and feather the join."
- ❌ **If it's a pale muddy wash:** too much water → **fix:** "Wipe the brush — juicy paint, not flooded. Strong colour blends best."

### ⏱ 20–31 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child paints a **sky strip melting from one colour into another** wet-into-wet (e.g. blue into pink). Once secure, interleave: blend a *third* colour in at the far end so the strip melts across three.
**Watch for:** waiting too long (hard lines); over-watering (pale mud); scrubbing instead of light feathering.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't paint it for them; 2) name *why* — let it dry, or too much water; 3) shrink — "just two colours, a tiny strip"; 4) one prompt: "add the next colour *while it still shines wet* and stroke once across the line"; 5) rebuild to the full strip; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. Smaller, wetter, faster — let's try again."

### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Run your eye down your strip — point to the spot where it melts best. What made that work?" → *listen for:* "both colours were wet there."
**Process praise:** "You moved quickly and kept the join wet — that timing is exactly what makes a smooth blend."
**Revisit later:** wet-into-wet returns when we paint a rounded apple (Lesson 5) and soft far layers (Lesson 11).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** two warm neighbours that blend easily (e.g. yellow into orange) on a short strip.
- **Stretch (challenge):** blend smoothly across **three** colours with no visible joins.

**Extension (if time allows):** paint a full small sunset using two or three wet-into-wet blends.
**Assessment evidence:** child produces a wet-into-wet blend with a soft, edge-free join and explains why the paint must stay wet.

## Lesson 5 — Painting a tonal apple (modelling form)
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Painting technique)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can paint one object using tints and shades so it looks 3-D."

**You will need:** paint **including white and black**, brushes, water, paper, sketchbooks; a real apple or ball lit by a lamp/window from one side.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** tints (add white → lighter) and shades (add black → darker) from Year 2; shading a sphere with one light source (Year 3).
**Key vocabulary:** **tint** (colour + white), **shade** (colour + a little black), **model** (make look 3-D), **highlight** (lightest lit spot), **shadow side**, **light source**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** to make a painted object look **round and 3-D**, use the **base colour** in the middle, a **tint (add white)** on the lit side as the **highlight**, and a **shade (add black)** on the side away from the light. Keep the highlight genuinely light — don't paint over it. One light source, so one lit side and one shadow side.

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Last year you *shaded* a ball in pencil so it looked round — light on one side, dark on the other. Today we do it in **paint**, in colour. By the end your apple should look so round and solid you could reach in and pick it up."
**Show:** the lit apple — point out its bright spot and its darker side.  **Do:** turn the lamp so the bright spot moves, then settle it.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "How do I make a colour *lighter*? And *darker*?" → *listen for:* "add white for lighter (tint); add a little black for darker (shade)."
*(If shaky: mix one tint and one shade of red live before starting.)*

### ⏱ 5–13 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "First I find my light — it's coming from the left, so the apple's *left* side is lit and its *right* side is in shadow. Watch me build it in three. **One:** the base colour, red, all over. **Two:** on the lit side I mix red + **white** — a **tint** — and dab the brightest spot, the **highlight**, and I *leave it light*, I don't go back over it. **Three:** on the shadow side I mix red + a *tiny* bit of **black** — a **shade** — and the curve turns round into the dark. Light tint, base, dark shade — the apple swells into 3-D."
**Show / Do:** paint base, then highlight tint (lit side), then shade (shadow side), blending wet-into-wet where they meet; protect the highlight.
**Emphasize:** "**Tint on the light side, shade on the dark side — and keep the highlight light.**"

### ⏱ 13–19 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's start yours. Point to where the light hits your apple — that's where the highlight goes. Lay the base colour with me first."
**Do:** child identifies the lit side, paints the base; tutor prompts where tint and shade will go.
**Ask:** "Which side gets the tint, and which gets the shade?" → *listen for:* "tint on the lit side, shade on the side away from the light."

### ⏱ 19–23 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "How did you make the shadow side and the lit side of your apple?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** a clear light side (tint/highlight) and dark side (shade) with a soft turn between; "shade with black for shadow, tint with white for highlight." — probe: *"Where's your light coming from? Does the shading agree?"*
- ❌ **If the whole apple is one flat colour:** no light source chosen → **fix:** "Pick one direction for the light. Now — which side is bright, which is dark? Lighten one, darken the other."
- ❌ **If they added black to *brighten*:** light/dark confusion → **fix:** "Black only darkens. To make it lighter, add **white**."
- ❌ **If the highlight got painted over:** → **fix:** "Leave the brightest spot the lightest — that highlight is what makes it shine."

### ⏱ 23–35 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child paints the **apple/ball modelled in tint and shade** from the lit setup, blending the lit and shadow sides smoothly. Once secure, interleave: add a **cast shadow** on the surface beneath, on the side away from the light.
**Watch for:** flat single-colour apples; highlight lost; light source not consistent.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't paint it for them; 2) name *why* — no chosen light, or tint/shade mixed up; 3) drop to pencil for a moment — re-shade the *idea* lightly, then return to paint; 4) one prompt: "put your hand by the lamp — which side of *your hand* is dark? Paint your apple the same"; 5) rebuild base → tint → shade; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. One light, one bright side, one dark side."

### ⏱ 35–40 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Hold your apple painting next to the real one. Does yours look round? What did the tint and shade do?" → *listen for:* "tint made the lit side, shade made the shadow — it looks 3-D."
**Process praise:** "You committed to one light direction and protected your highlight — that discipline is exactly what makes paint look solid."
**Revisit later:** the same modelling carries into the layered still life (Lesson 6) and monochrome study (Lesson 9).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** paint over a pre-drawn apple outline, with the lit side marked.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add a **cast shadow** *and* a tiny **reflected highlight** on the shadow side.

**Extension (if time allows):** paint a second fruit beside it under the *same* light, so both lit sides agree.
**Assessment evidence:** child paints an object with a tinted lit side, a shaded side and a preserved highlight, modelled from a single light source.

## Lesson 6 — Layering: background, then detail (Cézanne still life)
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 2 (Painting technique)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can paint in layers — big shapes first, details once dry."

**You will need:** paint, brushes, water, paper, sketchbooks, a simple still-life set-up (2–3 objects: fruit, a jug, a bowl); an image of **Paul Cézanne**'s still lifes (e.g. *Apples and Oranges* / *The Basket of Apples*); a hairdryer or fan to speed drying (optional).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** modelling form with tint and shade (Lesson 5); why wet paint smears (Year 1–3).
**Key vocabulary:** **layer** (one coat of paint over another), **block in** (paint the big shapes flat first), **dry**, **detail**, **patch** (a small area of one colour), **still life**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** paint in **layers** — **big shapes first, let them dry, then add detail and tone on top**. Detail painted onto **wet** paint smears; waiting for the base to **dry** keeps it crisp. Cézanne built solid fruit from patches of colour, layer on layer.

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "**Paul Cézanne** didn't paint a perfect apple in one go. Look closely — his fruit is built from little **patches** of colour, layered one over another, like bricks. Here's the puzzle: why would a painter *wait* — leaving paint to dry — instead of finishing it all at once?"
**Show:** a Cézanne still life — point to the patchy, built-up surface.  **Do:** trace one apple made of separate colour patches.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If I paint a dark line onto an apple that's still wet, what happens?" → *listen for:* "it smears / mixes in."

### ⏱ 5–13 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "I work in **layers**. **First layer:** I block in the big shapes flat — the whole apple one red, the jug one colour. No detail yet, just the big patches. Then I **wait until it's dry**. **Second layer:** *now* I add the detail and tone — the shadow side, the stalk, the highlight — and because the base is dry, my crisp marks stay crisp. If I'd added them while wet, watch — they'd smear into mud. Big shapes first, dry, then detail."
**Show / Do:** block in the big shapes; (briefly dry with fan / show a pre-dried version); add detail and tone on top; then show a wet-smear failure for contrast.
**Emphasize:** "**Big shapes first — dry — then detail on top.**"

### ⏱ 13–19 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's block in your still life together. We paint *only* the big flat shapes now — no stalks, no shadows yet. Which big shape first?"
**Do:** child blocks in the largest object flat, then the next; tutor holds them back from detail.
**Ask:** "Why are we not painting the details yet?" → *listen for:* "the base needs to dry first or it'll smear."

### ⏱ 19–23 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "Why let a layer dry before adding detail?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** flat big shapes blocked in, detail held back; "so the top layer doesn't smear into the wet base." — probe: *"What will you add once it's dry?"*
- ❌ **If they jump to detail on wet paint:** → **fix:** "Touch it — still wet? Detail now will smudge. Block all the big shapes first; we'll detail when it's dry."
- ❌ **If "to make it faster":** misunderstanding → **fix:** "It's not about speed — it's so your crisp marks don't sink and smear. Dry first."

### ⏱ 23–35 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child paints the **still life in layers**: block in the big shapes flat → let dry → add detail and tone (shadow sides, highlights, edges, stalks) on top. Once secure, interleave: add **cast shadows** beneath the objects in the final layer.
**Watch for:** detail rushed onto wet paint; trying to finish one object completely before drying (instead of blocking all big shapes first).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't paint it for them; 2) name *why* — impatience / detail too soon; 3) shrink — "paint just the *biggest* shape flat and stop"; 4) one prompt: "lay your finger on it — if it's wet, we wait; let's date a sketchbook note while it dries"; 5) once dry, add one detail, then build up; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. Big shape, dry, then one detail."

### ⏱ 35–40 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Look at your still life. Point to a detail that stayed crisp because the layer underneath was dry." → *listen for:* a named crisp detail + "the base was dry."
**Process praise:** "You were patient and let the base dry — that's why your details are sharp instead of smeared. Real painter's patience."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-unit check** — model an object in tint/shade, show a wet-into-wet blend, and explain why layers must dry. Layering also underpins the jungle layers in Unit 4.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** two objects, one block-in layer and one simple detail layer.
- **Stretch (challenge):** three objects with **cast shadows** added as a final layer, in Cézanne's patchy style.

**Extension (if time allows):** add a patterned cloth or table edge as a third, final layer.
**Assessment evidence:** child blocks in big shapes, allows drying, then adds crisp detail, and explains the reason for layering.

# Unit 3 — Completing tone

*Essential question: How do I shade with control to make any form look solid?*

---

## Lesson 7 — Hatching for tone
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Completing tone)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can build smooth tone using hatching and cross-hatching."

**You will need:** fine-liners and/or pencils, sketchbooks, a ruler (optional), a strip template (a long rectangle divided into 5 boxes).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** tone = how light or dark; the 5-step tone scale (Year 3).
**Key vocabulary:** **tone** (how light or dark), **hatching** (parallel lines for tone), **cross-hatching** (a second layer of lines crossing the first), **gradient** (smooth light-to-dark), **density** (how close together).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** you can make **tone with neat lines, not scribble** — **hatching** is parallel lines; **closer lines = darker**; **cross-hatching** (a crossing layer) makes it darker still. Pre-empt scribbling and jumping straight to black.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's a challenge for a careful artist: make an area look *dark* — but you're not allowed to scribble or scrub. Only neat, straight lines. Sounds impossible? It isn't — it's called hatching, and it's how illustrators and comic artists shade. Let's learn the trick."
**Show:** a hatched drawing (e.g. a comic / engraving) beside a scribbled one.  **Do:** point to how clean the hatched darkness looks.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "What does *tone* mean?" → *listen for:* "how light or dark something is."

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "Watch. **Hatching** is just **parallel lines** — all going the same way, evenly. When I want it *darker*, I put the lines **closer together**. Spaced out = light; squeezed close = dark. And if I want it darker still, I add a second layer **crossing** the first — that's **cross-hatching**. No scribbling anywhere — just controlled lines getting closer, then crossed. See how smooth and dark it gets?"
**Show / Do:** hatch a light patch (lines apart), a darker patch (lines close), then cross-hatch a third (a crossing layer).
**Emphasize:** "**Closer lines = darker. A crossing layer = darker still.**"

### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's hatch one box together — start with lines far apart for light, then make a patch where you bring them closer for dark."
**Do:** child hatches one box light-to-dark by changing line spacing.
**Ask:** "How do you make a hatched area darker?" → *listen for:* "put the lines closer together / add a crossing layer."

### ⏱ 16–20 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "How do you make a hatched area darker — without scribbling?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** even parallel lines; darker patches where lines are closer / cross-hatched; "closer together, or cross them." — probe: *"Show me your darkest box — what did you do?"*
- ❌ **If scribbling:** old habit → **fix:** "Lines, not scribble — keep them parallel and even. To darken, just bring them closer."
- ❌ **If jumping to solid black:** → **fix:** "Build up gradually — start light, squeeze the lines closer, *then* cross-hatch. Don't fill it solid."

### ⏱ 20–31 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child makes a **tone strip light → dark using hatching only** (lines getting closer across 5 boxes), then a **second strip using cross-hatching**. Once secure, interleave: hatch a simple shape (a leaf / pebble) lighter on one side, darker on the other.
**Watch for:** uneven, sloping lines; sudden jumps to black; scribbling when tired.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't shade it for them; 2) name *why* — scribbling, or no gradual build; 3) shrink — "just three boxes: far apart, medium, close"; 4) one prompt: "keep every line going the *same* way; to darken, slide them closer"; 5) rebuild the full strip; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. Slow, even lines — small step first."

### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Look along your strip — does it go smoothly from light to dark? What made the dark end dark?" → *listen for:* "the lines are closer / I cross-hatched."
**Process praise:** "You kept your lines neat and built the darkness slowly — that control is exactly what hatching is for."
**Revisit later:** hatching is a tool for shading the three forms next lesson and feeds the end-of-unit check.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** a 3-step hatched strip (light / medium / dark).
- **Stretch (challenge):** a **curved-hatching** strip where the lines bend to follow a rounded form (hint of the next lesson).

**Extension (if time allows):** hatch a small still object (a pebble) with a light and a dark side.
**Assessment evidence:** child builds a controlled light-to-dark gradient using hatching and cross-hatching, and explains how to darken without scribbling.

## Lesson 8 — Shade three forms with one light
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Completing tone)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can shade a sphere, cylinder and cube consistently from one light source."

**You will need:** soft pencils (2B+), sketchbooks, three plain white forms — a ball (sphere), a tin/tube (cylinder) and a box (cube) — lit by a single lamp from one side.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** one light source → one lit side, one shadow side (Year 3 shaded sphere); hatching/gradient (Lesson 7).
**Key vocabulary:** **form** (a 3-D shape), **sphere / cylinder / cube**, **light source**, **gradient** (smooth tone change), **plane** (a flat face), **band**, **core shadow**, **cast shadow**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **everything is built from a sphere, cylinder or cube** — master shading these three from **one light** and you can shade anything. Light falls differently: a **sphere** turns gradually (a **gradient**); a **cylinder** has soft vertical **bands**; a **cube** has **flat planes** of even tone. The light direction must be the **same** on all three.

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Here's a secret that real artists know: almost every solid object is really a **sphere, a cylinder or a cube** in disguise. A head? A sphere. An arm? A cylinder. A house? A cube. So if you can shade these three, you can shade *anything*. Today we master all three — from one light."
**Show:** the three lit white forms.  **Do:** point out the bright side and dark side on each; relate everyday objects to each form.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "If the light is on the left, which side of the ball is dark?" → *listen for:* "the right side."

### ⏱ 5–14 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "Same light — coming from the left — for all three; watch how differently it lands. The **sphere**: its surface curves *gradually* away from the light, so the tone is a smooth **gradient**, light melting to dark. The **cylinder**: it curves too, but only side to side, so I shade soft vertical **bands** — light strip, then darker, then dark edge. The **cube**: its faces are *flat*, so each face is one even tone — the lit face light, the side face mid, the far face dark, with crisp edges between. Curved = gradient; flat = even planes."
**Show / Do:** shade the sphere (gradient), the cylinder (banded), the cube (flat planes), keeping the light on the left for all; leave each highlight light.
**Emphasize:** "**Curved surfaces give a gradient; flat faces give even tone — and one light for all three.**"

### ⏱ 14–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's shade the sphere together. Where's the highlight? Now I want a smooth slide from light there into dark on the far side — no sudden jump."
**Do:** child shades the sphere as a gradient with the tutor; checks the light direction matches the setup.
**Ask:** "Why does the cube look different from the sphere when the same light hits both?" → *listen for:* "the cube has flat faces (even tone); the sphere curves (a gradient)."

### ⏱ 20–24 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "Why does the cube have flat areas of tone but the sphere has a gradient?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** sphere graded, cube flat-faced, cylinder banded, light direction consistent; "flat faces catch even light; a curved surface turns gradually away from the light." — probe: *"Point to the highlight on each — are they all on the same side?"*
- ❌ **If the cube is shaded with a gradient:** treating flat as curved → **fix:** "A flat face doesn't bend, so the light is even across it — one tone per face, with a crisp edge."
- ❌ **If light comes from different sides on different forms:** → **fix:** "One lamp, one direction. Keep every highlight on the *same* side."

### ⏱ 24–35 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child **shades all three forms** from the single lit setup — sphere (gradient), cylinder (bands), cube (flat planes) — light direction consistent. Once secure, interleave: add **cast shadows** that link each form to the surface, falling away from the light.
**Watch for:** inconsistent light direction; cube shaded like a sphere; highlights lost; tone jumping instead of gradating on the sphere.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't shade it for them; 2) name *why* — mixing flat and curved, or inconsistent light; 3) shrink — "just shade the **cube**: one tone per face, three faces"; 4) one prompt: "is this surface flat or curved? Flat = even; curved = slide"; 5) build back to all three; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. One form, one light — small step."

### ⏱ 35–40 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Look at your three forms together — do they all agree about where the light is? Tell me one difference between how you shaded the cube and the sphere." → *listen for:* "cube = flat even faces; sphere = smooth gradient."
**Process praise:** "You kept one light across all three and matched the shading to each shape — that's the building-block skill behind drawing anything."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-unit check** with hatching and the monochrome study; these three forms underpin all future tonal work in Year 5.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** shade **two** of the three (start with the cube and the sphere).
- **Stretch (challenge):** add **cast shadows** linking all three to the surface and a touch of **reflected light** in the sphere's shadow.

**Extension (if time allows):** find a real object that's secretly one of the three forms and shade it from the same light.
**Assessment evidence:** child shades sphere, cylinder and cube with a consistent light source, distinguishing gradient (curved) from even planes (flat).

## Lesson 9 — Tone in one colour (monochrome study)
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 3 (Completing tone)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can use tints and shades of one colour to model a form (tone in colour)."

**You will need:** **one** colour of paint + white and black, brushes, water, paper, sketchbooks; a simple lit object (a mug, a pear, a ball).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** tints (add white) and shades (add black) (Lesson 5); tone = light/dark; one light source (Lesson 8).
**Key vocabulary:** **monochrome** (one colour only), **tone** (light/dark), **tint** (+ white), **shade** (+ black), **model**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** **tone is not the same as colour** — tone is *how light or dark*. You can model a whole solid object using **one colour**: its **lightest tint** for highlights, its **darkest shade** for shadow, and the colour itself in between. Pre-empt "I need more colours" — one colour can do everything.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Big question: could you paint a *whole* picture — light, shadow, a round solid object — using only **one** colour? It sounds impossible. But if you've got light and dark of that colour, you've got everything you need. Let's prove it."
**Show:** the lit object; a one-colour (monochrome) painting if available.  **Do:** squint at the object to see its light and dark areas without the colour distracting.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "How do I make blue lighter? And darker?" → *listen for:* "add white (tint); add a little black (shade)."

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "Here's the idea that surprises people: **tone is *not* colour**. Tone is just how light or dark a thing is. So I can model this whole pear in **one colour**. Watch — I mix three: the colour + lots of **white** for the bright **highlight**; the colour on its own for the middle; the colour + a little **black** for the **shadow** side. Same colour throughout — only the *tone* changes. And look: it still looks round and solid. One colour, full form."
**Show / Do:** mix a light tint, the base, and a dark shade of one colour; paint a rounded object with highlight, mid and shadow.
**Emphasize:** "**Tone is light-and-dark, not colour — one colour can do it all.**"

### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Pick your one colour. Let's mix three tones first: a pale tint, the colour itself, a dark shade. Where will the lightest tint go on your object?"
**Do:** child mixes 3 tones of their chosen colour and identifies where the highlight belongs.
**Ask:** "What does *tone* mean — and is it the same as colour?" → *listen for:* "tone is how light or dark; no — you can have light and dark of one colour."

### ⏱ 16–20 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "What does *tone* mean — and is it the same as colour?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** at least 3 tones of one colour, placed by light/dark; "tone = how light or dark; not the same as colour." — probe: *"Show me your lightest and darkest of the same colour."*
- ❌ **If they reach for a second colour:** confusing tone with colour → **fix:** "Stay in one colour — change only the *tone* with white and black."
- ❌ **If it's flat (one tone):** → **fix:** "Where's your light? Make that side lighter with white and the far side darker with black — same colour."

### ⏱ 20–31 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child paints a simple object **monochrome** — one colour only — modelling form with its lightest tint (highlight), base, and darkest shade (shadow), from one light. Once secure, interleave: add a fourth/fifth in-between tone and blend for a smoother round form.
**Watch for:** sneaking in a second colour; only two tones (flat); highlight lost.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't paint it for them; 2) name *why* — too few tones, or reaching for colour; 3) shrink — "just three tones in three patches: light, mid, dark"; 4) one prompt: "squint — where's the lightest part of the object? Put your palest tint there"; 5) rebuild on the object; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. One colour, three tones — small step."

### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Look at your monochrome object — does it look solid with just one colour? What gave it its roundness?" → *listen for:* "the light and dark tones / tints and shades."
**Process praise:** "You proved one colour can show a whole 3-D form — that's a real understanding of what tone is."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-unit check** — hatch a gradient, shade the three forms, and model a monochrome study. **Tone is now complete**; Year 5 *applies* it within perspective rather than re-teaching it.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** 3 tones of one colour (light / mid / dark) on a pre-drawn object.
- **Stretch (challenge):** 5 blended tones on a rounded form, with a clean preserved highlight.

**Extension (if time allows):** paint a small monochrome scene (object + its cast shadow + a background tone) in a single colour.
**Assessment evidence:** child models a form in one colour using tints and shades, and explains that tone (light/dark) is distinct from colour.

# Unit 4 — First steps in space & depth

*Essential question: How can a flat page show what is near and what is far?*

---

## Lesson 10 — Depth tricks: smaller, higher, overlapped
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 4 (Space & depth)** | **Duration:** 35 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can show depth by drawing far things smaller, higher up and overlapped."

**You will need:** pencils, paper, sketchbooks; a photo of a landscape with clear near/far (a row of trees, a beach).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** foreground/background language; drawing simple shapes lightly.
**Key vocabulary:** **space / depth** (near and far on a flat page), **foreground** (near, front), **background** (far, behind), **overlap** (one thing partly in front of another), **scale** (size), **horizon**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** a flat page shows depth with **three tricks** — far things are drawn **smaller**, **higher up the page**, and **overlapped** (partly hidden) by nearer things. Pre-empt the common error of drawing far objects the same size as near ones.

---

### ⏱ 0–4 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Hold your thumb up at arm's length. Now 'squash' a faraway friend or tree behind it — they look tiny enough to fit on your thumbnail! They're not really tiny, of course. So how does an artist make a flat page show that something is *far away*? There are three tricks, and you'll know all three by the end."
**Show:** the landscape photo.  **Do:** compare a near tree and a far tree in the photo.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "In this picture, what's in the front — the foreground — and what's far back?" → *listen for:* near object named (front), far object named (back).

### ⏱ 4–11 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "Three tricks for 'far away'. **One — smaller:** the far tree is drawn *smaller* than the near one. **Two — higher:** far things sit *higher up the page*, near the horizon; near things are lower, at the bottom. **Three — overlapped:** the near tree is partly *in front of* the far one — overlap tells us which is closer. Watch me: big tree, low and at the front; small tree, higher up and partly hidden behind the first. Suddenly the page has depth."
**Show / Do:** draw a big near tree (low, front), then a small far tree (higher, overlapped behind it).
**Emphasize:** "**Far = smaller, higher, overlapped.**"

### ⏱ 11–16 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's place two trees together. Draw a big one at the bottom for the front. Now where does the *far* tree go — and what size?"
**Do:** child draws a near tree, then a smaller far tree higher up and overlapped.
**Ask:** "Name two ways you showed the second tree is far away." → *listen for:* "smaller; higher up; overlapped (any two)."

### ⏱ 16–20 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "Name two ways to show something is far away."
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** far object smaller, higher, and/or overlapped; "smaller; higher up; overlapped." — probe: *"Which trick did you use most?"*
- ❌ **If far objects same size as near:** scale not applied → **fix:** "Make the far one *smaller* — shrink it. Far away means small on the page."
- ❌ **If everything sits in a flat row:** no height/overlap → **fix:** "Slide the far things *higher up* and let a near thing *overlap* one — that pushes it back."

### ⏱ 20–31 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child draws a **simple landscape using all three cues**: a big tree (or animal) in front, smaller ones higher up and behind, with overlaps. Once secure, interleave: add a **middleground** layer — a medium tree between near and far.
**Watch for:** all objects the same size; no overlap; far things too low.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't draw it for them; 2) name *why* — scale/height not applied; 3) shrink — "just two trees: a big one and a *small* one"; 4) one prompt: "make the far one half the size and slide it up the page"; 5) add an overlap, then build up; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. Two trees, one big, one small and high — small step."

### ⏱ 31–35 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Look at your landscape — does it feel deep? Point to the trick that helped most." → *listen for:* "smaller / higher / overlapped."
**Process praise:** "You used more than one depth trick together — that's why your flat page suddenly feels like it goes back for miles."
**Revisit later:** these cues power the layered jungle (Lesson 11) and combine with a vanishing point (Lesson 12).

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** a page with pre-marked foreground and background bands to draw into.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add a clear **middleground** layer, using all three cues across three depths.

**Extension (if time allows):** draw the *same* scene wrongly (all same size) and rightly, and label why one feels deep.
**Assessment evidence:** child applies at least two depth cues (smaller / higher / overlapped) to create a sense of distance and names them.

## Lesson 11 — Layered jungle depth (Henri Rousseau)
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 4 (Space & depth)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can build a scene in foreground, middleground and background layers to show depth."

**You will need:** paint **or** pencils/coloured pencils, paper, sketchbooks; an image of **Henri Rousseau**'s jungle paintings (e.g. *Tiger in a Tropical Storm (Surprised!)* / *The Dream*).
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** the three depth cues (smaller, higher, overlapped) from Lesson 10; layering big-to-detail (Lesson 6).
**Key vocabulary:** **foreground / middleground / background** (the three depth layers), **overlap**, **layer**, **scale**, **atmospheric** (far things paler/bluer).
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** build depth by working in **three layers** — large overlapping shapes in the **foreground**, smaller behind in the **middleground**, smaller and paler in the **background**. **Overlap is the key clue:** what's in front is closer; what it covers is further back. Rousseau stacked leaf in front of leaf to make his jungles feel deep.

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "**Henri Rousseau** painted thick, steamy jungles — full of tigers and giant leaves — and here's the amazing part: he never once saw a real jungle. He imagined it. Look how *deep* it feels though — leaf in front of leaf in front of leaf, going back and back. How did he make a flat painting feel so deep and lush?"
**Show:** a Rousseau jungle.  **Do:** point from a big front leaf, to a smaller one behind, to the dim shapes at the back.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From last lesson — name a way to show something is further back." → *listen for:* "smaller / higher / overlapped."

### ⏱ 5–13 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "I build the jungle in **three layers**. **Foreground:** big leaves and a tiger at the front — large, low, bold, and they **overlap** everything behind. **Middleground:** smaller plants behind, partly hidden by the front leaves. **Background:** smallest, palest shapes far at the back. Overlap is the magic word — the leaf in front is *closer*, and whatever it covers is *further away*. Watch how stacking the layers, front to back, makes the jungle go deep."
**Show / Do:** draw/paint big overlapping foreground leaves, then smaller middleground plants behind, then pale small background; (if painting, block big first, like Cézanne layering).
**Emphasize:** "**Big and overlapping in front; smaller and paler behind. Overlap = which is closer.**"

### ⏱ 13–19 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's start your foreground together — one big leaf, low and bold, right at the front. Now where does a smaller leaf go to look *behind* it?"
**Do:** child draws a big front leaf, then a smaller one overlapped behind it.
**Ask:** "How does overlapping show depth?" → *listen for:* "the thing in front is closer; what it covers is further back."

### ⏱ 19–23 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "How does overlapping show depth?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** large overlapping front shapes; smaller behind; "the thing in front is closer; what it covers is further back." — probe: *"Which leaf is nearest? How can I tell?"*
- ❌ **If shapes sit side by side, no overlap:** → **fix:** "Let a front leaf *cover part* of one behind it — that overlap tells us which is closer."
- ❌ **If all shapes same size:** → **fix:** "Shrink the ones at the back, and slide them higher — far means smaller and higher."

### ⏱ 23–35 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child builds a **layered jungle** in foreground, middleground and background: large overlapping leaves/animals in front, smaller paler ones behind. (If painting: block the big shapes first, dry, then detail — Lesson 6 layering.) Once secure, interleave: make the far layer **paler/bluer** for an atmospheric hint.
**Watch for:** no overlap; uniform size; foreground and background equally bold (no fade).
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't draw it for them; 2) name *why* — no overlap or no size change; 3) shrink — "just two leaves: one big in front, one small peeking out *behind* it"; 4) one prompt: "let the front leaf cover a corner of the one behind"; 5) add the back layer, then build up; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. Two leaves, one in front of the other — small step."

### ⏱ 35–40 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Stand back from your jungle — can you see it going back in layers? Point from the nearest thing to the furthest." → *listen for:* a front-to-back tour naming the three layers.
**Process praise:** "You overlapped your layers and faded the far ones — that's exactly how Rousseau made a jungle he'd never seen feel so deep and real."
**Revisit later:** depth layers plus a vanishing point complete the unit next lesson.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** three pre-drawn depth bands (foreground / middleground / background) to fill.
- **Stretch (challenge):** make far layers **paler and bluer** (atmospheric perspective) and add a fourth, faintest layer.

**Extension (if time allows):** add a hidden animal peeking from behind a foreground leaf, using overlap to place it.
**Assessment evidence:** child builds a scene in three depth layers using overlap and scale, and explains how overlap shows what is nearer.

## Lesson 12 — Meet the vanishing point
**Art · Year 4 · Unit 4 (Space & depth)** | **Duration:** 40 min | **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a single vanishing point to make a road or path look like it stretches away."

**You will need:** rulers, pencils, paper, sketchbooks; a photo of a long straight road or railway track receding to the horizon.
**Prior knowledge to retrieve:** depth cues — smaller, higher (Lesson 10); the idea of a horizon.
**Key vocabulary:** **vanishing point** (the single far point where parallel lines appear to meet), **horizon** (the far line where land meets sky), **parallel** (lines that stay the same distance apart), **converge** (come together), **perspective**.
**What to emphasize (the heart of the lesson):** parallel lines — like the two edges of a road — appear to **rush together and meet at a single point on the horizon**: the **vanishing point**. Draw the dot first, draw both road edges *to that one dot*, and size things for distance (big near the front, small near the dot). Pre-empt lines going to *different* points.

---

### ⏱ 0–5 min · HOOK
> **Say:** "Imagine standing on a long, straight road that runs dead ahead. The two sides are *parallel* — they never really get closer. But look at this photo: they seem to **rush together** in the distance until they meet at a single point. Where exactly do they meet? That magic spot has a name — the **vanishing point** — and today you'll make a road that genuinely looks miles long."
**Show:** the long-road photo.  **Do:** trace the two road edges with fingers until they meet at one far point.
**Ask (retrieve prior knowledge):** "From earlier — how do we draw a far-away tree compared to a near one?" → *listen for:* "smaller and higher up."

### ⏱ 5–14 min · EXPLAIN (I do — demo)
> **Say:** "Watch the steps. **One:** I draw the **horizon** — a faint line across, where land meets sky. **Two:** on it, I mark a single dot — the **vanishing point**. **Three:** with a ruler, I draw a line from the *bottom-left* corner up to that dot, and another from the *bottom-right* corner to the *same* dot — those are my two road edges. See how they **converge**? **Four:** trees beside the road — big near the front, getting smaller as they near the dot. The whole road now looks like it stretches far, far away. The big rule: every line goes to the **same one point**."
**Show / Do:** draw horizon, mark the vanishing point, rule both road edges to it, add trees shrinking toward the dot.
**Emphasize:** "**All the road lines meet at ONE vanishing point — that's what makes it stretch away.**"

### ⏱ 14–20 min · EXAMPLE (we do)
> **Say:** "Let's set yours up together. Draw the horizon, mark your dot. Now rule the left road edge — from the bottom corner to where?"
**Do:** child draws horizon and vanishing point, then rules one road edge to the dot; tutor prompts the second to the *same* dot.
**Ask:** "What is the vanishing point?" → *listen for:* "the single point in the distance where parallel lines appear to meet."

### ⏱ 20–24 min · CHECK (look + question)
**Ask:** "What is the vanishing point — and where do your road lines have to go?"
- ✅ **Look for / Expected:** one dot on the horizon; both road edges ruled to *that* dot; "the single point where parallel lines appear to meet — both lines go to it." — probe: *"Do both your road lines land on the same dot?"*
- ❌ **If lines go to different points:** → **fix:** "Both edges must meet at the *same* dot — re-rule them to one point, or the road won't look right."
- ❌ **If road edges stay parallel (don't converge):** → **fix:** "Pull both lines *in* toward the dot so they get closer in the distance — that's what makes it recede."
- ❌ **If trees stay the same size:** → **fix:** "Shrink the trees as they get nearer the dot — far means small."

### ⏱ 24–35 min · PRACTICE (you do — create)
**Do:** Child draws their own **road-into-distance with one vanishing point**: horizon, vanishing point, two road edges to the dot, and trees/objects sized for distance (big near, small far). Once secure, interleave: add a **fence or telegraph poles** shrinking toward the point.
**Watch for:** two different vanishing points; non-converging edges; uniform-size objects; vanishing point drawn off the horizon.
> **If the child is stuck (missed twice):** 1) don't draw it for them; 2) name *why* — lines not to one point, or no convergence; 3) shrink — "just the dot and the two road edges to it — nothing else yet"; 4) one prompt: "lay your ruler from the corner to the dot and trace along it"; 5) add one tree, then build up; 6) "Mistakes show us what to practise. One dot, two lines to it — small step."

### ⏱ 35–40 min · RECAP (look → improve)
> **Say:** "Look down your road — does it feel like it goes miles away? What made that happen?" → *listen for:* "the lines meet at the vanishing point / things get smaller toward it."
**Process praise:** "You sent both road edges to a single point and shrank everything toward it — that's real perspective, and it's why your flat road genuinely looks long."
**Revisit later:** **End-of-unit check** — a depth-cue scene plus a vanishing-point road. **Year 5 completes full one-point perspective** — a whole street or room of boxes converging on the vanishing point.

---

**Differentiation**
- **Support (gentle):** vanishing point and road edges pre-marked — child adds the sized trees/objects.
- **Stretch (challenge):** add a **fence and telegraph poles** that shrink evenly toward the point, plus a converging path on the other side.

**Extension (if time allows):** add simple boxy buildings whose top and bottom edges also angle toward the vanishing point (a first taste of Year 5).
**Assessment evidence:** child draws a road receding to a single vanishing point with both edges converging and objects scaled for distance, and defines the vanishing point.

## End-of-year outcomes

By the end of Year 4, a child's sketchbook/portfolio should show:

1. **A 12-colour wheel** including tertiary colours, correctly mixed and placed (L1).
2. **A complementary "pop" piece and a harmonious "calm" piece**, with the pairs/groups named (L2–L3).
3. **A wet-into-wet blend** and an object **modelled in paint** with tints (highlight) and shades (shadow) (L4–L5).
4. **A layered painting** — big shapes first, detail once dry — in the spirit of **Cézanne** or **Monet** (L6).
5. **Hatching and cross-hatching tone**, and a **sphere, cylinder and cube** shaded from one light source (L7–L8).
6. **A monochrome study** showing tone in a single colour (L9).
7. **A depth drawing** using the three cues (smaller, higher, overlapped), e.g. a **Rousseau**-style layered scene (L10–L11).
8. **A first vanishing-point drawing** of a road or path receding into the distance (L12).

> **Closing note.** Year 4 turns "draw what you see" into *understand colour like a painter* and *make a flat page look deep*. Tone is now **complete** — Year 5 will apply it inside perspective and prints rather than re-teach it — and the single vanishing point introduced here grows into full **one-point perspective** next year. Keep the sketchbook running and the motto alive: **make → look → improve**. Praise the *process* — the rinse between mixes, the patience to let a layer dry, the nerve to commit both road lines to one point — because that is how a child becomes an artist who can make anything look real.
