# Year 4 Art & Design (ages 9–10) — Scope & Sequence

> Part of **the best international primary school** (see [`../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md`](../../../SCHOOL-CHARTER.md)).
> Written to [`../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md`](../../PLANNING-BRIEF.md). Standards reference:
> `knowledge-base/subjects/art-music/art-ks1-ks2.md` and `extension-topics.md`.
> Codes: **UK NC KS2 Art & Design** (sketchbooks, mastery of techniques, great artists, architects and designers) and **NCAS** (US National Core Arts Standards, Visual Arts, Grade 4: VA:Cr, VA:Pr, VA:Re, VA:Cn).

## Year overview

Year 4 art is about **space, surface and structure**: how artists create the illusion of depth on a flat page, how they build images through printmaking, how they make form in clay, and how makers across the world colour and pattern cloth. Each unit introduces **named artists and makers from many cultures** — from Albrecht Dürer (Germany), Edgar Degas (France) and Elizabeth Catlett (USA/Mexico) to Magdalene Odundo (Kenya/UK), Ladi Kwali (Nigeria), Barbara Hepworth (UK), Ai Weiwei (China), the adire dyers of Yoruba Nigeria, the bògòlanfini makers of Mali, shibori dyers of Japan and William Morris (UK) — and connects to the year's history (Benin, Baghdad) and science (materials; plant dyes as indicators).

Every lesson uses the **sketchbook** as a thinking tool: collecting, experimenting, annotating and reflecting. **Assessment is by looking at and discussing the work**, never by quizzes: each unit ends with a critique where children explain their intentions, the techniques they chose and what they would change, using the vocabulary of line, tone, colour, texture, pattern, shape, form and space.

**Techniques build across the year:** drawing (one-point perspective) → printmaking beyond Year 2's relief stamps (monoprint, collagraph, two-colour reduction print) → clay (secure joins retrieved, sgraffito, relief plaques, carving negative space) → textiles (natural dyes, adire and shibori resist, a William Morris-style repeat, embellishing with Year 3's stitches) → pattern and digital design (compass-and-ruler geometry, then a digital tessellation) → curating an exhibition.

**What Year 4 assumes (from Years 1–3):** the colour wheel, optical mixing, harmonious schemes and colour symbolism; washes and smooth colour; tone in pencil, charcoal and hatching; depth through overlap, size, mist and atmospheric colour (but not yet the vanishing point); relief printing and Hokusai (Year 2); pinch, coil, slab and score-and-slip clay and Hepworth (Years 1–2); weaving, Kente, Anni Albers and batik-style resist (Years 1–2); running stitch, backstitch, cross stitch and appliqué (Year 3); research sketchbooks.
**What Year 5 relies on:** secure perspective and proportion (for portraits); confident use of print, clay and textiles (for mixed media); experience of discussing artists' intentions (for the personal project and portfolio critique).

## Unit 1 — Drawing space: one-point perspective and architecture

**Essential question:** How can a flat drawing make us feel we could walk into it?

**Key vocabulary:** perspective, horizon line, eye level, vanishing point, orthogonal lines, foreground, background, scale, architecture, facade, viewpoint

### Lesson 1 — Looking into pictures
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how artists create depth, using examples from different times and places." (UK NC KS2 great artists; NCAS VA:Re7.1.4)
- **Hook:** Compare a medieval painting with flat space and a Renaissance street scene: "Which one could you walk into?"
- **Key activity:** Look closely (see–think–wonder) at works that use perspective (e.g. a Canaletto view of Venice) and a work that plays with it (a Hiroshige print from *One Hundred Famous Views of Edo*); retrieve Year 3 depth tricks; sketch thumbnail diagrams of each.
- **Check for understanding:** "What three tricks does the artist use to make the street look deep?" → e.g. objects get smaller, lines lead to one point, far things are higher and paler.
- **Differentiation:** support: a checklist of depth tricks to spot. / stretch: explain why some artists (e.g. in traditional Japanese or medieval art) chose not to use one-point perspective.
- **Materials:** printed reproductions, sketchbooks, pencils.
- **Joy:** "Step into the picture" — describe what you'd see walking down the painted street.

### Lesson 2 — Horizon line and vanishing point
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw a horizon line and vanishing point and use orthogonal lines to show depth." (UK NC KS2 drawing techniques; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** Photos of long roads and railway tracks: "Do the rails really meet?"
- **Key activity:** Find the horizon at eye level (look through a window, tape it on the glass); mark a vanishing point; draw a road, railway and fence using a ruler; add objects that shrink with distance.
- **Check for understanding:** "Where should the horizon line go if you are lying on the floor looking up a road?" → Very low on the page, because the horizon is always at eye level.
- **Differentiation:** support: a template with horizon and vanishing point marked. / stretch: add trees or lamp posts that shrink correctly in size and spacing.
- **Materials:** rulers, sharp pencils, erasers, window tape, road photos.
- **Joy:** "Endless road" drawing that seems to disappear.

### Lesson 3 — Boxes in perspective
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw 3-D boxes above, below and on the horizon line." (UK NC KS2 drawing; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Can you draw a box that floats in the sky and one you look down on?"
- **Key activity:** Draw the front face, connect corners to the vanishing point, cut off the back with lines parallel to the front; place boxes above (see the bottom), below (see the top) and across the horizon.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can you see the top of a box below the horizon but the underneath of a box above it?" → Because you are looking down on things below eye level and up at things above it.
- **Differentiation:** support: step-by-step visual guide. / stretch: turn boxes into buildings with windows and doors in perspective.
- **Materials:** rulers, pencils, cardboard boxes to observe.
- **Joy:** "Flying boxes" drawing.

### Lesson 4 — Architects and cities of the world
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how architects use drawings to plan buildings, with examples from around the world." (UK NC KS2 great architects and designers; NCAS VA:Cn11.1.4)
- **Hook:** Sketches and photographs of buildings by Zaha Hadid (Iraqi-British) and Antoni Gaudí (Spain), and the Round City of Baghdad (links to history).
- **Key activity:** Compare an architect's sketch with the finished building; discuss straight vs curving forms; children sketch a building from school or a photo using perspective.
- **Check for understanding:** "How is Zaha Hadid's building different from a classic box-shaped building, and why might she have designed it that way?" → Curving, flowing forms; she wanted movement and surprise.
- **Differentiation:** support: trace key lines on a photo first. / stretch: draw a building from two viewpoints.
- **Materials:** printed images, sketchbooks, tracing paper.
- **Joy:** "Architect for a day" — sketch a dream building.

### Lesson 5 — A street in perspective (final drawing)
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can draw a street or corridor in one-point perspective with details and tone." (UK NC KS2 drawing — mastery; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** Photos of streets from around the world (Marrakech, Kyoto, Havana, your own town).
- **Key activity:** Plan in the sketchbook; draw the street with buildings on both sides; add doors, windows and people at the right scale; add tone with hatching (retrieving Year 3).
- **Check for understanding:** "Show me how you made sure a person far away is the right size." → They drew guide lines to the vanishing point to scale the figure.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-drawn horizon and vanishing point; fewer buildings. / stretch: add a second street joining at an angle or a reflection in water.
- **Materials:** cartridge paper, rulers, pencils (HB, 2B, 4B), fine liners.
- **Joy:** Seeing the street "open up" as lines are added.

### Lesson 6 — Critique: does it look deep?
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can discuss my drawing and others' using perspective vocabulary and suggest improvements." (UK NC KS2 evaluate; NCAS VA:Re9.1.4)
- **Hook:** A gallery walk with sticky notes.
- **Key activity:** "Two stars and a wish" feedback using vocabulary (horizon line, vanishing point, scale); children write a sketchbook reflection.
- **Check for understanding:** "Point to the part of your drawing that shows depth best and explain how." → Use of vanishing point, scale or overlap explained.
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence stems. / stretch: compare their drawing with Canaletto's approach.
- **Materials:** drawings, sticky notes.
- **Joy:** Gallery walk.

**End-of-unit check:** Discussion of the final street drawing: the child identifies horizon line, vanishing point and scale, and explains one choice they made and one improvement.

## Unit 2 — Printmaking: monoprint, collagraph and reduction

**Essential question:** How many different ways can an artist make a print — and why have artists across the world loved printmaking?

**Key vocabulary:** print, relief, block, plate, brayer (roller), ink, monoprint, collagraph, texture, impression, edition, reverse (mirror) image, positive and negative space, registration, reduction print, woodcut, linocut

### Lesson 7 — What is a print? Dürer's *Rhinoceros*
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how a woodcut is made and why printing spread images across the world." (UK NC KS2 great artists; NCAS VA:Re7.2.4)
- **Hook:** Albrecht Dürer's *Rhinoceros* woodcut (Germany, 1515): "Dürer never saw a rhinoceros — yet thousands of people saw his picture. How?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 2's relief stamping and Hokusai in two minutes. New: Dürer drew the rhinoceros from a written description and a sketch sent from Lisbon; a woodcutter carved it and the block printed thousands of copies, so for centuries many Europeans pictured a rhino with armour plates. Discuss what prints did before photography; look at the mirror image on a real block or stamp.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must you carve letters backwards on a printing block — and why did Dürer's mistakes about the rhinoceros spread so far?" → the print is a mirror image of the block; the same block printed many copies, so everyone who saw a copy saw the same errors
- **Differentiation:** support: stamp demo with an eraser block. / stretch: explain the difference between Dürer's single-block woodcut and Hokusai's colour woodblock prints (one block per colour, Year 2).
- **Materials:** reproductions, a simple stamp, ink pad.
- **Joy:** Draw an animal only from a partner's written description, then compare with the real one.

### Lesson 8 — Monoprinting: drawing through ink
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make monoprints by drawing into and through ink, and explain how they differ from a relief edition." (UK NC KS2 printing; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** Edgar Degas's monotypes (France, 1870s–80s): "He made each of these by painting on a metal plate and printing it once. Why only once?"
- **Key activity:** Roll a thin layer of ink on a plate; (1) wipe away shapes with a cotton bud for a light-on-dark print; (2) lay paper on an inked plate and draw on the back for a 'trace monoprint' with dark lines; compare the two methods and a ghost print.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why can you only make one good print from a monoprint, but an edition from a relief block?" → the image is in the ink layer itself, which is used up when it transfers to the paper; a carved block keeps its design and can be inked again and again
- **Differentiation:** support: the wipe-away method only, with a simple shape. / stretch: combine both methods in one print and explain the order you used.
- **Materials:** acetate or perspex plates, water-based printing ink, brayers, paper, cotton buds, pencils, Degas reproductions.
- **Joy:** The surprise of the 'ghost' second print.

### Lesson 9 — Collagraph plates: printing texture
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can build a collagraph printing plate from textured materials and predict how each will print." (UK NC KS2 printing techniques; NCAS VA:Cr1.2.4)
- **Hook:** "String, lace, leaves and corrugated card — can they become a printing plate?"
- **Key activity:** Plan a design in the sketchbook (a landscape or animal); build a plate on card by gluing textured materials of similar height; seal with PVA and dry; test-print one corner and predict which textures will print darkest.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which materials on your plate printed darkest, and why?" → the raised, textured materials (string, corrugated card) take the most ink and press hardest on the paper; lower, smooth areas print lighter or not at all
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-cut textured shapes to arrange. / stretch: plan textures to suggest tone (dark foreground, light sky) and explain the choices.
- **Materials:** thick card, PVA, string, lace, leaves, corrugated card, fabric scraps, scissors, sketchbooks.
- **Joy:** "Texture tasting" — rub each material with crayon before gluing.

### Lesson 10 — Inking and pulling an edition
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can ink a block evenly and pull a clean edition of three prints." (UK NC KS2 printing — mastery; NCAS VA:Cr2.2.4)
- **Hook:** The printmaker's reveal — peel back the paper.
- **Key activity:** Ink the collagraph plate thinly with a brayer; press with a clean roller or spoon; number and sign prints (1/3, 2/3, 3/3) as artists do; evaluate each.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why is your second print often better than your first?" → The plate has soaked up some ink and you've adjusted pressure and ink thickness.
- **Differentiation:** support: work in pairs (inker and presser). / stretch: experiment with coloured paper and ink combinations.
- **Materials:** water-based printing ink, brayers, inking trays, paper, spoons, drying rack, aprons.
- **Joy:** Signing and numbering an edition like a real printmaker.

### Lesson 11 — Elizabeth Catlett: prints with a message
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain how an artist used printmaking to share a message." (UK NC KS2 great artists; NCAS VA:Re8.1.4)
- **Hook:** A linocut by Elizabeth Catlett (African American artist who worked in Mexico with the Taller de Gráfica Popular): "What is this picture saying?"
- **Key activity:** Discuss how Catlett's bold prints celebrated working people and women; why prints are good for spreading messages (many copies, cheap); sketch ideas for a print with a message about something children care about.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might an artist who wants many people to see a message choose printmaking?" → Many copies can be made cheaply and shared widely.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided looking questions. / stretch: compare Catlett's message with the purpose of Dürer's *Rhinoceros*.
- **Materials:** reproductions, sketchbooks.
- **Joy:** Message-print brainstorm.

### Lesson 12 — Two-colour reduction print
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make a two-colour print by printing, cutting more away and printing again." (UK NC KS2 printing — increasing complexity; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Picasso made bright colour linocuts from just one block — by cutting it away between colours. How is that possible?"
- **Key activity:** Print a polystyrene block in a light colour for the whole edition; press or cut more lines into the same block; print in a darker colour on top using registration marks.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do you print the lighter colour first?" → The darker colour on top covers it; light on top of dark wouldn't show.
- **Differentiation:** support: a second block with pre-cut shapes instead of reduction. / stretch: plan three colours.
- **Materials:** tiles, two ink colours, registration jig (card corner), paper.
- **Joy:** Watching the second colour transform the print.

### Lesson 13 — Printmaking critique
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can evaluate my prints and discuss what worked and why." (UK NC KS2 evaluate; NCAS VA:Re9.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Print fair" — lay out every edition.
- **Key activity:** Choose the best print; explain choices (line, texture, colour order, registration); annotate a sketchbook page.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which print in your edition is best and what made it better?" → Specific reasons (even ink, clear edges, good registration).
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence stems. / stretch: suggest how a professional printmaker would improve it.
- **Materials:** prints, sketchbooks.
- **Joy:** Print fair.

**End-of-unit check:** In discussion of their prints, the child explains the difference between a monoprint, a collagraph and a reduction print, identifies their strongest print and why, and names a printmaker who influenced them.

## Unit 3 — Clay and sculpture

**Essential question:** How do sculptors turn soft clay into strong, expressive forms?

**Key vocabulary:** clay, pinch pot, coil, slab, score and slip, relief, form, texture, negative space, hollow, kiln, glaze, ceramicist, sculpture

### Lesson 14 — Meet the ceramicists
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe the work of ceramic artists from different cultures." (UK NC KS2 great artists; NCAS VA:Re7.1.4)
- **Hook:** Handle real pottery; look at vessels by Magdalene Odundo (born in Kenya, works in Britain) and pots from Nigeria (e.g. the tradition of Ladi Kwali), and ancient Maya ceramics (links to history).
- **Key activity:** Compare shapes, surfaces and purposes; sketch forms from observation.
- **Check for understanding:** "What do you notice about the shape of Odundo's vessels and how might she have made them?" → Smooth, curving, elegant forms; built by hand (coiling) and burnished.
- **Differentiation:** support: guided looking prompts. / stretch: compare a functional pot with a sculptural one.
- **Materials:** real pottery pieces, reproductions, sketchbooks.
- **Joy:** "Mystery pot" — handle with eyes closed and describe.

### Lesson 15 — Strong joins: pinch, coil and slab revisited
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make secure score-and-slip joins in pinch, coil and slab forms, recalling Years 1–2." (UK NC KS2 sculpture — clay; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Last year's clay animal lost its ears when it dried. Why?"
- **Key activity:** Quick retrieval stations: a small pinch pot, a coil and a slab; the new focus is the join — scratch (score) both surfaces, add slip, press and blend the seam inside and out. Test joins by gently tugging when leather-hard next lesson; keep pieces damp for sgraffito.
- **Check for understanding:** "Your coil fell off when it dried. What went wrong, and how will you join it next time?" → it was not scored and slipped, or the clay was too dry, so the pieces didn't bond; score both surfaces, add slip, press and smooth the join
- **Differentiation:** support: a guided step card and one join type. / stretch: build a small hollow form joining two pinch pots and a slab base.
- **Materials:** air-drying or kiln clay, slip, clay tools, boards, damp cloths, plastic bags to keep work leather-hard.
- **Joy:** "Join-strength" test next lesson.

### Lesson 16 — Sgraffito: drawing through slip
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can decorate leather-hard clay by scratching a design through a layer of coloured slip." (UK NC KS2 sculpture — clay decoration; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Scratch through the top colour and a second colour appears underneath — potters from Renaissance Italy to today have used this trick."
- **Key activity:** Brush coloured slip onto a leather-hard tile or the Lesson 15 pot; let it dry to leather-hard; plan a design in the sketchbook (line pattern, Benin-style figure or plant); scratch through with wooden tools to reveal the clay below; brush away crumbs.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why must you scratch the design when the slip is leather-hard, not wet or bone-dry?" → wet slip smudges and fills back in; bone-dry clay chips and cracks; leather-hard clay cuts cleanly
- **Differentiation:** support: a simple line pattern and a template. / stretch: plan areas of fine and bold line to create tone.
- **Materials:** leather-hard clay tiles or pots, coloured slip, brushes, wooden sgraffito tools, sketchbooks.
- **Joy:** The first scratch revealing the colour underneath.

### Lesson 17 — Relief plaques inspired by Benin
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make a clay relief plaque that tells a story, inspired by Benin plaques." (UK NC KS2 sculpture; history link; NCAS VA:Cn11.1.4)
- **Hook:** Revisit Benin plaques from history: "Every plaque tells a story from the royal court."
- **Key activity:** Roll a slab; add raised forms (low relief) and carved details; tell a story about the class or school.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the difference between high relief and low relief?" → High relief sticks out a lot from the background; low relief only a little.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-cut slabs and templates. / stretch: include figures of different importance at different sizes (as in Benin plaques, where the Oba is often largest).
- **Materials:** clay, rolling pins, guide sticks, tools, reproductions of Benin plaques.
- **Joy:** A class "story wall" of plaques.

### Lesson 18 — Form and negative space: Barbara Hepworth
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain negative space and use it in a small sculpture." (UK NC KS2 sculpture; great artists; NCAS VA:Cr1.1.4)
- **Hook:** A Barbara Hepworth sculpture with a hole through it: "Is the hole part of the sculpture?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 1's Hepworth in one minute; the new step is *carving* (taking away) rather than modelling. Discuss negative space; carve a soap or plaster block (or shape clay) to include a hole or opening; draw it from different angles.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does the hole change how we see the sculpture?" → It lets light and space through, making the form feel lighter and connecting inside and outside.
- **Differentiation:** support: shape clay instead of carving. / stretch: plan with three viewpoint sketches first.
- **Materials:** soap bars or plaster blocks, blunt carving tools (lolly sticks, plastic knives), clay.
- **Joy:** Carving soap sculptures (safe, blunt tools).

### Lesson 19 — Painting or glazing and finishing
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can finish a clay piece with paint or glaze, choosing colours for effect." (UK NC KS2 clay — finishing; NCAS VA:Cr2.2.4)
- **Hook:** "How will colour change your sculpture's mood?"
- **Key activity:** Paint (air-drying clay) or glaze (kiln clay, if available); use colour choices from Year 3 colour theory; seal and display.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did you choose those colours for your piece?" → A reasoned link to mood, the artist inspiration or the story.
- **Differentiation:** support: a limited palette. / stretch: combine texture and colour to emphasise form.
- **Materials:** acrylic paint or glazes, brushes, varnish/PVA sealant. Safety: follow glaze and kiln safety guidance; adults handle the kiln.
- **Joy:** Seeing finished pieces come together.

### Lesson 20 — Ai Weiwei and collective art; clay critique
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can discuss a large collective artwork and evaluate our clay pieces as a group." (UK NC KS2 evaluate; NCAS VA:Re8.1.4)
- **Hook:** Ai Weiwei's *Sunflower Seeds* (2010, Tate Modern): about 100 million hand-painted porcelain seeds, made by about 1,600 craftspeople in Jingdezhen, China. "What does it mean when many hands make one artwork?"
- **Key activity:** Discuss meaning; arrange everyone's clay pieces as one installation; critique individual pieces.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is one strength of your piece and one thing you'd change next time?" → Specific evaluation using clay vocabulary.
- **Differentiation:** support: sentence stems. / stretch: write an artist's statement for the installation.
- **Materials:** clay pieces, display space.
- **Joy:** Class installation.

**End-of-unit check:** In a critique, the child demonstrates a secure score-and-slip join, explains sgraffito and why it is done at leather-hard, and discusses how an artist (Odundo, Ladi Kwali, Hepworth or Benin plaque makers) influenced their piece.

## Unit 4 — Textiles: dye, print and pattern

**Essential question:** How do makers around the world colour and pattern cloth?

**Key vocabulary:** textile, fibre, dye, natural dye, mordant, indicator, resist, adire, starch resist, tie-dye, shibori, bògòlanfini, repeat, half-drop repeat, block, motif, embellish

### Lesson 21 — Dyed and printed cloth around the world
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe how dyed and printed textiles are made in different cultures." (UK NC KS2 great artists and designers; NCAS VA:Cn11.1.4)
- **Hook:** A table of real textiles or large images: adire (Yoruba, Nigeria), bògòlanfini 'mud cloth' (Mali), Japanese indigo shibori, Indonesian batik, Indian block-printed cotton and a William Morris furnishing fabric.
- **Key activity:** One-minute retrieval of Years 1–2 weaving and batik-style resist and Year 3 stitching (ways of making and decorating cloth). New: how colour and pattern get into cloth — dyeing, resisting and printing. Handle and compare; map each textile's origin; sketch motifs.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is one way adire and bògòlanfini are similar, and one way they are different?" → both pattern cotton cloth with traditional dye methods and meaningful motifs; adire uses indigo dye with starch-paste or tied resist, while bògòlanfini is dyed in a leaf solution and then painted with fermented mud
- **Differentiation:** support: picture matching cards. / stretch: research what a particular motif means.
- **Materials:** textile samples or high-quality images, world map, sketchbooks.
- **Joy:** "Textile museum" handling session.

### Lesson 22 — Colour from plants: natural dyes
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can test natural dyes on cotton and explain why some colours change." (UK NC KS2 textiles — techniques; science link; NCAS VA:Cr1.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Before factories made dyes, every colour in clothes came from plants, insects or minerals. Which kitchen plants can dye cloth?"
- **Key activity:** An adult prepares dye baths from turmeric, onion skins and red cabbage; children dip labelled cotton strips, record colours, then add a drop of lemon juice or bicarbonate of soda to the cabbage dye and observe; keep a dye-test page in the sketchbook for Lesson 24.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why did the red-cabbage dye change colour when we added lemon juice?" → red cabbage dye changes colour with acids and alkalis (it is an indicator) — lemon juice is an acid, so it turned pinker
- **Differentiation:** support: two dyes only with a colour chart to match. / stretch: predict and test what bicarbonate of soda will do, and explain.
- **Materials:** cotton strips, turmeric, onion skins, red cabbage, lemon juice, bicarbonate of soda, jars, gloves, aprons. Safety: an adult handles hot water; gloves on; nothing is tasted.
- **Joy:** The "colour lab" test strips.

### Lesson 23 — Adire: Yoruba indigo resist
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a paste resist to draw a pattern on cloth, inspired by Yoruba adire eleko from Nigeria." (UK NC KS2 textiles; different cultures; NCAS VA:Cr1.2.4)
- **Hook:** "In Abeokuta and Ibadan, women painted cassava-starch paste onto cloth with feathers and sticks before dipping it in indigo. What happens to the painted parts?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 2's batik-style resist in one minute; new: adire eleko (starch-paste resist). Children design a grid of motifs, paint flour-and-water paste with a stick or brush, let it dry, then brush on blue fabric paint or dip in a cold indigo substitute; scrape and wash off the paste to reveal pale patterns.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does the paste make pale patterns when the cloth is dyed?" → the paste covers those areas and stops the dye soaking in; when it is washed off, the covered parts stay pale
- **Differentiation:** support: a simple repeated motif in a large grid. / stretch: plan a design mixing fine lines and solid shapes and explain which will resist best.
- **Materials:** cotton squares, flour, water, sticks and brushes, blue fabric paint or cold-water dye, gloves, aprons, images of adire.
- **Joy:** The reveal when the paste is scraped away.

### Lesson 24 — Resist dyeing: tie-dye and shibori
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use folding and binding to create resist patterns in dyed fabric." (UK NC KS2 textiles — techniques; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** Japanese indigo shibori: "How did the white patterns appear if the whole cloth went in the dye?"
- **Key activity:** Fold, twist, clamp and bind cotton; dye (cold-water or natural dyes like red cabbage or turmeric); unwrap and compare methods.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do the bound areas stay white?" → The binding stops dye reaching that part of the fabric (it resists the dye).
- **Differentiation:** support: one simple folding method. / stretch: plan a pattern by predicting the result.
- **Materials:** cotton squares, elastic bands, pegs, blocks, cold-water dye or the natural dyes from Lesson 22, gloves, aprons. Safety: gloves and ventilation; adult handles dye.
- **Joy:** The unwrapping reveal.

### Lesson 25 — William Morris: designing a repeating pattern
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can design a motif and repeat it as a half-drop pattern for printed fabric." (UK NC KS2 great designers; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** William Morris's *Strawberry Thief* (1883): "Where does the pattern begin and end? Can you find the square it repeats from?"
- **Key activity:** Find the repeating unit in a Morris design; draw a motif (plant, bird, animal) on a square tile; trace it into a straight repeat, then a half-drop repeat (each column shifted down half a tile); print the motif with a foam stamp onto the dyed cotton from Lessons 22–24.
- **Check for understanding:** "How does a half-drop repeat help the pattern flow instead of looking like a grid?" → each column is shifted down by half a tile, so the joins don't line up in straight rows, and the eye follows the flowing design rather than the squares
- **Differentiation:** support: a straight repeat only, with a gridded template. / stretch: design a motif whose edges join invisibly with the next tile.
- **Materials:** square paper tiles, tracing paper, craft foam, printing ink, brayers, dyed cotton, images of Morris designs.
- **Joy:** "Wallpaper wall" of everyone's repeats.

### Lesson 26 — Embellishing: stitch, print and dye together
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can combine dyed, printed and stitched surfaces in one panel and choose stitches for effect." (UK NC KS2 textiles — combining techniques; NCAS VA:Cr2.2.4)
- **Hook:** "You can now dye, resist, print and stitch. What happens when you use them all on one piece of cloth?"
- **Key activity:** Retrieve Year 3's running stitch, backstitch and cross stitch in one minute; plan a panel on the dyed and printed cotton; add stitches, buttons or beads to pick out parts of the design; join pieces into a small panel for the class banner.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which Year 3 stitch did you choose to add detail, and why that one?" → e.g. backstitch for a strong outline, running stitch for a dashed texture, cross stitch for a small repeating pattern
- **Differentiation:** support: large-hole fabric and big needles, one stitch type. / stretch: add a decorative stitch (e.g. chain stitch) and explain where it adds most.
- **Materials:** needles, embroidery thread, buttons, dyed and printed fabric pieces. Safety: needle-counting routine.
- **Joy:** Personal panels for a class banner.

### Lesson 27 — Class textile banner and critique
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can join my panel to a class banner and discuss dyeing, resist and pattern techniques and influences." (UK NC KS2 evaluate; NCAS VA:Pr5.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Every panel tells how it was coloured and patterned."
- **Key activity:** Assemble the class banner; each child explains their panel's dye, resist, print and stitch choices and the tradition that inspired them.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which techniques did you use on your panel, and which culture's textiles inspired you?" → accurate techniques (e.g. turmeric dye, adire paste resist, half-drop print) and an inspiration
- **Differentiation:** support: speaking frame. / stretch: explain how the whole banner works as one design.
- **Materials:** panels, backing fabric, stitching or fabric glue.
- **Joy:** The finished class banner.

**End-of-unit check:** In discussion of their panel, the child explains a resist technique, a natural-dye result and a half-drop repeat, and names at least two textile traditions or designers from different continents.

## Unit 5 — Geometric pattern: from compass to computer

**Essential question:** How can simple rules — a circle and a straight line — create endlessly beautiful patterns?

**Key vocabulary:** geometric, compass, radius, construction, symmetry, rotation, reflection, tessellation, star pattern, motif, grid, digital design

### Lesson 28 — Islamic geometric art
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can describe features of Islamic geometric design and where it is found." (UK NC KS2 great designers; history link; NCAS VA:Cn11.1.4)
- **Hook:** Tilework from the Alhambra (Spain), the Shah Mosque in Isfahan (Iran) and a Moroccan zellij fountain: "Where does the pattern begin and end?"
- **Key activity:** Look for repeated shapes, symmetry, stars and interlacing; connect to Baghdad's scholars and geometry (history Unit 3); sketch motifs.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why do many of these patterns seem to go on forever?" → They are built from repeating units on a grid, which can continue in every direction.
- **Differentiation:** support: coloured tracing of a pattern. / stretch: find lines of symmetry and centres of rotation.
- **Materials:** reproductions, tracing paper, coloured pencils.
- **Joy:** "Pattern detectives".

### Lesson 29 — Constructing a six-point pattern with compass and ruler
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can construct a six-fold geometric pattern using a compass and ruler." (UK NC KS2 drawing — accuracy; maths link; NCAS VA:Cr2.1.4)
- **Hook:** "Draw a circle; keep the same radius; step around the edge. What happens?"
- **Key activity:** Construct the six "petals" (flower of life style) with a compass; join points to make a hexagon and a six-point star; colour with symmetry.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does stepping the radius around the circle give exactly six points?" → The radius fits around the circle exactly six times (making six equilateral triangles meeting at the centre).
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-marked circles and safety compasses. / stretch: extend into a repeating grid of overlapping circles.
- **Materials:** safety compasses, rulers, sharp pencils, coloured pencils/fine liners.
- **Joy:** Watching the star appear from circles.

### Lesson 30 — Tessellation: M. C. Escher and repeating tiles
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can make a tessellating tile by cutting and sliding a shape." (UK NC KS2 design; maths link — translation; NCAS VA:Cr1.2.4)
- **Hook:** M. C. Escher's interlocking birds and fish (the Netherlands), inspired partly by his visits to the Alhambra.
- **Key activity:** Cut a shape from one side of a square and slide (translate) it to the opposite side; tape; trace to tessellate; decorate as a creature.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why does a slide (translation) keep the tile tessellating?" → Whatever you remove from one side is added exactly to the opposite side, so the tiles still fit with no gaps.
- **Differentiation:** support: one slide only. / stretch: slide on both pairs of sides.
- **Materials:** card squares, scissors, tape, paper, fine liners.
- **Joy:** Creature tessellations.

### Lesson 31 — Digital pattern design
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can use a digital tool to repeat, rotate and reflect a motif to make a pattern." (UK NC KS2 digital media; computing link; NCAS VA:Cr2.3.4)
- **Hook:** "Could a computer help us build the Alhambra's patterns?"
- **Key activity:** Use a free drawing or pattern app (or Scratch pen/turtle from computing) to repeat a motif using rotation and reflection; export and print.
- **Check for understanding:** "How did you use rotation or reflection to build your pattern?" → The child explains the transformation settings used.
- **Differentiation:** support: pre-made motif. / stretch: design a motif from scratch and compare with the hand-drawn version.
- **Materials:** tablets or computers, drawing app, printer.
- **Joy:** Printing digital designs as wrapping paper.

**End-of-unit check:** In discussion, the child explains how they constructed their pattern (compass/radius or translation), identifies symmetry or tessellation in it, and connects it to Islamic geometric art or Escher.

## Unit 6 — Curating our exhibition

**Essential question:** How do curators choose and present art so that people see and think?

**Key vocabulary:** curator, exhibition, gallery, label, artist's statement, theme, display, audience, portfolio, critique

### Lesson 32 — What does a curator do?
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can explain the role of a curator and plan how to display artwork." (UK NC KS2 evaluate and present; NCAS VA:Pr4.1.4)
- **Hook:** A virtual tour of a museum gallery (e.g. from a major museum's online collection): "Why is this painting next to that one?"
- **Key activity:** Discuss themes, grouping, lighting and labels; choose a theme for the class exhibition ("Space, Surface and Structure"); select pieces from the year.
- **Check for understanding:** "Why might a curator group two different artworks together?" → They share a theme, technique or idea that helps visitors see connections.
- **Differentiation:** support: selection checklist. / stretch: plan the layout of a room.
- **Materials:** year's portfolios, floor plan paper.
- **Joy:** Becoming curators.

### Lesson 33 — Artist statements and labels
- **Duration:** 35 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can write an artist's statement explaining my intention, process and influences." (UK NC KS2 evaluate; English link; NCAS VA:Pr5.1.4)
- **Hook:** Read real exhibition labels: "What do visitors need to know?"
- **Key activity:** Write 50–80 word statements with title, technique, inspiration and intention.
- **Check for understanding:** "What is the most important thing your statement tells a visitor?" → The intention or the story behind the work.
- **Differentiation:** support: a statement frame. / stretch: include one comparison with an artist studied.
- **Materials:** label templates.
- **Joy:** Naming their artworks.

### Lesson 34 — Installing the exhibition
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can mount, arrange and label artwork thoughtfully." (UK NC KS2 present work; NCAS VA:Pr5.1.4)
- **Hook:** "The gallery opens tomorrow!"
- **Key activity:** Mount work; arrange by theme; place labels; plan a visitor route; prepare to be gallery guides.
- **Check for understanding:** "Explain one decision you made about where a piece was displayed." → A reasoned curatorial choice.
- **Differentiation:** support: assigned roles. / stretch: lead a team.
- **Materials:** mounting card, display boards, labels, adhesive.
- **Joy:** Building a real gallery.

### Lesson 35 — Exhibition opening and year-end critique
- **Duration:** 40 min
- **Objective:** "By the end, I can talk about my work and others' to visitors and reflect on my growth as an artist." (UK NC KS2 evaluate; NCAS VA:Re9.1.4)
- **Hook:** Families and other classes arrive for the opening.
- **Key activity:** Children act as guides; visitors leave comments; final sketchbook reflection comparing September and July work.
- **Check for understanding:** "Which piece shows your greatest progress this year, and why?" → A specific, reasoned reflection.
- **Differentiation:** support: guide script. / stretch: lead a tour.
- **Materials:** exhibition, comment cards.
- **Joy:** The exhibition opening.

**End-of-unit check:** Each child guides a visitor through two of their works, explaining techniques, influences and one improvement they would make — the year's final assessment by discussion.

## End-of-year mastery checks

1. **Perspective drawing** — draws a street or room in one-point perspective with correct scale. *See it:* the Unit 1 street drawing, explained in critique.
2. **Printmaking** — makes a monoprint, a collagraph edition and a two-colour reduction print, and explains how each works. *See it:* the numbered editions, discussed with a partner.
3. **Clay** — makes secure score-and-slip joins, decorates with sgraffito, builds a relief plaque and carves negative space. *See it:* the Benin-inspired plaque and the sgraffito piece.

## Teacher guidance

**Assessment.** Assess by looking and talking: keep brief notes during critiques, photograph work in progress, and use sketchbook annotations. Ask "What were you trying to do? How did you do it? What would you change?" rather than testing facts.

**Common misconceptions (and fixes).**
- *"The horizon line is always in the middle of the page."* — it is at eye level; move it for high and low viewpoints.
- *"Printing ink should be thick."* — thin, even layers print more clearly.
- *"Just press the coils together."* — always score and slip.
- *"Craft from other cultures is 'simple'."* — adire, bògòlanfini and shibori are highly skilled traditions with deep meanings; present them with names, places and respect.
- *"Good art must look realistic."* — study Hepworth, Morris's stylised plants and Ai Weiwei.
- *"A print is always a copy."* — a monoprint is one of a kind.

**Cultural respect.** Present traditional art (adire and bògòlanfini motifs, Día de los Muertos imagery, Islamic geometry) with its names, meanings and makers; children take *inspiration* from techniques and principles rather than copying sacred or ceremonial designs.

**Safety.** Blunt tools for carving; safety compasses; needle counts; gloves and adult supervision for dyes; kiln used only by trained adults; check allergies (e.g. to latex or wool).

**Vertical links.** Years 1–3: sketchbooks, colour (wheel to optical mixing and symbolism), painting, tone in several media, depth without a vanishing point, relief stamping and Hokusai, pinch/coil/slab clay, weaving, batik-style resist and stitch and appliqué. Year 4: one-point perspective, monoprint/collagraph/reduction printing, sgraffito and carving, natural dyes, adire, shibori and repeat pattern, geometric construction. Year 5: portraits, sculpture with armatures, digital and photographic art and a personal project — it should retrieve rather than re-teach Year 4's perspective, geometric pattern and printmaking.

**Cross-curricular links.** History (Benin plaques; Baghdad and Islamic geometry; Maya ceramics), geography (streets of the world; textile traditions of Nigeria, Mali and Japan), maths (perspective lines, compass constructions, tessellation and translation), computing (digital pattern), languages (Mexican *papel picado* and *calaveras*), science (properties of materials; red cabbage as an indicator).
